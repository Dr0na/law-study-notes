## 📘 Coparcenary: Mitakshara and Dayabhaga, Women as Coparceners, Rights and Powers, Sole Surviving Coparcener, HSA 1956 Amendments

🎚️ Difficulty: 🔴 Hard

---

### 🧠 Introduction

**Coparcenary** is a narrow institution within the Hindu joint family consisting of persons who share (by birth or statutory extension) a **right in coparcenary property** with incidents such as **partition** and (classically, Mitakshara) **survivorship**. The two major schools—**Mitakshara** and **Dayabhaga**—differ fundamentally on **when** coparcenary unity arises and how **devolution** interacts with it.

The **Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005** made daughters **coparceners by birth** in Mitakshara coparcenary (like sons), altering a historically gendered structure. Examiners routinely test **school differences**, **sole surviving coparcener**, and **amendment** impact.

---

### 📖 Main Content

#### 🔹 Mitakshara coparcenary: core features

In the **Mitakshara** system (followed over much of India outside Bengal):

- Coparcenary is **limited** (traditionally sons, grandsons, great-grandsons of a common male ancestor—**four generations** including the last-born holder).
- A son’s interest **arises by birth** (classical “**unobstructed heritage**” language in textbooks).
- Coparcenary property is characterised by **four-generation** ancestral links and **blended** property in many disputes.
- Classical Mitakshara carried **survivorship**: on a coparcener’s death, his interest could **merge** into surviving coparceners rather than pass by testament freely (subject to exceptions and statutory overlay).

#### 🔹 Dayabhaga coparcenary: core differences (Bengal school)

In **Dayabhaga**:

- Coparcenary unity among sons typically **crystallises on the father’s death** (father holds stronger ownership during lifetime in many formulations).
- Devolution is more **succession-oriented**; textbooks contrast Mitakshara’s “**community by birth**” with Dayabhaga’s “**community on death**” framing.
- **Partition** rules and **shares** differ; survivorship plays a reduced/different role compared to classical Mitakshara descriptions.

Exam tip: Always name the **school** before applying rules; mixing schools loses marks.

#### 🔹 Women as coparceners (statutory)

**Pre-2005 (Mitakshara regions):** daughters were members of the joint family but **not coparceners** in the classical sense; wives were not coparceners.

**Post Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005:** daughter is a **coparcener by birth** in her own right in the same manner as a son. She has corresponding rights including seeking **partition** and bearing liabilities as coparcenary law provides.

**Retrospectivity controversy (exam favourite):** The Supreme Court in **Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020)** held that the daughter’s coparcenary right exists **by birth**, and the coparcenary **need not** be living as on 9 September 2005 for her to claim rights (settling conflicting precedents). Memorise the **ratio** crisply.

#### 🔹 Rights and powers of coparceners

Generally includes:

- **Right to joint possession/enjoyment** (subject to management by karta)
- **Right to restrain unauthorised alienation** (except where karta’s valid powers apply)
- **Right to seek partition** (subject to special disabilities in classical law—many modified)
- **Right to restrain improper waste** of coparcenary assets

#### 🔹 Sole surviving coparcener

When only **one** coparcener remains:

- He is **technically** the sole coparcener; the **joint family may still continue** if other non-coparcener members exist.
- He may **alienate** more freely in many situations (subject to judicial nuance: not a licence to defeat family rights if property still joint in character).
- **New coparceners** can be born/adopted; the estate may again become multi-member coparcenary.

#### 🔹 Effect of amendments to HSA 1956 (summary)

- **2005 amendment**: daughter as coparcener; some related changes in **Section 6** devolution scheme (follow bare Act numbering in exam).
- **Partition post-2005**: daughters treated on **parity** with sons for coparcenary shares in many situations.
- Always cross-read **Section 6** (as amended) with your textbook chart on **notional partition** concepts where taught.

### 🧾 Conclusion

Coparcenary is the **engine room** of Hindu joint family property law: it decides **who partitions**, **how shares expand**, and how **survivorship** (Mitakshara) interacts with **succession**. After **2005** and **Vineeta Sharma**, Mitakshara coparcenary is **gender-inclusive** at the membership level; Dayabhaga analysis remains **school-specific** in examinations.

### 🧾 Model Answers

#### 🎯 15 Mark — “Discuss the position of women as coparceners and the effect of amendments to the Hindu Succession Act, 1956.”

##### 🧾 Answer Synopsis

- Classical Mitakshara: **daughters not coparceners**; limited coparcenary membership.
- Reform impulse: gender equality + constitutional values.
- **2005 amendment**: daughter as **coparcener by birth** with partition/enjoyment incidents.
- **Judicial evolution**: from conflicting High Court tests to **Vineeta Sharma (SC 2020)**.
- Conclusion: statutory + judicial **parity** framework.

##### 🧠 Introduction

Coparcenary under classical **Mitakshara** law was largely a **male lineal** institution. Daughters were joint family members entitled to **maintenance** and **succession** reforms in other parts of the Act, but not **coparceners** in the same sense as sons. This created **gender asymmetry** in **birth-right**, **survivorship**, and **partition** outcomes. Amendments to the **Hindu Succession Act, 1956**, especially in **2005**, restructured **Section 6** to recognise daughters as coparceners, triggering extensive litigation on **retrospectivity** and **living coparcenary**.

##### 📖 Body

**1. Pre-amendment position**  
Before 2005, coparcenary membership in Mitakshara law excluded daughters. Their economic security depended on **maintenance**, **marriage arrangements**, and **succession to separate/intestate shares**, but not on **coparcenary birth-right**.

**2. 2005 amendment: legislative intent**  
The amendment aimed to remove gender discrimination by giving daughters the **same coparcenary rights** as sons—**by birth**—including the right to seek **partition** and liability for debts where law provides.

**3. Conflicting judicial approaches (exam narrative)**  
Some decisions linked the daughter’s entitlement to the father being **alive** on the amendment date; others disagreed. This split created uncertainty in **pending suits** and **oral partitions**.

**4. Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (Supreme Court, 2020)**  
The Supreme Court held that a daughter is a coparcener **by birth** in the same manner as a son. The coparcenary **need not** be “living” as on **9 September 2005** in the sense some judgments used; the Court emphasised **equal coparcenary rights** consistent with the amendment’s purpose.

**5. Practical consequences**  
Partition shares, **notional partition** arguments, and **alienation** disputes must now be assessed with daughters inside the coparcenary circle. Dayabhaga discussions remain distinct and should be mentioned only where syllabus requires comparison.

##### ⚖️ Case Laws Section

- **Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020)** — definitive ratio for coparcenary rights of daughters.  
- **Danamma @ Suman Surpur v. Amar (2018)** — illustrates trajectory towards parity (supporting narrative).  
- **Prakash v. Phulavati (2016)** — mention only as **superseded/clarified** context.

##### 🧾 Conclusion

Amendments to **HSA 1956** transformed coparcenary from a **patrilineal birth-right club** into a **gender-inclusive** institution for Mitakshara law, with **Vineeta Sharma** supplying the stable interpretive foundation. Examiners expect **Section 6** awareness plus **one crisp ratio**.
