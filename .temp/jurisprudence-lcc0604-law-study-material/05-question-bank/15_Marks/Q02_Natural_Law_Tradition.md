## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- Natural law = higher moral order discoverable by reason.
- Classical era — Plato, Aristotle, Stoics, Cicero, Aquinas.
- Social-contract phase — Grotius, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau.
- Decline in 19th century; positivist eclipse.
- 20th-century revival — Fuller, Hart’s minimum content, Finnis.
- Indian touchpoints — constitutional morality and basic structure.

### 🧠 Introduction

**Natural law** is the doctrine that there exists a **higher moral order**, discoverable by reason, to which all positive law ought to conform. Its history mirrors the history of Western legal thought — from Plato to Finnis — and it experienced a sharp **revival** after the second world war.

### 📖 Body

#### 1. Classical natural law

- **Plato (Republic):** justice is *“each doing his own and getting his own.”*
- **Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics):** distinguishes **natural justice** (universal) from **legal justice** (conventional).
- **Stoics and Cicero:** *“True law is right reason in agreement with nature; universal, unchangeable, eternal.”*
- **St. Augustine** — divine order founding human law.
- **St. Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologica):** fourfold law (**EDNH**) — Eternal, Divine, Natural, Human.

#### 2. Social-contract phase

- **Hugo Grotius (1625):** natural law binds states; founder of modern international law.
- **Thomas Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651):** absolute sovereign needed to escape the state of nature.
- **John Locke (1689):** government rests on consent; right to revolt against tyranny.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762):** general will = source of legitimacy; democratic foundation.

#### 3. Decline (19th century)

The rise of utilitarianism (Bentham) and analytical positivism (Austin, Kelsen) reduced natural law to a "ghost story". *Lex est lex.*

#### 4. Revival (20th century)

- **Lon L. Fuller (Morality of Law, 1964):** law has an **inner morality** of eight desiderata (**GPPC-CPCC**).
- **H.L.A. Hart (Concept of Law, 1961):** **minimum content of natural law** — five truisms about humans yield minimum rules on persons, property, promises.
- **John Finnis (Natural Law and Natural Rights, 1980):** seven **basic goods** (**LKPASPR**) and **practical reasonableness**.

### 📖 Indian touchpoints

- *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973) — **basic-structure doctrine** anchors natural-law style constraints on the constitutional amending power.
- *Maneka Gandhi v UoI* (1978) — substantive due process read into Article 21.
- *Navtej Singh Johar v UoI* (2018) — constitutional morality over popular morality.

### ⚖️ Cases to cite

- *Riggs v Palmer* (1889) — moral principle limits literal text.
- *Nazi grudge-informer cases* — failure of inner morality.
- *Kesavananda Bharati* — basic structure as Indian natural-law equivalent.

### 📊 Strengths vs Weaknesses

| 🎯 Strength | 🔻 Weakness |
|:---|:---|
| Moral check on law | Disagreement on content |
| Inspired human rights and Nuremberg | Risk of judicial subjectivism |
| Re-explains *lex iniusta* (Fuller) | Hart: morality of aspiration ≠ validity |
| Finnis offers workable list | Basic goods themselves contested |

### 🧾 Conclusion

Natural law has been **rebuilt three times**: by Aquinas on theology, by the social contract on consent, and by Fuller and Finnis on procedure and basic goods. The tradition continues to provide the moral vocabulary by which positive law is judged.
