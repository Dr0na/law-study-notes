## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- A *source* of law is anything from which a rule derives binding force.
- Salmond’s **formal–material** distinction.
- Four material sources — Custom, Legislation, Precedent, Juristic writings (**CLPJ**).
- Each source examined with essentials, kinds, merits, and Indian touchpoints.
- Conclusion: layered authority — legislation primary, precedent dominant in courts, custom residual, juristic writings persuasive.

### 🧠 Introduction

The phrase **source of law** answers the question, *whence does this rule derive its authority?* Salmond classifies sources into **formal** sources — the authorities (sovereign, constitution) that give validity — and **material** sources — where the *content* of legal rules comes from. The dominant material sources in any modern legal system are **Custom, Legislation, Precedent, and Juristic Writings**.

### 📖 Body

#### 1. Custom (essentials — ACPRCC)

A custom is a uniform practice followed by a community for so long that it has acquired the force of law. To be enforced it must satisfy six **essentials**:

1. **Antiquity** — observed from time immemorial.
2. **Continuity** — without interruption.
3. **Peaceable enjoyment** — *nec vi, nec clam, nec precario*.
4. **Reasonableness** — withstands rational scrutiny.
5. **Certainty** — clear and definite.
6. **Consistency** with statute and public policy.

**Kinds:** legal customs (general or local) bind universally; conventional customs (usages) bind only parties who knowingly adopt them.

**Indian touch:** *Article 13(3)(a)* of the Constitution treats "custom or usage having the force of law" as *law*. Hindu and Muslim personal laws rest substantially on custom.

#### 2. Legislation (merits — CSDR)

Legislation is the **deliberate making of legal rules** by an authority competent under the constitution. It is today the **dominant source** of legal change.

- **Kinds:** supreme (Parliament / state legislatures) and subordinate (delegated rules).
- **Merits — CSDR:** Clear, Speedy, Democratic, Repealable.
- **Demerits:** rigidity if poorly drafted, detachment from social practice, interpretation problems.

**Indian touch:** the Indian Penal Code, 1860, and the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, are model statutes that draw heavily on Bentham’s codification ideal.

#### 3. Precedent

A precedent is a previously decided case supplying authority for similar issues.

- **Theories:**
  - **Declaratory** (Blackstone) — judges merely declare pre-existing law.
  - **Constitutive** — judges make law in deciding novel issues.
- **Article 141:** *"The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India."*
- **Ratio decidendi binds**; **obiter dicta** persuade.
- **Prospective overruling** introduced in *Golak Nath v State of Punjab* (1967).
- **Per incuriam, statutory change, and higher-bench overruling** destroy binding force.

#### 4. Juristic writings

Textbook writers (Salmond, Pollock, Mulla, Dias, Hart, Kelsen) act as **persuasive secondary authority**. Indian courts routinely cite *Pollock & Mulla*, *Halsbury’s Laws of England*, and academic articles in hard cases.

### ⚖️ Cases to cite

- *Golak Nath v State of Punjab* (1967) — prospective overruling.
- *Bengal Immunity Co. v State of Bihar* (1955) — SC not bound by its own decisions.
- *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) — judicial law-making.

### 🧾 Conclusion

The four sources are **complementary**, not competitive. **Legislation** dominates routine output; **precedent** does the day-to-day work in courts; **custom** survives in personal laws and trade usage; **juristic writings** fertilise the whole. A topper answer must name all four, articulate their **rank-order**, and pin precedent down with **Article 141**.
