# Q03 (15 Marks) — Compare Austin, Hart, and Kelsen as legal positivists

**Module:** 04 — Legal Positivism
**Marks:** 15
**Difficulty:** 🔴 Hard
**ID:** JUR-15M-Q003

## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- Common ground — separation of *is* and *ought*.
- Austin’s command theory.
- Hart’s union of primary and secondary rules.
- Kelsen’s Pure Theory and Grundnorm.
- Comparative table; criticism; Indian application.

### 🧠 Introduction

Legal positivism holds that the **validity** of a legal rule depends on its **social source**, not its moral merit. Although Bentham, Austin, Hart, and Kelsen all subscribe to the **separation thesis**, they differ profoundly on the *structure* of law.

### 📖 Body

#### 1. Austin — command of the sovereign

Austin defined law as *"a command of the sovereign backed by sanction."* Four elements (**CSSD**):

- **Command** — wish expressed by superior.
- **Sovereign** — receiving habitual obedience, not habitually obedient.
- **Sanction** — threatened evil.
- **Duty** — obligation to obey.

#### 2. Hart — union of primary and secondary rules

Hart (Concept of Law, 1961) replaced the command model with a **rules** model.

- **Primary rules** impose duties (don’t steal).
- **Secondary rules** confer powers (**RCA**) — recognition, change, adjudication.
- The **rule of recognition** identifies what counts as law; it is sustained by the **internal point of view** of officials.

#### 3. Kelsen — Pure Theory

Kelsen demands a **purely formal** science of law. Norms form a **hierarchy** (Stufenbau), and the highest **Grundnorm** is presupposed.

- Distinguishes **static** (content-validity) and **dynamic** (formal-validity) systems; legal systems are predominantly dynamic.
- Famous application — *State v Dosso* (Pakistan, 1958) explained revolutionary change.

### 📊 Comparison table

| 🔍 | **Austin** | **Hart** | **Kelsen** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Definition of law | Command + sovereign + sanction | Union of primary + secondary rules | Hierarchy of norms |
| Source of validity | Habitual obedience to sovereign | Rule of recognition | Grundnorm |
| Treatment of custom | Difficult (no command) | Comfortably as recognised rule | As a norm derived from a higher norm |
| Treatment of judges | Apply command | Apply rules; discretion in hard cases | Apply individual norm derived from higher |
| Role of morality | Outside law | Outside, with minimum content | Outside, pure science |
| Famous critic | Hart (gunman analogy) | Dworkin (principles) | Hart (Grundnorm mysterious) |

### ⚖️ Cases / examples

- *State v Dosso* (Pakistan, 1958) — Kelsen’s Grundnorm in courtroom.
- *Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke* (Rhodesia, 1968) — limits of Grundnorm.
- *Riggs v Palmer* (1889) — Dworkin uses it to challenge Hart.

### 🔻 Criticism

- **Austin** — too narrow; fails on custom, constitutional limits, power-conferring rules.
- **Hart** — neglects principles (Dworkin); rule of recognition itself uncertain in pluralist societies.
- **Kelsen** — Grundnorm is a metaphysical fiction; reduces law to pure form.

### 🧾 Conclusion

The three positivists offer a **progression** — Austin gives us the start, Hart the discipline of rule-analysis, Kelsen the rigorous formalism. Together they constitute the **mainstream** account of legal validity, even as natural-law and realist critics continue to push back.
