## 🛡️ Q3. Define Contract of Indemnity. Discuss the rights of the indemnity holder when sued. Explain when the liability of the indemnifier commences.

### 1. Introduction and Definition
The term "indemnity" means "security against loss" or "compensation for damage." It is a specialized form of contract designed to protect a party against potential financial hazards.
**Section 124** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 defines it as:
*"A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person, is called a contract of indemnity."*
- **Parties:** The person making the promise is the *Indemnifier*, and the person protected is the *Indemnity Holder*.

### 2. Essential Elements
A valid contract of indemnity must possess:
1. Two parties.
2. An express or implied promise to save from loss.
3. The loss must be caused by human agency (promisor or third party). In India, unlike English law, losses due to accidents/acts of God are strictly outside this definition unless courts apply equitable principles.
4. It must satisfy all essential elements of a valid contract under Section 10 (free consent, lawful object, etc.).

### 3. Rights of the Indemnity Holder (When Sued)
The rights of the indemnity holder are explicitly laid down in **Section 125**. If the indemnity holder acts within the scope of their authority and is sued, they are entitled to recover the following from the indemnifier:

**I. Right to Recover Damages (Sec 125(1))**
All damages which they may be compelled to pay in any suit in respect of any matter to which the promise to indemnify applies.

**II. Right to Recover Costs (Sec 125(2))**
All costs which they may be compelled to pay in bringing or defending any such suit. 
*Conditions:* They must not have contravened the orders of the promisor, and must have acted as a prudent person would have in the absence of the indemnity, or the promisor must have authorized them to bring/defend the suit.

**III. Right to Recover Sums Paid under Compromise (Sec 125(3))**
All sums which they may have paid under the terms of any compromise of any such suit.
*Conditions:* The compromise must not be contrary to the orders of the promisor, and it must be one which it would have been prudent to make, or the promisor must have authorized it.

### 4. Commencement of Liability of the Indemnifier
**The core question:** Does the indemnity holder have to actually suffer and pay the loss out of their pocket before asking the indemnifier to pay? 
The Indian Contract Act is silent on when exactly the liability commences. Under original English common law, the rule was "you must be damnified before you can claim to be indemnified" (i.e., you must actually pay the loss first).

However, modern equitable principles dictate a more progressive approach, which has been widely adopted by Indian courts.
**The modern rule:** The liability of the indemnifier commences as soon as the liability of the indemnity holder becomes **absolute and certain**.

**Landmark Case:** *Gajanan Moreshwar Parelkar vs. Moreshwar Madan Mantri (1942)*
*Facts:* The plaintiff took a lease of land and transferred it to the defendant. For construction on the land, the plaintiff gave an indemnity bond to the municipal corporation. The defendant failed to pay for the materials, and the plaintiff was sued. The plaintiff asked the defendant to indemnify him even before he actually paid the amount.
*Judgment:* The Bombay High Court held that the Indian Contract Act is not exhaustive. If the indemnity holder has incurred a liability which is absolute, he is entitled to call upon the indemnifier to pay the amount. He does not have to wait to pay it himself and then sue the indemnifier.

### 5. Conclusion
A contract of indemnity is a powerful commercial tool. The law ensures that an indemnity holder is not left financially stranded, allowing them to invoke the indemnifier's protection the moment their liability to a third party becomes imminent and absolute, rather than forcing them to suffer an out-of-pocket loss first.
