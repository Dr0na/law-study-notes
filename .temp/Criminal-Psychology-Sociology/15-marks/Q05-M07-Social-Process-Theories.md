## 🎯 Introduction

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Social Process theories focus on how individuals become criminals through their interactions with others and social institutions. Unlike structural theories that emphasize economic position, process theories examine socialization, learning, social bonds, and labeling processes. These theories explain why individuals from same social class have different outcomes - some become criminals, others don't.

## 📖 Sutherland's Differential Association Theory (1939)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Edwin H. Sutherland** developed this theory arguing that **criminal behavior is learned** through interaction with others, just like any other behavior.

### Core Principle:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**"A person becomes delinquent because of an excess of definitions favorable to violation of law over definitions unfavorable to violation of law."**

**In simple terms:** If you associate more with people who view crime favorably, you're more likely to commit crime.

</div>

### 9 Propositions of Differential Association:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Proposition 1: Criminal Behavior is Learned**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Not inherited or biological - acquired through learning process.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 2: Learned Through Interaction**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Learned in process of communication with other persons - particularly intimate personal groups.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 3: Learning Occurs in Intimate Groups**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Principal part of learning occurs within intimate personal groups (family, friends, peers) - not impersonal agencies like media.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 4: Learning Includes Techniques**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Learning includes: **Techniques** of committing crime (how to break lock, forge signature) AND **Motives, drives, rationalizations, attitudes** (why it's okay to commit crime).
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 5: Direction of Motives**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Specific direction of motives learned from definitions of legal codes as favorable or unfavorable - some view law as rules to be followed, others as obstacles to circumvent.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 6: Excess of Definitions**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Person becomes delinquent when exposed to **excess of definitions favorable** to law violation over definitions unfavorable - balance tips toward crime when pro-criminal associations outweigh anti-criminal.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 7: Vary in Frequency, Duration, Priority, Intensity**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Frequency:** How often exposed to pro-criminal definitions

**Duration:** How long the associations last

**Priority:** Earlier in life = more influential

**Intensity:** Prestige/importance of source (admired person = more influence)

</div>

<br>

**Proposition 8: Same Learning Process**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Learning criminal behavior involves same mechanisms as learning any other behavior - no special process.
</div>

<br>

**Proposition 9: General Needs Don't Explain**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Criminal behavior is not explained by general needs/values (everyone wants money) - it's explained by specific learning of criminal patterns.
</div>

</div>

### Example:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Youth in Criminal Gang:**
- Associates primarily with gang members (delinquent peers)
- Exposed to pro-crime attitudes daily ("cops are enemies," "stealing is smart")
- Learns techniques (how to steal cars)
- Learns rationalizations ("rich people don't miss it")
- Pro-criminal definitions exceed anti-criminal
- **Result:** Engages in criminal behavior

</div>

### Policy Implications:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

- Early intervention programs
- Positive peer groups
- Mentor programs
- Change association patterns

</div>

</div>

## 🏷️ Becker's Labelling Theory (1963)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Howard Becker** developed labelling theory arguing that **society creates criminals by labeling certain individuals and behaviors as deviant.**

### Core Principle:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**"Deviance is not a quality of the act the person commits, but rather a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions to an 'offender.' The deviant is one to whom that label has successfully been applied."**

**In simple terms:** Crime is not inherent in behavior - it becomes crime when society labels it so.

</div>

### Key Concepts:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Concept 1: Primary vs. Secondary Deviance**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Primary Deviance:**
- Initial act of rule-breaking
- Minor, temporary
- Not part of self-concept
- Person doesn't see self as deviant
- **Example:** Teenager shoplifts once

**Secondary Deviance:**
- Deviance that occurs AFTER being labeled
- Person accepts deviant identity
- Organizes life around deviant role
- **Example:** After arrest, labeled "thief" → Accepts identity → Continues stealing → Criminal career

**Key Point:** Label transforms occasional deviant act into deviant identity

</div>

<br>

**Concept 2: Master Status**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Once labeled "criminal," that becomes **master status** - overrides all other statuses

**Effect:**
- "Ex-convict" label sticks
- Difficult to get job (employment discrimination)
- Social rejection
- Limited opportunities
- Pushed back into crime

**Self-Fulfilling Prophecy:** Labeled as criminal → Treated as criminal → Behaves as criminal

</div>

<br>

**Concept 3: Societal Reaction (Labelling Process)**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Who Gets Labeled:**
- Poor more likely labeled than rich (same act)
- Minorities more likely labeled
- Young people more likely labeled
- Those without power

**Example:** Rich person caught with drugs → "Needs help" - Poor person caught with drugs → "Criminal"

**Selective Enforcement:** Police, courts selectively apply labels based on class, race, appearance

</div>

</div>

### Process of Becoming Criminal (Labelling Perspective):

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Stage 1:** Minor deviant act (primary deviance)

**Stage 2:** Caught and labeled by authorities

**Stage 3:** Public ceremony of degradation (arrest, trial, conviction)

**Stage 4:** Person accepts deviant identity

**Stage 5:** Excluded from conventional society

**Stage 6:** Associates with other deviants

**Stage 7:** Secondary deviance - full criminal career

</div>

### Policy Implications:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**1. Diversion Programs:** Keep youth out of formal system to avoid labeling

**2. Decriminalization:** Some acts (drug possession) shouldn't create criminal label

**3. Restorative Justice:** Focus on repair, not punishment and labeling

**4. Expungement:** Seal criminal records after certain period

**5. Reintegration Programs:** Help ex-offenders shed criminal label

</div>

</div>

## 🔗 Hirschi's Social Control Theory (Social Bond Theory)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Travis Hirschi** (1969) proposed that crime occurs when individual's bonds to society are weak or broken.

### Core Question:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Not "Why do people commit crime?" but **"Why DON'T people commit crime?"**

**Answer:** Social bonds prevent crime. When bonds weak → Crime likely.

</div>

### 4 Elements of Social Bond:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Bond 1: Attachment**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Emotional connection to others (family, friends, teachers) - care about their opinion - don't want to disappoint them - attachment prevents crime.
</div>

<br>

**Bond 2: Commitment**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Investment in conventional activities (education, career, reputation) - have "stake in conformity" - crime would risk losing investments - commitment prevents crime.
</div>

<br>

**Bond 3: Involvement**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Time spent in conventional activities (school, work, sports) - too busy for crime - involvement prevents crime through limiting opportunities.
</div>

<br>

**Bond 4: Belief**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Acceptance of conventional values and moral validity of law - believe law is right - belief prevents crime through internalized morality.
</div>

**Mnemonic: "ACIB"** - Attachment, Commitment, Involvement, Belief

</div>

**When Bonds Weak:** Individual free to commit crime without social constraints.

</div>

## ⚖️ Comparison and Application

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

| **Theory** | **Key Thinker** | **Core Idea** | **Why Crime?** |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| Differential Association | Sutherland | Learning theory | Learn from criminal associations |
| Labelling | Becker | Social reaction | Society labels → creates criminals |
| Social Control | Hirschi | Social bonds | Weak bonds → no restraint |

### Practical Applications:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**All Three Inform:**
- Youth intervention programs (strengthen bonds, change associations, avoid labels)
- School-based prevention (build attachments, conventional activities)
- Community policing (avoid stigmatizing labels)
- Restorative justice (repair bonds, avoid formal labeling)

</div>

## 🎯 Conclusion

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Social Process theories shift focus from "what makes someone criminal" to "how does someone become criminal." Differential Association explains crime as learned behavior through criminal associations. Labelling Theory shows how society creates criminals by applying deviant labels. Social Control Theory explains crime through weak social bonds.

Together, these theories emphasize:
- Importance of socialization
- Role of peer groups
- Impact of official reactions
- Significance of social bonds

For criminal justice policy, these theories suggest:
- Prevent harmful labeling (diversion, restorative justice)
- Strengthen positive associations (mentoring, community programs)
- Build social bonds (family support, school engagement)
- Early intervention before deviant identity solidifies

Understanding these processes is crucial for developing effective crime prevention and rehabilitation strategies that address how individuals become enmeshed in criminal lifestyles through social processes.
