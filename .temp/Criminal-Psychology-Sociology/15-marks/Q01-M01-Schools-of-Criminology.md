## 🎯 Introduction

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Criminology as a science has evolved through various schools of thought, each offering different explanations for criminal behavior and proposing different approaches to crime control. The three major schools - Classical, Neo-Classical, and Positivist - represent fundamental shifts in understanding crime causation and shaped modern criminal justice systems worldwide including India.

## ⚖️ Classical School of Criminology (1760s-1800s)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

The Classical School emerged in the 18th century as a reaction against the arbitrary, cruel, and inconsistent punishments of the pre-classical era.

### Key Contributors:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**1. Cesare Beccaria (1738-1794)** - Father of Classical Criminology

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Major Work:** "On Crimes and Punishments" (1764)

**Key Contributions:**
- Crimes and punishments should be defined by written laws
- Punishment should be proportionate to the crime
- Certainty and swiftness of punishment more important than severity
- Torture and capital punishment should be abolished
- Prevention better than punishment

</div>

<br>

**2. Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832)** - Utilitarian Approach

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Key Contributions:**
- Principle of "greatest happiness for greatest number"
- Hedonistic calculus - people seek pleasure, avoid pain
- Punishment should inflict just enough pain to deter crime
- Designed the "Panopticon" prison model

</div>

</div>

### 5 Core Principles of Classical School:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Principle 1: Free Will**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Humans have free will and rational choice. Individuals choose to commit crimes after weighing costs and benefits.
</div>

<br>

**Principle 2: Rationality**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Criminals are rational beings who calculate pleasure vs. pain before acting.
</div>

<br>

**Principle 3: Deterrence**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Punishment should be swift, certain, and proportionate to deter crime. Fear of punishment prevents criminal behavior.
</div>

<br>

**Principle 4: Equality Before Law**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
All individuals equal before law. Same crime = same punishment regardless of status.
</div>

<br>

**Principle 5: Social Contract**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Individuals surrender some freedom to state for protection. State has right to punish those who violate social contract.
</div>

</div>

### Impact on Criminal Justice:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

- Codification of crimes and punishments
- Proportionate sentencing
- Presumption of innocence
- Right to fair trial
- Abolition of torture
- Foundation for modern criminal law

</div>

</div>

## ⚖️ Neo-Classical School (Early 1800s)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

The Neo-Classical School emerged as a modification of the Classical School, recognizing that pure classical principles were too rigid and didn't account for individual differences.

### Key Modifications to Classical School:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Modification 1: Recognition of Mitigating Circumstances**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Neo-classicists recognized that certain factors reduce culpability:
- Insanity or mental illness
- Age (children lack full rational capacity)
- Provocation
- Self-defense
- Diminished capacity

**Example:** A child committing theft cannot be treated same as adult - lacks full rational decision-making capacity.

</div>

<br>

**Modification 2: Individual Differences**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Acknowledged that not all individuals are equally rational or have equal capacity for choice. Physical, mental, and environmental factors affect behavior.

</div>

<br>

**Modification 3: Flexibility in Sentencing**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Introduced judicial discretion - judges can consider individual circumstances when determining punishment. Same crime may warrant different punishments based on circumstances.

</div>

<br>

**Modification 4: Concept of Criminal Intent**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Greater emphasis on mens rea (criminal intent). Act alone not sufficient - must prove guilty mind (except strict liability offenses).

</div>

</div>

### Retained from Classical School:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

- Free will as basis (but modified)
- Deterrence as goal
- Proportionality in punishment
- Codified laws

</div>

### Contribution to Indian Law:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Indian Penal Code reflects neo-classical principles:
- **Section 82-83:** Infancy defense (children below 7 and 7-12)
- **Section 84:** Insanity defense
- **Section 76-106:** General exceptions (necessity, self-defense, provocation)
- **Section 34-38:** Joint liability with mens rea requirement

</div>

</div>

## 🔬 Positivist School of Criminology (1870s onwards)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

The Positivist School marked a fundamental shift - from philosophical speculation to scientific investigation. It rejected free will, focusing instead on determinism and scientific methods to study criminal behavior.

### Core Philosophy:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Criminal behavior is determined by biological, psychological, and social factors** - not free rational choice. Crime can be scientifically studied, measured, and predicted.

**Methodology:** Empirical research, data collection, case studies, statistical analysis

</div>

### 3 Key Contributors:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Contributor 1: Cesare Lombroso (1835-1909) - Father of Positivist Criminology**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Theory:** **Biological/Anthropological Positivism**

**Major Work:** "L'Uomo Delinquente" (The Criminal Man) - 1876

**Key Concepts:**

**"Born Criminal" Theory:**
- Criminals are born, not made
- Can be identified by physical characteristics (atavistic features)
- Criminals are evolutionary throwbacks to primitive humans

**Atavistic Features (Criminal Stigmata):**
- Large jaw, prominent cheekbones
- Excessive arm length
- Large ears
- Asymmetrical face
- Insensitivity to pain

**Criminal Types:**
- **Born criminals** (atavistic - most dangerous)
- **Insane criminals** (mentally ill)
- **Occasional criminals** (circumstantial)
- **Criminals of passion** (emotional crimes)

**Criticism:**
- Oversimplified - not all criminals have physical features
- Ignored social/environmental factors
- Methodologically flawed studies
- Deterministic - denied rehabilitation possibility

**However, Lombroso's contribution:** First to use scientific methods in criminology, established criminology as science, shifted focus from crime to criminal.

</div>

<br>

**Contributor 2: Enrico Ferri (1856-1929) - Social Positivism**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Theory:** **Sociological Positivism**

**Major Work:** "Criminal Sociology" (1884)

**Key Concepts:**

**Crime has multiple causes - biological, physical, social:**

**1. Anthropological (Individual) Factors:**
- Age, sex, psychology, race
- Biological constitution

**2. Physical (Geographic) Factors:**
- Climate, temperature
- Geographic location
- Seasonal variations

**3. Social Factors (Most Important):**
- Poverty, unemployment
- Education level
- Family structure
- Population density
- Religion, customs

**Classification of Criminals:**
- **Born criminals** (genetic predisposition)
- **Insane criminals** (mental illness)
- **Occasional criminals** (circumstances)
- **Criminals by passion** (intense emotions)
- **Habitual criminals** (repeat offenders)

**Law of Criminal Saturation:** Every society has criminal saturation point - given social conditions, certain amount of crime inevitable.

**Penal Substitutes:** Instead of just punishment, use social reforms:
- Improve economic conditions
- Better education
- Housing reforms
- Reduce poverty

**Contribution:** Broadened criminology beyond biology to include social factors, emphasized crime prevention through social reform, influenced social welfare policies.

</div>

<br>

**Contributor 3: Raffaele Garofalo (1851-1934) - Psychological Positivism**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Theory:** **Psychological/Legal Positivism**

**Major Work:** "Criminology" (1885)

**Key Concepts:**

**Natural Crime Concept:**
- Some acts are "naturally" criminal (mala in se)
- Violate basic human sentiments: pity and probity
- **Pity:** Compassion, altruism (violated by murder, assault)
- **Probity:** Honesty, integrity (violated by theft, fraud)

**Psychic Anomaly:**
- Criminals lack basic moral sentiments
- Psychic or moral deficiency
- Cannot feel empathy or respect property

**Treatment:**
- Eliminate defective individuals from society
- Death penalty or life imprisonment for incorrigibles
- Reparation and compensation for victims
- Treatment based on criminal type

**Contribution:** Emphasized psychological factors, developed concept of natural crime, focused on victim rights and compensation.

</div>

</div>

### Common Features of Positivist School:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**1. Determinism:** Crime caused by factors beyond individual control

**2. Scientific Method:** Use empirical research and data

**3. Focus on Criminal:** Study the criminal, not just the crime

**4. Rehabilitation:** Treatment and reform over punishment

**5. Crime Prevention:** Address root causes (poverty, inequality, etc.)

</div>

</div>

## ⚖️ Comparison of Three Schools

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

| **Aspect** | **Classical** | **Neo-Classical** | **Positivist** |
|-----------|--------------|------------------|---------------|
| **Period** | 1760s | Early 1800s | 1870s onwards |
| **Key Thinkers** | Beccaria, Bentham | Modified Classical | Lombroso, Ferri, Garofalo |
| **View of Man** | Rational, free will | Rational but factors considered | Determined by factors |
| **Focus** | Crime (act) | Crime + circumstances | Criminal (person) |
| **Cause of Crime** | Rational choice | Choice + circumstances | Biological/social/psychological |
| **Punishment** | Deterrence | Deterrence + reform | Treatment/rehabilitation |
| **Equality** | Same crime = same punishment | Individual consideration | Treatment based on type |
| **Method** | Philosophical | Legal/philosophical | Scientific/empirical |

</div>

## 🎯 Conclusion

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

The three schools of criminology represent the evolution of criminal justice thinking. The Classical School established rational, humane principles replacing arbitrary punishments. The Neo-Classical School introduced flexibility and recognition of individual circumstances. The Positivist School brought scientific methods and shifted focus from punishment to understanding and treating criminals.

Modern criminology and criminal justice systems incorporate elements from all three schools:
- **Classical:** Codified laws, proportionate punishment, deterrence
- **Neo-Classical:** Mitigating circumstances, judicial discretion, mens rea
- **Positivist:** Scientific investigation, rehabilitation programs, addressing root causes

Understanding these schools is crucial for comprehending how contemporary criminal justice systems balance punishment with reform, deterrence with rehabilitation, and legal principles with scientific knowledge about criminal behavior.

</div>
