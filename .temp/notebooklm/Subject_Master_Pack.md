# Module 1: Introduction to Law of Evidence & Key Definitions

## Introduction
The Law of Evidence is a procedural law that governs the rules and principles regarding the admissibility, relevancy, and weight of evidence in judicial proceedings. In India, the law was historically governed by the **Indian Evidence Act, 1872** (IEA), which was drafted by Sir James Fitzjames Stephen. To modernize the legal system, integrate digital advancements, and remove colonial terminology, the Indian Parliament enacted the **Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023** (BSS), which received Presidential assent on December 25, 2023, and came into force on **1st July 2024**.

Evidence law acts as the bridge between substantive law (like Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023) and procedural law (like Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023). It ensures that judicial decisions are based on facts proved through a standardized, rational process rather than conjecture or hearsay.

---

## 1. Central Conceptions and Definitions under BSS, 2023

Section 2 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023 consolidates the primary legal definitions:

### A. Fact — Sec. 2(f)
A "Fact" means and includes:
1. Any thing, state of things, or relation of things, capable of being perceived by the senses (Physical Facts).
2. Any mental condition of which any person is conscious (Psychological Facts).

*   **Illustration 1 (Physical Fact)**: That a man heard or saw something; that there are certain goods in a room; that a man uttered certain words.
*   **Illustration 2 (Psychological Fact)**: That a man has a certain reputation; that a man acted in good faith, with fraudulent intent, or maliciously.

### B. Relevant Fact — Sec. 2(k)
One fact is said to be relevant to another when the one is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of the BSS relating to the relevancy of facts (Sections 3 to 50).
*   **Legal Position**: Relevance is determined solely by law (legal relevancy), not just by logic (logical relevancy).

### C. Facts in Issue — Sec. 2(g)
"Facts in issue" means and includes any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature or extent of any right, liability or disability, asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding, necessarily follows.
*   **Meaning**: These are the principal facts which the plaintiff/prosecution must prove to establish their case, or which the defendant/accused must prove to establish a defence.
*   **Illustration**: A is accused of the murder of B. In trial, the facts in issue are:
    - That A caused B’s death.
    - That A intended to cause B’s death.
    - That A had received grave and sudden provocation from B.

---

## 2. Classification of Evidence — Sec. 2(e)

"Evidence" means and includes:
1.  **Oral Evidence**: All statements which the court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses, in relation to matters of fact under inquiry.
2.  **Documentary Evidence**: All documents including electronic or digital records produced for the inspection of the Court.

### Sub-Classifications:
*   **Primary Evidence (Sec. 2(d) & Sec. 57)**: The document itself produced for the inspection of the court (e.g., the original contract, original letter).
*   **Secondary Evidence (Sec. 2(d) & Sec. 58)**: Certified copies, copies made from the original by mechanical processes, oral accounts of the contents of a document by a person who has seen it, etc.
*   **Direct Evidence**: Evidence of a witness who saw, heard, or perceived the fact with their own senses.
*   **Circumstantial Evidence**: Evidence of a set of circumstances from which the existence of a fact in issue can be inferred (indirect evidence).
*   **Hearsay Evidence**: Second-hand evidence; statements made by a person who is not called as a witness, reported by a witness who heard them. Generally inadmissible unless exceptions apply.

---

## 3. Standard of Proof — Proved, Disproved, and Not Proved

The court evaluates evidence based on the standard of a "prudent man":

*   **Proved — Sec. 2(j)**: A fact is said to be proved when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes it to exist, or considers its existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it exists.
*   **Disproved — Sec. 2(c)**: A fact is said to be disproved when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes that it does not exist, or considers its non-existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it does not exist.
*   **Not Proved — Sec. 2(i)**: A fact is said to be not proved when it is neither proved nor disproved.

---

## 4. Appreciation of Evidence & Presumptions

Presumptions are inferences drawn by the court as to the existence of certain facts without direct proof.

*   **May Presume — Sec. 2(h)**: Whenever it is provided by BSS that the Court may presume a fact, it may either regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved, or may call for proof of it (discretionary presumption of fact).
*   **Shall Presume — Sec. 2(l)**: Whenever it is directed by BSS that the Court shall presume a fact, it shall regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved (mandatory presumption of law).
*   **Conclusive Proof — Sec. 2(b)**: When one fact is declared by BSS to be conclusive proof of another, the Court shall, on proof of the one fact, regard the other as proved, and shall not allow evidence to be given for the purpose of disproving it.

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **State of UP v. Deoman Upadhyaya (AIR 1960 SC 1125)**
    - *Ratio*: Constitutionality of section provisions relating to confessions and admissions. Distinguishes between statements made inside and outside custody.
2.  **Hanumant v. State of Madhya Pradesh (AIR 1952 SC 343)**
    - *Ratio*: Laid down the five golden principles ("Panchsheel") of circumstantial evidence. The chain of circumstances must be so complete as to leave no reasonable ground for a conclusion consistent with the innocence of the accused.
3.  **Bodgeshwar v. State of Maharashtra (1996)**
    - *Ratio*: Dealt with the appreciation of circumstantial evidence and standard of proof in criminal trials.

---

## 6. Model Answers (5 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 5 Marks Answer: Distinguish between "May Presume" and "Shall Presume"
**Answer**:
1.  **May Presume (Sec. 2(h) BSS)**: It represents a discretionary presumption of fact. The court has the option to presume a fact or call for independent proof. The presumption is rebuttable.
    - *Example*: Under BSS, the court may presume that a person in possession of stolen goods soon after the theft is either the thief or has received the goods knowing them to be stolen.
2.  **Shall Presume (Sec. 2(l) BSS)**: It represents a mandatory presumption of law. The court has no discretion and *must* presume the fact as proved until it is disproved.
    - *Example*: Under BSS, the court shall presume the genuineness of certified copies.
3.  **Comparison Table**:
    | Feature | May Presume | Shall Presume |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Type | Presumption of Fact | Presumption of Law |
    | Discretion | Court has discretion to presume or call for proof | Court has no discretion; must presume |
    | Rebuttal | Rebuttable | Rebuttable, but must be presumed until disproved |

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Explain the concept of "Circumstantial Evidence" and the principles governing its appreciation.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Circumstantial evidence is evidence of circumstances from which the existence of the fact in issue can be logically inferred. It is indirect evidence, contrasted with direct evidence (where a witness saw the crime happen).
2.  **Legal Admissibility**: In English and Indian law, circumstantial evidence is fully admissible and can form the sole basis of conviction if it meets the strict tests of reliability.
3.  **The Panchsheel Principles (Hanumant v. State of MP)**:
    - The circumstances from which the conclusion of guilt is to be drawn must be fully established.
    - The facts so established must be consistent only with the hypothesis of the guilt of the accused.
    - The circumstances must be of a conclusive nature and tendency.
    - They must exclude every possible hypothesis except the one to be proved.
    - There must be a chain of evidence so complete as not to leave any reasonable ground for the conclusion consistent with the innocence of the accused and show that in all human probability the act must have been done by the accused.
4.  **Landmark Cases**:
    - *Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra (1984)*: Reaffirmed the Hanumant rules and held that if two views are possible, the view favorable to the accused must be taken.
5.  **Conclusion**: Circumstantial evidence is not inferior to direct evidence; rather, as the maxim goes, "witnesses may lie, but circumstances do not."

# Module 2: Relevance and Admissibility

## Introduction
Relevancy and Admissibility are the two foundational pillars of the Law of Evidence. Section 3 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023 (BSS) mandates that evidence may be given in any suit or proceeding of the existence or non-existence of every fact in issue, and of such other facts as are declared to be relevant under Sections 4 to 50 of the Sanhita, and of no others.

*   **Logical Relevancy**: When one fact is connected to another by logic, common sense, or human experience.
*   **Legal Relevancy**: When a fact is declared relevant by the provisions of the Evidence law (Sections 4 to 50 of BSS). Only legally relevant facts are admissible.
*   **Admissibility**: The process by which the court allows a relevant fact to be brought on record as evidence. Admissibility is a question of law determined by the judge.

---

## 1. Key Doctrines of Relevancy (BSS Sections 4 to 14)

### A. Doctrine of Res Gestae — Sec. 4 BSS
Facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the same transaction, are relevant, whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places.
*   **Meaning**: Latin for "things done". It is an exception to the hearsay rule. It covers spontaneous declarations made during, immediately before, or immediately after the occurrence of the transaction.
*   **Illustration**: A is accused of the murder of B by beating him. Whatever was said or done by A or B or the by-standers at the beating, or so shortly before or after it as to form part of the transaction, is a relevant fact.
*   **Key Case**: *Sukhar v. State of UP (1999)*: Held that for a statement to be relevant under Res Gestae, it must be spontaneous and contemporaneous with the act, leaving no time for fabrication.

### B. Test Identification Parade (TIP) — Sec. 7 BSS
Facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact, or which support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact, or which establish the identity of any thing or person whose identity is relevant, are relevant.
*   **Meaning**: Conducted during investigation by a Magistrate to test the reliability of a witness’s memory. The witness identifies the suspect from a lineup of similar persons.
*   **Key Case**: *State of Maharashtra v. Sukhdev Singh (1992)*: Held that TIP is corroborative evidence, not substantive, but is of great value to test the veracity of the witness.

### C. Evidence of Common Intention — Sec. 8 BSS
Where there is reasonable ground to believe that two or more persons have conspired together to commit an offence, anything said, done, or written by any one of such persons in reference to their common intention, after the time when such intention was first entertained by any one of them, is a relevant fact against each of the conspirators.
*   **Key Case**: *Mirza Akbar v. Emperor (1940)*: Clarified that statements made after the conspiracy has ended or the objective is achieved are not relevant under this section.

### D. Plea of Alibi — Sec. 9 BSS (Facts showing inconsistency)
Facts not otherwise relevant are relevant:
1. If they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact.
2. If by themselves or in connection with other facts they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable.
*   **Meaning**: "Alibi" means elsewhere. The accused pleads that they were at a different place at the time of the offence, making it impossible for them to commit the crime.
*   **Burden of Proof**: The burden to prove the plea of alibi is strictly on the accused.
*   **Key Case**: *Dudh Nath Pandey v. State of UP (1981)*: The plea of alibi must be proved with absolute certainty, showing physical impossibility of the accused's presence at the scene of the crime.

---

## 2. Admissions — Secs. 15 to 21, 25 BSS

An **Admission** is a statement, oral or documentary or contained in electronic form, which suggests any inference as to any fact in issue or relevant fact, made by any of the persons and under the circumstances mentioned in BSS.

### General Principles:
- Can be made by a party to the proceeding, their agent, or interested parties (Sec. 16, 17).
- Admission in civil cases (Sec. 25 BSS): No admission is relevant in civil cases if it is made upon an express condition that evidence of it is not to be given, or under circumstances from which the court can infer that the parties agreed together that evidence of it should not be given (without prejudice).

---

## 3. Confessions — Secs. 22 to 24 BSS

A **Confession** is an admission made at any time by a person charged with a crime, stating or suggesting the inference that he committed that crime.

### Key Provisions under BSS:
- **Confession before Police — Sec. 23 BSS**: A confession made to a police officer is inadmissible and cannot be proved against the accused.
- **Confession in Police Custody — Sec. 23 BSS Exception**: A confession made in police custody is inadmissible unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate.
- **Discovery of Fact — Sec. 23(2) BSS (formerly Sec. 27 IEA)**: When any fact is deposed to as discovered in consequence of information received from a person accused of any offence, in the custody of a police officer, so much of such information, whether it amounts to a confession or not, as relates distinctly to the fact thereby discovered, may be proved.
- **Confession by Co-Accused — Sec. 24 BSS**: When more persons than one are being tried jointly for the same offence, and a confession made by one of such persons affecting himself and some other of such persons is proved, the Court may take into consideration such confession as against such other person as well as against the person who makes such confession.

---

## 4. Differences between Admission and Confession

| Basis | Admission | Confession |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | General statement suggesting inference in civil or criminal cases | Specific statement admitting guilt in criminal cases |
| Proceedings | Applicable to both civil and criminal proceedings | Applicable only to criminal proceedings |
| Evidentiary Value | Not conclusive proof, but acts as estoppel | Substantive evidence if voluntary and truthful |
| Police Presence | Admission to police is generally admissible | Confession to police is strictly inadmissible |

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor (AIR 1947 PC 67)**
    - *Ratio*: Interpreted Section 27 of the IEA (now Sec. 23(2) BSS). Held that "discovery of fact" means the discovery of the physical object in connection with the mental knowledge of the accused, and only the distinct info leading to discovery is admissible.
2.  **Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor (AIR 1939 PC 47)**
    - *Ratio*: Defined confession. Held that a confession must either admit in terms the offence, or at any rate substantially all the facts which constitute the offence. An admission of a gravely incriminating fact is not of itself a confession.
3.  **Bhuboni Sahu v. The King (1949)**
    - *Ratio*: Dealt with confession of co-accused. Held that it is a weak type of evidence and cannot be used as the foundation of a conviction; it can only be used to lend assurance to other evidence.

---

## 6. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Explain the Doctrine of Res Gestae with relevant case laws.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Under Section 4 of BSS, facts forming part of the same transaction are relevant. Res Gestae is a Latin phrase meaning "things done". It is an exception to the hearsay rule.
2.  **Essentials**:
    - The statement or act must be contemporaneous with the transaction.
    - There must be no interval which would allow fabrication or concoction.
    - It must explain, illustrate, or be connected with the main transaction.
3.  **Hearsay Exception**: Hearsay is generally excluded because it lacks an oath and cross-examination. Res Gestae is admitted because the spontaneity of the statement under stress of the event guarantees its truthfulness.
4.  **Case Laws**:
    - *R v. Bedingfield (1879)*: The throat of a woman was cut, and she ran out of the room crying "Oh dear, Aunt, see what Bedingfield has done to me!" Held inadmissible as Res Gestae because the transaction was already over. This is a very strict English view.
    - *R v. Foster (1834)*: A witness saw a speeding carriage and later heard the victim groan. The victim's statement explaining the accident was held admissible.
    - *Sawaldas v. State of Bihar (1974)*: The cry of the deceased "save me, my husband is killing me" heard by neighbors was held admissible under Res Gestae.
5.  **Conclusion**: Res Gestae is a highly valuable exception, but the court must apply it cautiously to prevent hearsay from bypassing the rules of reliability.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Critically analyze the admissibility of confessions made by an accused under police custody and the scope of "Discovery of Fact" under BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Under BSS, confessions made to a police officer or under custody are viewed with suspicion due to the fear of torture and coercion. Section 23 of BSS lays down the general rule of exclusion.
2.  **General Exclusionary Rule**:
    - Section 23(1) BSS: Confession to police is inadmissible.
    - Rationale: To protect individuals from custodial torture and ensure police rely on scientific investigation rather than forced confessions.
3.  **The Exception — Discovery of Fact (Sec. 23(2) BSS)**:
    - When an accused in police custody gives information that leads to the discovery of a distinct fact (e.g., weapon of offence, stolen money, dead body), that portion of the information which distinctly relates to the discovery is admissible.
4.  **Scope of Sec. 23(2) / Pulukuri Kottaya case**:
    - The Privy Council held that "fact discovered" is not just the physical object, but the place from where it is produced and the accused's knowledge of it.
    - *Example*: "I hid the knife used to kill B under the roof of my house" -> Only the fact that he hid the knife under the roof is admissible; the statement "used to kill B" is inadmissible confession.
5.  **Constitutional Validity**:
    - *State of UP v. Deoman Upadhyaya (1960)*: Upheld the validity of this provision under Article 14 of the Constitution.
    - *Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010)*: Held that narco-analysis or lie detector tests conducted forcibly violate Article 20(3) (self-incrimination) and cannot be admitted under the guise of discovery of fact.
6.  **Conclusion**: Section 23(2) of BSS is a powerful tool for investigators, but must be strictly construed to safeguard the constitutional rights of the accused.

# Module 3: Statements by Persons (Dying Declarations & Expert Opinions)

## Introduction
Module 3 deals with statements made by persons under special circumstances, including statements by individuals who cannot be called as witnesses (e.g., deceased or unavailable persons), relevancy of expert opinions, judgments of courts, and character evidence. 

These provisions govern exceptions to the hearsay rule, allowing secondary statements or expert analysis on record when direct testimony is impossible or requires technical expertise.

---

## 1. Statements by Persons Who Cannot be Called as Witnesses (BSS Sections 26 & 27)

Section 26 of BSS (formerly Section 32 of IEA) outlines cases in which statements of relevant facts by a person who is dead, who cannot be found, who has become incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay or expense, are relevant:

### A. Dying Declaration — Sec. 26(1) BSS
A statement made by a person as to the cause of his death, or as to any of the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death, in cases in which the cause of that person's death comes into question.
*   **Admissibility Rules**:
    - Admissible in both civil and criminal proceedings.
    - The person making the statement must be dead at the time of the trial.
    - Rationale: *Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri* (A man will not meet his Maker with a lie in his mouth).
*   **Key Case**: *Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor (1939)*: The deceased received a letter inviting him to collect a debt. He told his wife he was going to that place, and his dismembered body was later found. His statement to his wife was held admissible as a circumstance of the transaction resulting in his death.
*   **Key Case**: *Kaushal Rao v. State of Bombay (AIR 1958 SC 22)*: The Supreme Court held that a dying declaration can form the sole basis of conviction without corroboration, provided it is voluntary, truthful, and recorded properly.

---

## 2. Relevancy of Expert Witness — Secs. 39 to 45 BSS

Section 39 BSS (formerly Section 45 of IEA) governs the opinion of experts:
When the Court has to form an opinion upon a point of foreign law, science, art, or as to identity of handwriting or finger impressions, the opinions upon that point of persons specially skilled in such foreign law, science, art, or in questions as to identity of handwriting or finger impressions are relevant facts. Such persons are called experts.

### A. Key Provisions:
- **Expert vs Ordinary Witness**: An ordinary witness testifies to facts perceived by their senses (what they saw/heard). An expert testifies to opinions and conclusions drawn from scientific or technical analysis.
- **Evidentiary Value**: Expert opinion is corroborative and advisory in nature. The court is not bound by it and must form its own independent opinion (*State of HP v. Jailal, 1999*).
- **Handwriting and Electronic Expert Opinions**: Section 39 extends relevancy to digital and electronic signatures, and Section 40 provides for the opinion of the Examiner of Electronic Evidence (IT Act, 2000).

---

## 3. Judgments of Courts when Relevant — Secs. 34 to 38 BSS

These sections govern the relevancy of prior judgments:
- **Res Judicata**: A final judgment in a prior case between the same parties is relevant to bar a second suit or trial (Sec. 34 BSS).
- **Judgments in Rem (Sec. 35 BSS)**: Judgments in probate, matrimonial, admiralty, or insolvency proceedings are conclusive proof of legal character and are binding on the whole world (in rem), not just parties to the suit (in personam).

---

## 4. Evidence of Character — Secs. 46 to 50 BSS

Character evidence is generally excluded unless it is directly in issue:
- **Civil Cases (Sec. 46 BSS)**: Character to prove conduct imputed is irrelevant in civil cases, except in so far as such character appears from facts otherwise relevant.
- **Criminal Cases (Sec. 47 & 48 BSS)**: In criminal proceedings, the fact that the accused person has a good character is relevant. However, the fact that he has a bad character is irrelevant, unless evidence has been given that he has a good character, in which case it becomes relevant (rebuttal).
- **Exceptions**: Bad character is relevant where the bad character of any person is itself a fact in issue (e.g., under dacoity or bad livelihood acts).

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra (1984 4 SCC 116)**
    - *Ratio*: Re-emphasized the value of dying declarations and laid down that statements made by the deceased regarding her mental state and distress prior to death are admissible as circumstances of the transaction.
2.  **State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu (2005 11 SCC 600)**
    - *Ratio*: Parliament Attack case. Discussed the admissibility of mobile phone records and the role of expert opinion in digital forensics.

---

## 6. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Explain the evidentiary value and admissibility of a Dying Declaration under BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Definition**: Section 26(1) of BSS defines a dying declaration as a statement made by a person who is dead, explaining the cause of his death or circumstances of the transaction leading to his death.
2.  **Rationale**: A dying person is highly unlikely to lie, as they face imminent death. *Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri*.
3.  **Admissibility Conditions**:
    - The declarant must have died after making the statement. If they survive, it is not a dying declaration (but can be used as a prior statement under Sec. 160 BSS to corroborate or contradict).
    - Must relate to the cause of death.
    - Declarant must be in a fit mental condition to make the statement (Magistrates usually require a doctor's certificate of fitness).
4.  **Sole Basis of Conviction (Kaushal Rao case)**:
    - There is no absolute rule of law that a dying declaration cannot form the sole basis of conviction.
    - If the court is satisfied that the declaration was true, voluntary, and free from prompting, a conviction can be based on it without corroboration.
5.  **Conclusion**: It is a unique and powerful exception to the hearsay rule, but requires careful judicial scrutiny.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Discuss the relevancy of Expert Opinion and the problems associated with Expert Testimony under BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Section 39 of BSS allows the court to admit opinions of persons skilled in science, art, foreign law, handwriting, or finger impressions when the court itself lacks that specialized knowledge.
2.  **Who is an Expert**: An expert is someone who has acquired special knowledge, skill, or experience in a field through study or practice (e.g., doctors, ballistic experts, handwriting examiners).
3.  **Evidentiary Value**:
    - It is not substantive evidence; it is merely corroborative and opinion-based.
    - The opinion is advisory. The judge must evaluate the reasons given by the expert and make the final decision.
4.  **Conflict of Opinions**: When two experts give conflicting reports (e.g., one doctor says death was by suicide, another says homicide), the court must look at the direct eye-witness evidence or other circumstantial evidence to resolve the conflict (*Ram Narain v. State of UP*).
5.  **Digital Forensics under BSS**: BSS has modernized expert opinions to include Examiner of Electronic Evidence (Sec. 40) due to the rising share of cybercrimes and electronic documents.
6.  **Conclusion**: Expert testimony is essential in modern trials, but it remains an aid to the court rather than a replacement of judicial decision-making.

# Module 4: Documentary Evidence & Electronic Records

## Introduction
Documentary evidence is governed by Sections 56 to 103 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023 (BSS). Under BSS, documents are given precedence over oral evidence, and oral agreements cannot be admitted to contradict the terms of a written document (exclusion of oral by documentary evidence). 

Additionally, BSS has significantly modernized the legal framework by placing electronic and digital records on the same footing as physical documents.

---

## 1. Primary and Secondary Evidence (BSS Sections 57 to 60)

### A. Primary Evidence — Sec. 57 BSS
Primary evidence means the document itself produced for the inspection of the Court.
*   **Meaning**: The original source document.
*   **Explanation**: Where a document is executed in several parts, each part is primary evidence of the document. Where a document is executed in counterpart, each counterpart is primary evidence against the party executing it.

### B. Secondary Evidence — Sec. 58 BSS
Secondary evidence means and includes:
1. Certified copies.
2. Copies made from the original by mechanical processes which in themselves assure the accuracy of the copy.
3. Copies made from or compared with the original.
4. Counterparts of documents as against the parties who did not execute them.
5. Oral accounts of the contents of a document given by some person who has himself seen it.

### C. Rule of Admissibility — Sec. 60 BSS
Secondary evidence may be given of the existence, condition, or contents of a document only under specific circumstances, such as:
- When the original is in the possession of the adverse party who refuses to produce it.
- When the original has been destroyed or lost.
- When the original is of such a nature that it cannot be easily moved (e.g., an inscription on a wall).
- When the original is a public document.

---

## 2. Electronic and Digital Records — Secs. 61 to 63 BSS

BSS, 2023 has overhauled the admissibility of electronic evidence to simplify procedures while maintaining integrity:

- **Section 61 BSS**: Establishes that the contents of electronic records may be proved in accordance with the provisions of Section 62.
- **Section 62 BSS**: Places electronic records under the definition of primary evidence if they are original media or simultaneous outputs.
- **Section 63 BSS (formerly Sec. 65B of IEA)**: Sets out the conditions for admissibility of electronic records. An electronic record (emails, phone logs, CCTV, hard drives) is admissible as evidence without further proof or production of the original, provided it is accompanied by a **Certificate** signed by a person in charge of the device or system, declaring that the computer/system was operating properly and the data was not tampered with.

*   **Key Case**: *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014)*: The Supreme Court held that the certificate under Section 65B IEA (now Sec. 63 BSS) is mandatory for admissibility of secondary electronic evidence.
*   **Key Case**: *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020)*: Clarified that the certificate is a condition precedent to admissibility when the original device cannot be produced in court.

---

## 3. Public and Private Documents — Secs. 74 to 77 BSS

- **Public Documents (Sec. 74 BSS)**: Documents forming the acts or records of the acts of sovereign authorities, official bodies, tribunals, and public officers (e.g., acts, birth certificates, police reports, records of court).
- **Private Documents (Sec. 75 BSS)**: All other documents that are not public documents (e.g., contracts, wills, private letters).
- **Evidentiary Value**: Public documents can be proved by producing certified copies (Sec. 77 BSS). Private documents must be proved by producing the original and proving execution (attestation).

---

## 4. Exclusion of Oral by Documentary Evidence — Secs. 94 to 103 BSS

- **Section 94 BSS (formerly Sec. 91 IEA)**: When the terms of a contract, grant, or disposition of property have been reduced to the form of a document, no evidence shall be given in proof of such terms except the document itself or secondary evidence of its contents where admissible.
- **Section 95 BSS (formerly Sec. 92 IEA)**: When the terms of any such contract, etc. have been proved by the document, no evidence of any oral agreement or statement shall be admitted as between the parties for the purpose of contradicting, varying, adding to, or subtracting from its terms.
- **Exceptions**: Oral evidence can be given to show fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution, or separate oral agreements where the document is silent (collateral agreements).

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **State of Delhi v. Shri Ram Lohia (AIR 1960 SC 490)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed the rules for proving the execution of documents and the limits of secondary evidence.
2.  **Kundan Singh v. State of UP (2023)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed the requirements of certificates for admissibility of digital data.

---

## 6. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Distinguish between Primary and Secondary Evidence. Under what conditions is Secondary Evidence admissible?
**Answer**:
1.  **Definitions**:
    - **Primary Evidence (Sec. 57 BSS)**: The original document itself produced for court inspection.
    - **Secondary Evidence (Sec. 58 BSS)**: Copies, certified duplicates, or oral accounts of the document.
2.  **Evidentiary Weight**: Primary evidence is the best evidence and is highly preferred. Secondary evidence is of lower priority and is only admitted under exceptions.
3.  **Conditions for Admissibility of Secondary Evidence (Sec. 60 BSS)**:
    - **Lost or Destroyed**: When the original has been lost or destroyed.
    - **Possession of Opponent**: When the original is with the opposite party who has been given notice to produce it but fails to do so.
    - **Immovable Original**: When the original cannot be easily moved (e.g., inscription on a rock).
    - **Public Document**: When the original is a public document.
4.  **Conclusion**: Secondary evidence is admissible only when the absence of the primary document is satisfactorily explained to the court.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Critically examine the legal framework governing the admissibility of Electronic and Digital Records under Section 63 of BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Electronic records are now the most common form of evidence. BSS Section 61 to 63 provides a specialized statutory code for their admissibility, recognizing the risk of digital tampering.
2.  **Admissibility Conditions under Section 63 BSS**:
    - The computer output containing the information was produced by the computer during the period over which the computer was used regularly to store or process information.
    - The computer was operating properly.
    - The information contained in the electronic record reproduces or is derived from the information fed into the computer in the ordinary course of activities.
3.  **The Certificate Requirement**:
    - Any secondary electronic record (like a printout of an email or copy of a WhatsApp chat on a CD) must be accompanied by a **Section 63 Certificate**.
    - The certificate must identify the electronic record, describe the manner of its production, give details of the device, and be signed by a person in charge of the device.
4.  **Landmark Judicial Trends**:
    - *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014)*: The Supreme Court ruled that Section 65B of IEA (equivalent to Section 63 BSS) is a complete code, and oral evidence cannot bypass it.
    - *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020)*: The court ruled that the certificate is mandatory for secondary electronic records, but if the original device is brought to court, no certificate is needed.
5.  **BSS, 2023 Improvements**: BSS clarifies that if electronic records are stored on primary servers or clouds in the ordinary course, they can be treated as primary evidence directly.
6.  **Conclusion**: The certification requirement is a necessary safeguard to prevent deepfakes and manipulated digital evidence from polluting the judicial record.

# Module 5: Witnesses (Competency, Compellability & Privileges)

## Introduction
Witnesses play a critical role in the judicial system; they are described by Jeremy Bentham as the "eyes and ears of justice." Module 5 covers the rules governing who can testify (competency), who can be forced to testify (compellability), the protection of confidential information (privileged communications), and the status of accomplices.

---

## 1. Competency and Compellability of Witnesses (BSS Sections 124 & 125)

*   **Competency — Sec. 124 BSS (formerly Sec. 118 IEA)**: All persons shall be competent to testify unless the Court considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions put to them, or from giving rational answers to those questions, by tender years, extreme old age, disease, whether of body or mind, or any other cause of the same kind.
    - *Child Witness*: A child is a competent witness if they can understand the questions and give rational answers. The court usually conducts a *voir dire* test (preliminary examination) to test the child's understanding.
    - *Lunatic Witness*: A lunatic is not incompetent to testify, unless he is prevented by his lunacy from understanding the questions and giving rational answers.
*   **Dumb/Deaf Witness — Sec. 125 BSS (formerly Sec. 119 IEA)**: A witness who is unable to speak may give his evidence in any other manner in which he can make it intelligible, as by writing or by signs. Such writing must be written and the signs made in open Court. Evidence so given shall be deemed to be oral evidence.

---

## 2. Privileged Communications (BSS Sections 128 to 134)

Privileged communications are confidential disclosures which the law protects from being forced into evidence, to preserve relationships and state interests:

- **Matrimonial Privilege — Sec. 128 BSS**: No person who is or has been married shall be compelled to disclose any communication made to him during marriage by any person to whom he is or has been married, unless the person who made it, or his representative in interest, consents, except in suits between married partners or in criminal proceedings for crimes committed by one against the other.
- **State Privilege — Sec. 129 BSS**: No one shall be permitted to give any evidence derived from unpublished official records relating to any affairs of State, except with the permission of the officer at the head of the department concerned, who shall give or withhold such permission as he thinks fit.
- **Official Communications — Sec. 130 BSS**: No public officer shall be compelled to disclose communications made to him in official confidence, when he considers that the public interests would suffer by the disclosure.
- **Information as to Commission of Offences — Sec. 131 BSS**: No Magistrate or police officer shall be compelled to say whence he got any information as to the commission of any offence, and no revenue officer shall be compelled to say whence he got any information as to the commission of any offence against the public revenue.
- **Professional Privilege — Sec. 132-134 BSS**: No legal practitioner (advocate, attorney) shall be permitted, unless with his client's express consent, to disclose any communication made to him in the course and for the purpose of his employment.
    - *Exception*: Any communication made in furtherance of any illegal purpose or any fact showing that a crime or fraud has been committed since the employment.

---

## 3. Accomplice Evidence — Sec. 138 BSS (formerly Sec. 133 IEA)

An **Accomplice** is an associate in crime, a partner in guilt. 
*   **Evidentiary Position**: Section 138 BSS states that an accomplice shall be a competent witness against an accused person; and a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds upon the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice.
*   **The Conflict with Presumptions (BSS Sec. 117 Illustration (b))**: The court may presume that an accomplice is unworthy of credit, unless he is corroborated in material particulars.
*   **Reconciliation (Dagdu v. State of Maharashtra)**: The Supreme Court reconciled the two provisions by stating that while a conviction based on uncorroborated accomplice evidence is technically *legal* under Section 133 IEA (now Sec. 138 BSS), prudence and long judicial practice dictate that the court should not convict unless the accomplice's testimony is corroborated by independent evidence in material particulars linking the accused to the crime.

---

## 4. Landmark Judgments

1.  **Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010 7 SCC 263)**
    - *Ratio*: Addressed the self-incrimination clause and held that compulsory administration of narco-analysis, polygraph, or brain mapping violates Article 20(3) of the Constitution and Section 132 BSS protections.
2.  **State of Bihar v. Kalu Miah (1975)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed the admissibility and reliability of deaf and dumb witness testimony given through signs.

---

## 5. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Discuss the competency of a Child Witness. How does the court appreciate their testimony?
**Answer**:
1.  **Competency**: Section 124 BSS states that competency is determined by the capacity of the witness to understand questions and give rational answers, not by age. A child is a competent witness.
2.  **Voir Dire Test**: Before recording a child's evidence, the judge conducts a preliminary examination (voir dire) by asking general questions (e.g., about their school, family) to test if the child has sufficient intelligence to understand and give rational answers.
3.  **Appreciation of Child Evidence**:
    - Child evidence is viewed as "frail" because children are highly suggestible, easily coached, and have active imaginations.
    - The court must look for independent corroboration.
    - *Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan (1952)*: The Supreme Court held that while corroboration is highly desirable as a rule of prudence, it is not an absolute rule of law. If the child’s evidence is highly natural and convincing, a conviction can stand.
4.  **Conclusion**: A child witness is legally competent, but the court must exercise extreme caution to rule out coaching or fantasy before relying on their testimony.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Critically analyze the law relating to Privileged Communications under BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Privileged communications represent a balance between the search for truth and the protection of confidential relationships and public interest. Under BSS, certain communications are protected from disclosure.
2.  **Matrimonial Privilege (Sec. 128 BSS)**:
    - Protects the sanctity of marriage. Communications between spouses during marriage are protected even after divorce.
    - *Exceptions*: Suits between the spouses, or criminal trials where one spouse is prosecuted for a crime against the other.
3.  **Legal Professional Privilege (Sec. 132 BSS)**:
    - Protects client-attorney trust. An advocate cannot disclose communications made by the client during professional employment.
    - *Exceptions*: Furtherance of illegal purpose, or discovery of fraud/crime committed after the employment began.
4.  **State Privilege (Sec. 129 BSS)**:
    - Protects the state's security. No one can give evidence from unpublished records of State affairs without permission.
    - *State of Punjab v. Sodhi Sukhdev Singh (1961)*: The court held that while the head of the department decides on the privilege, the court has the power to inspect the document in chambers to verify if it relates to affairs of state.
5.  **Conclusion**: Privileged communication is an essential shield in a democratic legal system, but it must not be allowed to act as a cloak for criminal activities.

# Module 6: Burden of Proof & Presumptions

## Introduction
The Burden of Proof is a central concept in procedural law that determines which party has the duty to prove a fact to the court. Module 6 covers the general principles of the burden of proof, the distinction between the legal burden and the evidentiary burden, statutory presumptions in criminal trials, and the concepts of judicial notice and facts admitted.

---

## 1. General Principles of Burden of Proof (BSS Sections 104 to 107)

*   **Legal Burden — Sec. 104 BSS (formerly Sec. 101 IEA)**: Whoever desires any Court to give judgment as to any legal right or liability dependent on the existence of facts which he asserts, must prove that those facts exist.
    - *Criminal Case*: The burden of proving the guilt of the accused beyond reasonable doubt is always on the prosecution. This burden never shifts.
    - *Civil Case*: The burden lies on the plaintiff to prove the facts asserting their claim.
*   **Evidentiary Burden (Onus of Proof) — Sec. 105 BSS (formerly Sec. 102 IEA)**: The burden of proof in a suit or proceeding lies on that person who would fail if no evidence at all were given on either side.
    - *Shifting Onus*: Unlike the legal burden under Sec. 104, the onus of proof under Sec. 105 shifts continuously from one party to the other during the trial as evidence is led.

---

## 2. Exceptions and Specific Burden Rules (BSS Sections 108 to 116)

- **Proof of Particular Fact — Sec. 106 BSS**: The burden of proof as to any particular fact lies on that person who wishes the Court to believe in its existence, unless it is provided by law that the proof of that fact shall lie on any particular person.
- **Fact Especially within Knowledge — Sec. 107 BSS**: When any fact is especially within the knowledge of any person, the burden of proving that fact is upon him.
    - *Illustration*: A is charged with traveling on a railway without a ticket. The burden of proving that he had a ticket is on him.
- **Proof of General Exceptions — Sec. 108 BSS (formerly Sec. 105 IEA)**: When a person is accused of any offence, the burden of proving the existence of circumstances bringing the case within any of the General Exceptions in the BNS, or within any special exception, is upon him, and the Court shall presume the absence of such circumstances.

---

## 3. Presumptions of Law and Fact (BSS Sections 117 to 120)

BSS provides rules for presuming the existence of facts to protect vulnerable parties:
- **Presumption of Certain Facts — Sec. 117 BSS**: The court may presume the existence of any fact which it thinks likely to have happened, regard being had to the common course of natural events, human conduct, and public/private business (e.g., possession of stolen goods, accomplice untrustworthiness).
- **Presumption of Abetment of Suicide by Married Woman — Sec. 119 BSS**: If a married woman commits suicide within seven years of marriage and it is shown that her husband or his relatives subjected her to cruelty, the court may presume that such suicide was abetted by her husband.
- **Presumption as to Dowry Death — Sec. 120 BSS**: When the question is whether a person has committed the dowry death of a woman, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment in connection with dowry, the court *shall* presume that such person caused the dowry death.

---

## 4. Judicial Notice & Facts Admitted (BSS Sections 51 to 53)

Evidence is not required for facts that are obvious or already agreed upon:
- **Judicial Notice (Sec. 51 & 52 BSS)**: Facts of which the Court must take judicial notice need not be proved (e.g., laws in force in India, geographical divisions, public festivals).
- **Facts Admitted (Sec. 53 BSS)**: No fact need be proved in any proceeding which the parties thereto or their agents agree to admit at the hearing, or which, before the hearing, they agree to admit by writing.

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **KM Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (AIR 1962 SC 605)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed Section 105 of IEA (now Sec. 108 BSS). Held that the burden of proving the plea of grave and sudden provocation is on the accused, and the court shall presume the absence of such circumstances until proved otherwise.
2.  **Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer (AIR 1956 SC 404)**
    - *Ratio*: Clarified Section 106 IEA (now Sec. 107 BSS) on facts especially within knowledge. Held that this section cannot be used to shift the prosecution's primary burden of proving the ingredients of the offence; it only applies to facts which are uniquely within the accused's knowledge and cannot be discovered by the prosecution.

---

## 6. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Distinguish between "Burden of Proof" (Sec. 104) and "Onus of Proof" (Sec. 105).
**Answer**:
1.  **Definitions**:
    - **Burden of Proof (Sec. 104 BSS)**: The legal obligation to establish a case. It rests on the party who seeks to obtain a judgment in their favor.
    - **Onus of Proof (Sec. 105 BSS)**: The evidentiary duty to lead evidence to rebut or support a fact.
2.  **Shifting of Burden**:
    - The legal Burden of Proof under Sec. 104 **never shifts**. In criminal cases, it always rests on the prosecution.
    - The evidentiary Onus of Proof under Sec. 105 **shifts continuously** during the trial from one party to the other.
3.  **Comparison Table**:
    | Feature | Burden of Proof (Sec. 104) | Onus of Proof (Sec. 105) |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Nature | Legal obligation to establish the case | Duty to lead evidence at a particular stage |
    | Shifting | Fixed; never shifts | Shifts from party to party |
    | Constant | Remains constant throughout the trial | Changes as the case develops |

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Explain the statutory presumptions regarding Dowry Death under BSS, 2023. Discuss how it shifts the burden of proof.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Dowry death is a grave social evil. To combat this, the legislature introduced a strict presumption of law under Section 120 BSS (formerly Sec. 113B IEA) to aid the prosecution.
2.  **Statutory Requirements for Presumption**:
    - The question before the court must be whether a person has committed the dowry death of a woman (defined under Section 80 BNS).
    - It must be shown that "soon before her death" she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by such person for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry.
    - The death must have occurred within 7 years of marriage.
3.  **Nature of the Presumption**:
    - The section uses the words **"shall presume"**. It is a mandatory presumption of law.
    - Once the prosecution proves that the woman was subjected to cruelty in connection with dowry demands soon before her death, the court has no option but to presume that the husband/relative caused her death.
4.  **Shifting the Burden of Proof**:
    - Once the primary conditions are proved by the prosecution, the legal burden shifts to the accused (husband) to disprove that he caused her death. He must show that her death was natural, accidental, or not related to dowry harassment.
5.  **Landmark Cases**:
    - *State of Punjab v. Iqbal Singh (1991)*: Clarified that the presumption is rebuttable, but requires the accused to lead active evidence of innocence.
    - *Rajinder Singh v. State of Punjab (2015)*: Defined the phrase "soon before" as having a live link with the demand for dowry, not necessarily immediately before.
6.  **Conclusion**: The presumption under Section 120 BSS is a necessary exception to the rule of presumption of innocence, designed to ensure justice for victims of dowry harassment.

# Module 7: Oral Evidence & Examination of Witnesses

## Introduction
Oral evidence is governed by Sections 54 to 55 and Sections 140 to 168 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023 (BSS). Under evidence law, oral evidence is the most common way of bringing facts to the record. This module covers the general principles of oral evidence, the hearsay rule, the process of examination of witnesses in court, and refreshing memory.

---

## 1. General Principles of Oral Evidence (BSS Sections 54 & 55)

*   **Proof of Facts by Oral Evidence — Sec. 54 BSS (formerly Sec. 59 IEA)**: All facts, except the contents of documents or electronic records, may be proved by oral evidence.
*   **Oral Evidence must be Direct — Sec. 55 BSS (formerly Sec. 60 IEA)**: Oral evidence must, in all cases whatever, be direct; that is to say:
    - If it refers to a fact which could be seen, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he saw it.
    - If it refers to a fact which could be heard, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he heard it.
    - If it refers to a fact which could be perceived by any other sense, it must be the evidence of a witness who says he perceived it by that sense.
    - If it refers to an opinion or the grounds on which that opinion is held, it must be the evidence of the person who holds that opinion on those grounds.

### Hearsay Evidence:
- **Meaning**: Hearsay is second-hand or derivative evidence. It is a statement made by a person who is not called as a witness, reported by a witness in court.
- **Rule of Exclusion**: Hearsay evidence is strictly inadmissible.
- **Exceptions to Hearsay Rule**:
  - Res Gestae (Sec. 4 BSS)
  - Admissions and Confessions (Sec. 15-24 BSS)
  - Dying Declarations (Sec. 26(1) BSS)
  - Public records or expert opinions in books.

---

## 2. Examination of Witnesses (BSS Sections 140 to 168)

Witnesses are examined in court in a structured order to ensure fairness and logical flow:

*   **Order of Examinations — Sec. 141 BSS (formerly Sec. 138 IEA)**:
    1.  **Examination-in-chief**: The examination of a witness by the party who calls him.
    2.  **Cross-examination**: The examination of a witness by the adverse party. (Crucial for testing truthfulness).
    3.  **Re-examination**: The examination of a witness, subsequent to the cross-examination, by the party who called him. (Used to explain matters introduced in cross-examination).
*   **Leading Questions — Sec. 144-146 BSS (formerly Sec. 141-143 IEA)**: A leading question is a question which suggests the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive (e.g., "Was the suspect wearing a red shirt?").
    - *When they must NOT be asked*: In Examination-in-chief or Re-examination, if objected to by the adverse party, except with permission of the Court.
    - *When they MAY be asked*: In Cross-examination.
*   **Hostile Witness — Sec. 157 BSS (formerly Sec. 154 IEA)**: A witness who turns against the party that called them. The Court may, in its discretion, permit the party who calls a witness to put any questions to him which might be put in cross-examination by the adverse party (including leading questions and impeaching their credit).
*   **Impeaching Credit of Witness — Sec. 158 BSS (formerly Sec. 155 IEA)**: The credit of a witness may be impeached by the adverse party or, with the consent of the Court, by the party who calls him:
    - By evidence of persons who testify that they believe him to be unworthy of credit.
    - By proof that the witness has been bribed.
    - By proof of former statements inconsistent with any part of his evidence.

---

## 3. Refreshing Memory — Sec. 162 BSS (formerly Sec. 159 IEA)

A witness may, while under examination, refresh his memory by referring to any writing made by himself at the time of the transaction concerning which he is questioned, or so soon afterwards that the Court considers it likely that the transaction was at that time fresh in his memory.
- The witness can also refer to a writing made by any other person, and read by the witness within the time aforesaid, if when he read it he knew it to be correct.

---

## 4. Landmark Judgments

1.  **State of Rajasthan v. Kartar Singh (AIR 1970 SC 1305)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed the rules for declaring a witness hostile and the evidentiary value of a hostile witness's statements (their testimony is not completely discarded; parts that support the prosecution or defence can still be accepted).
2.  **Kalyan Kumar Gogoi v. Ashutosh Agnihotri (2011 2 SCC 532)**
    - *Ratio*: Defined hearsay evidence and discussed the rationale behind its exclusion (unreliability, lack of oath, inability to cross-examine).

---

## 5. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: What are Leading Questions? When can they be asked and when can they not be asked?
**Answer**:
1.  **Definition**: Section 144 BSS defines a leading question as any question which suggests the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive.
2.  **Rules for Asking Leading Questions**:
    - **In Examination-in-Chief / Re-examination (Sec. 145 BSS)**: They must not, if objected to by the adverse party, be asked in examination-in-chief, or in a re-examination, except with the permission of the Court. The court will readily permit leading questions on matters which are introductory, undisputed, or have already been sufficiently proved.
    - **In Cross-Examination (Sec. 146 BSS)**: They may be asked in cross-examination. This is because the witness is being tested by the opposite party, and leading questions are essential to check consistency and probe for lies.
3.  **Rationale**: In chief-examination, the witness is friendly to the lawyer calling them; hence, leading questions would allow the lawyer to put words into the witness’s mouth. In cross-examination, the witness is hostile to the cross-examiner, so leading questions are necessary to test their veracity.
4.  **Conclusion**: Leading questions are vital cross-examination tools, but are strictly controlled in chief-examination to preserve the purity of witness testimony.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Explain the order of examination of witnesses. What is a "Hostile Witness" and what is the evidentiary value of their testimony under BSS, 2023?
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Witnesses are examined sequentially to establish facts. Section 141 of BSS lays down the three stages: Examination-in-chief, Cross-examination, and Re-examination.
2.  **Order of Examination**:
    - *Examination-in-chief*: Conducted by the party calling the witness. Focuses on bringing out positive facts.
    - *Cross-examination*: Conducted by the opposite party. Focuses on testing accuracy, credibility, and detecting bias.
    - *Re-examination*: Conducted by the party calling the witness to clarify any new facts brought out in cross-examination.
3.  **Hostile Witness (Sec. 157 BSS)**:
    - A hostile witness is one who is unwilling to tell the truth at the instance of the party calling him, showing bias against that party.
    - Under Section 157, the court may permit the party who called the witness to cross-examine their own witness (ask leading questions and impeach their credit).
4.  **Evidentiary Value of Hostile Witness**:
    - In English law, a hostile witness’s evidence is completely rejected.
    - In Indian law (*Kartar Singh case*), a hostile witness's testimony is **not completely wiped off** the record.
    - The court can rely on those parts of the hostile witness’s testimony which conform to other evidence and support either the prosecution or the defence.
5.  **Conclusion**: The concept of hostile witness ensures that a party is not sabotaged by their own witness turning rogue, allowing the court to still extract truth from the testimony.

# Module 8: Doctrine of Estoppel & Improper Admission

## Introduction
Module 8 covers the Doctrine of Estoppel under Sections 121 to 123 of the BSS, its comparison with Res Judicata and waiver, and the consequences of improper admission or rejection of evidence under Section 169 of BSS. 

Estoppel is an important rule of equity and civil procedure designed to prevent injustice by holding parties to their representations.

---

## 1. The Doctrine of Estoppel (BSS Sections 121 to 123)

*   **Estoppel — Sec. 121 BSS (formerly Sec. 115 IEA)**: When one person has, by his declaration, act or omission, intentionally caused or permitted another person to believe a thing to be true and to act upon such belief, neither he nor his representative shall be allowed, in any suit or proceeding between himself and such person or his representative, to deny the truth of that thing.
    - *Meaning*: It stops or debars a person from blowing hot and cold at the same time. It is a rule of pleading and evidence.
    - *Illustration*: A intentionally and falsely leads B to believe that certain land belongs to A, and thereby induces B to buy and pay for it. The land afterwards becomes the property of A, and A seeks to set aside the sale on the ground that, at the time of the sale, he had no title. He must not be allowed to prove his want of title.
*   **Tenant and Licensee Estoppel — Sec. 122 BSS**: No tenant of immovable property, or person claiming through such tenant, shall, during the continuance of the tenancy, be permitted to deny that the landlord of such tenant had, at the beginning of the tenancy, a title to such immovable property.
*   **Acceptor of Bill of Exchange Estoppel — Sec. 123 BSS**: No acceptor of a bill of exchange shall be permitted to deny that the drawer had authority to draw such bill or to endorse it.

---

## 2. Promissory Estoppel

Promissory Estoppel is an equitable doctrine developed by courts to enforce promises made by one party to another, which the other party acted upon to their detriment, even in the absence of a formal contract.
*   **Key Case**: *Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of UP (1979)*: The Supreme Court held that the government was bound by its promise to exempt the sugar mill from sales tax, as the mill owner had set up the factory acting on that promise. Promissory Estoppel applies against the government in its administrative capacity.
*   **Exception**: No estoppel lies against the law or the Constitution (e.g., the government cannot be estopped from enforcing a statute).

---

## 3. Comparison: Estoppel, Res Judicata, and Waiver

| Feature | Estoppel | Res Judicata | Waiver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

## 4. Effect of Improper Admission and Rejection of Evidence — Sec. 169 BSS (formerly Sec. 167 IEA)

The improper admission or rejection of evidence shall not be ground of itself for a new trial or reversal of any decision in any case:
1. If it appears to the Court that, independently of the evidence objected to and admitted, there was sufficient evidence to justify the decision.
2. If, reject the evidence, the rejected evidence, if received, ought not to have varied the decision.

*   **Evidentiary Rule**: A trial decision will not be set aside on appeal simply because the trial judge admitted some inadmissible evidence or rejected some admissible evidence, provided the overall record contains enough valid evidence to support the judgment.

---

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **Pickard v. Sears (1837 6 Ad & El 469)**
    - *Ratio*: The foundation case of Estoppel in English law. Held that where a party by their conduct willfully causes another to believe in a state of things, and induces them to act on it, the former is precluded from averring a different state of things.
2.  **Sharat Chunder Dey v. Gopal Chunder Laha (1892)**
    - *Ratio*: Clarified that representation under estoppel need not be fraudulent; even if made under mistake or innocently, if the other party acted on it and suffered detriment, estoppel applies.

## 6. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Explain Section 169 BSS regarding the effect of Improper Admission and Rejection of Evidence.
**Answer**:
1.  **Statutory Principle**: Section 169 of BSS establishes that procedural errors regarding admission or rejection of evidence are not fatal to a decision, provided substantial justice has been done.
2.  **Improper Admission**:
    - If some hearsay or irrelevant evidence was wrongly admitted by the trial court, the appellate court will not order a retrial if there is other sufficient, independent, and legally admissible evidence on record to justify the conviction or decree.
3.  **Improper Rejection**:
    - If some valid evidence was wrongly rejected by the trial court, the appellate court will not interfere if it finds that even if that rejected evidence had been admitted, it would not have changed the final outcome of the case.
4.  **Rationale**: To prevent unnecessary delays, endless litigation, and retrials over minor technical errors that do not affect the core merits of the case.
5.  **Conclusion**: Section 169 is a pragmatic rule that prioritizes substantive justice over technical procedural compliance.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Critically analyze the Doctrine of Estoppel under BSS, 2023. Can Estoppel be pleaded against the State or against a Statute?
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Section 121 of BSS codifies the Doctrine of Estoppel, which prevents a person from denying a statement of fact they previously made, after another person has acted on it to their detriment.
2.  **Essentials of Estoppel**:
    - There must be a representation made by a person to another through a declaration, act, or omission.
    - The representation must be intentional, causing the other person to believe it to be true.
    - The other person must act upon such belief to their detriment.
3.  **Estoppel against the State (Promissory Estoppel)**:
    - *Motilal Padampat case*: Promissory Estoppel can be pleaded against the State when public authorities make promises (like tax holidays or land grants) acting in an administrative capacity, and citizens act on those promises.
4.  **No Estoppel against a Statute (No Estoppel against Law)**:
    - Estoppel cannot be used to defeat the law. If a representation is contrary to an Act of Parliament, the person making the representation is not bound by it.
    - *Example*: A minor represents himself as a major and enters a contract. He cannot be estopped from pleading minority later to declare the contract void (*Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*), because the law declares minor agreements void ab initio.
5.  **No Estoppel against the Constitution**:
    - Fundamental rights cannot be waived, and no estoppel can lie against constitutional provisions (*Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation*).
6.  **Conclusion**: Estoppel is a valuable shield of equity, but it must bow down to statutory and constitutional mandates.
