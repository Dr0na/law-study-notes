# Module 1: Introduction to Law of Evidence & Key Definitions

## Introduction
The Law of Evidence is a procedural law that governs the rules and principles regarding the admissibility, relevancy, and weight of evidence in judicial proceedings. In India, the law was historically governed by the **Indian Evidence Act, 1872** (IEA), which was drafted by Sir James Fitzjames Stephen. To modernize the legal system, integrate digital advancements, and remove colonial terminology, the Indian Parliament enacted the **Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023** (BSS), which received Presidential assent on December 25, 2023, and came into force on **1st July 2024**.

Evidence law acts as the bridge between substantive law (like Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023) and procedural law (like Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023). It ensures that judicial decisions are based on facts proved through a standardized, rational process rather than conjecture or hearsay.

---

## 1. Central Conceptions and Definitions under BSS, 2023

Section 2 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Sanhita, 2023 consolidates the primary legal definitions:

### A. Fact — Sec. 2(f)
A "Fact" means and includes:
1. Any thing, state of things, or relation of things, capable of being perceived by the senses (Physical Facts).
2. Any mental condition of which any person is conscious (Psychological Facts).

*   **Illustration 1 (Physical Fact)**: That a man heard or saw something; that there are certain goods in a room; that a man uttered certain words.
*   **Illustration 2 (Psychological Fact)**: That a man has a certain reputation; that a man acted in good faith, with fraudulent intent, or maliciously.

### B. Relevant Fact — Sec. 2(k)
One fact is said to be relevant to another when the one is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of the BSS relating to the relevancy of facts (Sections 3 to 50).
*   **Legal Position**: Relevance is determined solely by law (legal relevancy), not just by logic (logical relevancy).

### C. Facts in Issue — Sec. 2(g)
"Facts in issue" means and includes any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature or extent of any right, liability or disability, asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding, necessarily follows.
*   **Meaning**: These are the principal facts which the plaintiff/prosecution must prove to establish their case, or which the defendant/accused must prove to establish a defence.
*   **Illustration**: A is accused of the murder of B. In trial, the facts in issue are:
    - That A caused B’s death.
    - That A intended to cause B’s death.
    - That A had received grave and sudden provocation from B.

## 2. Classification of Evidence — Sec. 2(e)

"Evidence" means and includes:
1.  **Oral Evidence**: All statements which the court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses, in relation to matters of fact under inquiry.
2.  **Documentary Evidence**: All documents including electronic or digital records produced for the inspection of the Court.

### Sub-Classifications:
*   **Primary Evidence (Sec. 2(d) & Sec. 57)**: The document itself produced for the inspection of the court (e.g., the original contract, original letter).
*   **Secondary Evidence (Sec. 2(d) & Sec. 58)**: Certified copies, copies made from the original by mechanical processes, oral accounts of the contents of a document by a person who has seen it, etc.
*   **Direct Evidence**: Evidence of a witness who saw, heard, or perceived the fact with their own senses.
*   **Circumstantial Evidence**: Evidence of a set of circumstances from which the existence of a fact in issue can be inferred (indirect evidence).
*   **Hearsay Evidence**: Second-hand evidence; statements made by a person who is not called as a witness, reported by a witness who heard them. Generally inadmissible unless exceptions apply.

---

## 3. Standard of Proof — Proved, Disproved, and Not Proved

The court evaluates evidence based on the standard of a "prudent man":

*   **Proved — Sec. 2(j)**: A fact is said to be proved when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes it to exist, or considers its existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it exists.
*   **Disproved — Sec. 2(c)**: A fact is said to be disproved when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes that it does not exist, or considers its non-existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it does not exist.
*   **Not Proved — Sec. 2(i)**: A fact is said to be not proved when it is neither proved nor disproved.

---

## 4. Appreciation of Evidence & Presumptions

Presumptions are inferences drawn by the court as to the existence of certain facts without direct proof.

*   **May Presume — Sec. 2(h)**: Whenever it is provided by BSS that the Court may presume a fact, it may either regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved, or may call for proof of it (discretionary presumption of fact).
*   **Shall Presume — Sec. 2(l)**: Whenever it is directed by BSS that the Court shall presume a fact, it shall regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved (mandatory presumption of law).
*   **Conclusive Proof — Sec. 2(b)**: When one fact is declared by BSS to be conclusive proof of another, the Court shall, on proof of the one fact, regard the other as proved, and shall not allow evidence to be given for the purpose of disproving it.

## 5. Landmark Judgments

1.  **State of UP v. Deoman Upadhyaya (AIR 1960 SC 1125)**
    - *Ratio*: Constitutionality of section provisions relating to confessions and admissions. Distinguishes between statements made inside and outside custody.
2.  **Hanumant v. State of Madhya Pradesh (AIR 1952 SC 343)**
    - *Ratio*: Laid down the five golden principles ("Panchsheel") of circumstantial evidence. The chain of circumstances must be so complete as to leave no reasonable ground for a conclusion consistent with the innocence of the accused.
3.  **Bodgeshwar v. State of Maharashtra (1996)**
    - *Ratio*: Dealt with the appreciation of circumstantial evidence and standard of proof in criminal trials.

## 6. Model Answers (5 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 5 Marks Answer: Distinguish between "May Presume" and "Shall Presume"
**Answer**:
1.  **May Presume (Sec. 2(h) BSS)**: It represents a discretionary presumption of fact. The court has the option to presume a fact or call for independent proof. The presumption is rebuttable.
    - *Example*: Under BSS, the court may presume that a person in possession of stolen goods soon after the theft is either the thief or has received the goods knowing them to be stolen.
2.  **Shall Presume (Sec. 2(l) BSS)**: It represents a mandatory presumption of law. The court has no discretion and *must* presume the fact as proved until it is disproved.
    - *Example*: Under BSS, the court shall presume the genuineness of certified copies.
3.  **Comparison Table**:
    | Feature | May Presume | Shall Presume |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Type | Presumption of Fact | Presumption of Law |
    | Discretion | Court has discretion to presume or call for proof | Court has no discretion; must presume |
    | Rebuttal | Rebuttable | Rebuttable, but must be presumed until disproved |

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Explain the concept of "Circumstantial Evidence" and the principles governing its appreciation.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Circumstantial evidence is evidence of circumstances from which the existence of the fact in issue can be logically inferred. It is indirect evidence, contrasted with direct evidence (where a witness saw the crime happen).
2.  **Legal Admissibility**: In English and Indian law, circumstantial evidence is fully admissible and can form the sole basis of conviction if it meets the strict tests of reliability.
3.  **The Panchsheel Principles (Hanumant v. State of MP)**:
    - The circumstances from which the conclusion of guilt is to be drawn must be fully established.
    - The facts so established must be consistent only with the hypothesis of the guilt of the accused.
    - The circumstances must be of a conclusive nature and tendency.
    - They must exclude every possible hypothesis except the one to be proved.
    - There must be a chain of evidence so complete as not to leave any reasonable ground for the conclusion consistent with the innocence of the accused and show that in all human probability the act must have been done by the accused.
4.  **Landmark Cases**:
    - *Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra (1984)*: Reaffirmed the Hanumant rules and held that if two views are possible, the view favorable to the accused must be taken.
5.  **Conclusion**: Circumstantial evidence is not inferior to direct evidence; rather, as the maxim goes, "witnesses may lie, but circumstances do not."
