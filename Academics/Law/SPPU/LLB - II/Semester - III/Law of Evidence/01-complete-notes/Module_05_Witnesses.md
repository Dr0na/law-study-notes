# Module 5: Witnesses (Competency, Compellability & Privileges)

## Introduction
Witnesses play a critical role in the judicial system; they are described by Jeremy Bentham as the "eyes and ears of justice." Module 5 covers the rules governing who can testify (competency), who can be forced to testify (compellability), the protection of confidential information (privileged communications), and the status of accomplices.

---

## 1. Competency and Compellability of Witnesses (BSS Sections 124 & 125)

*   **Competency — Sec. 124 BSS (formerly Sec. 118 IEA)**: All persons shall be competent to testify unless the Court considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions put to them, or from giving rational answers to those questions, by tender years, extreme old age, disease, whether of body or mind, or any other cause of the same kind.
    - *Child Witness*: A child is a competent witness if they can understand the questions and give rational answers. The court usually conducts a *voir dire* test (preliminary examination) to test the child's understanding.
    - *Lunatic Witness*: A lunatic is not incompetent to testify, unless he is prevented by his lunacy from understanding the questions and giving rational answers.
*   **Dumb/Deaf Witness — Sec. 125 BSS (formerly Sec. 119 IEA)**: A witness who is unable to speak may give his evidence in any other manner in which he can make it intelligible, as by writing or by signs. Such writing must be written and the signs made in open Court. Evidence so given shall be deemed to be oral evidence.

---

## 2. Privileged Communications (BSS Sections 128 to 134)

Privileged communications are confidential disclosures which the law protects from being forced into evidence, to preserve relationships and state interests:

- **Matrimonial Privilege — Sec. 128 BSS**: No person who is or has been married shall be compelled to disclose any communication made to him during marriage by any person to whom he is or has been married, unless the person who made it, or his representative in interest, consents, except in suits between married partners or in criminal proceedings for crimes committed by one against the other.
- **State Privilege — Sec. 129 BSS**: No one shall be permitted to give any evidence derived from unpublished official records relating to any affairs of State, except with the permission of the officer at the head of the department concerned, who shall give or withhold such permission as he thinks fit.
- **Official Communications — Sec. 130 BSS**: No public officer shall be compelled to disclose communications made to him in official confidence, when he considers that the public interests would suffer by the disclosure.
- **Information as to Commission of Offences — Sec. 131 BSS**: No Magistrate or police officer shall be compelled to say whence he got any information as to the commission of any offence, and no revenue officer shall be compelled to say whence he got any information as to the commission of any offence against the public revenue.
- **Professional Privilege — Sec. 132-134 BSS**: No legal practitioner (advocate, attorney) shall be permitted, unless with his client's express consent, to disclose any communication made to him in the course and for the purpose of his employment.
    - *Exception*: Any communication made in furtherance of any illegal purpose or any fact showing that a crime or fraud has been committed since the employment.

---

## 3. Accomplice Evidence — Sec. 138 BSS (formerly Sec. 133 IEA)

An **Accomplice** is an associate in crime, a partner in guilt. 
*   **Evidentiary Position**: Section 138 BSS states that an accomplice shall be a competent witness against an accused person; and a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds upon the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice.
*   **The Conflict with Presumptions (BSS Sec. 117 Illustration (b))**: The court may presume that an accomplice is unworthy of credit, unless he is corroborated in material particulars.
*   **Reconciliation (Dagdu v. State of Maharashtra)**: The Supreme Court reconciled the two provisions by stating that while a conviction based on uncorroborated accomplice evidence is technically *legal* under Section 133 IEA (now Sec. 138 BSS), prudence and long judicial practice dictate that the court should not convict unless the accomplice's testimony is corroborated by independent evidence in material particulars linking the accused to the crime.

## 4. Landmark Judgments

1.  **Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010 7 SCC 263)**
    - *Ratio*: Addressed the self-incrimination clause and held that compulsory administration of narco-analysis, polygraph, or brain mapping violates Article 20(3) of the Constitution and Section 132 BSS protections.
2.  **State of Bihar v. Kalu Miah (1975)**
    - *Ratio*: Discussed the admissibility and reliability of deaf and dumb witness testimony given through signs.

## 5. Model Answers (10 Marks & 15 Marks)

### A. Model 10 Marks Answer: Discuss the competency of a Child Witness. How does the court appreciate their testimony?
**Answer**:
1.  **Competency**: Section 124 BSS states that competency is determined by the capacity of the witness to understand questions and give rational answers, not by age. A child is a competent witness.
2.  **Voir Dire Test**: Before recording a child's evidence, the judge conducts a preliminary examination (voir dire) by asking general questions (e.g., about their school, family) to test if the child has sufficient intelligence to understand and give rational answers.
3.  **Appreciation of Child Evidence**:
    - Child evidence is viewed as "frail" because children are highly suggestible, easily coached, and have active imaginations.
    - The court must look for independent corroboration.
    - *Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan (1952)*: The Supreme Court held that while corroboration is highly desirable as a rule of prudence, it is not an absolute rule of law. If the child’s evidence is highly natural and convincing, a conviction can stand.
4.  **Conclusion**: A child witness is legally competent, but the court must exercise extreme caution to rule out coaching or fantasy before relying on their testimony.

### B. Model 15 Marks Answer: Critically analyze the law relating to Privileged Communications under BSS, 2023.
**Answer**:
1.  **Introduction**: Privileged communications represent a balance between the search for truth and the protection of confidential relationships and public interest. Under BSS, certain communications are protected from disclosure.
2.  **Matrimonial Privilege (Sec. 128 BSS)**:
    - Protects the sanctity of marriage. Communications between spouses during marriage are protected even after divorce.
    - *Exceptions*: Suits between the spouses, or criminal trials where one spouse is prosecuted for a crime against the other.
3.  **Legal Professional Privilege (Sec. 132 BSS)**:
    - Protects client-attorney trust. An advocate cannot disclose communications made by the client during professional employment.
    - *Exceptions*: Furtherance of illegal purpose, or discovery of fraud/crime committed after the employment began.
4.  **State Privilege (Sec. 129 BSS)**:
    - Protects the state's security. No one can give evidence from unpublished records of State affairs without permission.
    - *State of Punjab v. Sodhi Sukhdev Singh (1961)*: The court held that while the head of the department decides on the privilege, the court has the power to inspect the document in chambers to verify if it relates to affairs of state.
5.  **Conclusion**: Privileged communication is an essential shield in a democratic legal system, but it must not be allowed to act as a cloak for criminal activities.
