# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Q&A — Law of Evidence (LCC 0702)

Reasoned predictions from the syllabus and 2025 papers; not guaranteed. Current 70-mark format: Part A 2×15, Part B 3×10, Part C 2×5. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA) applies to proceedings within section 1, subject to its savings. Do not copy Indian Evidence Act section numbers without checking the BSA.

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain primary and secondary evidence and how documents are proved under the BSA.

Section 56 states that contents of documents may be proved by primary or secondary evidence. Section 57 defines primary evidence and includes statutory explanations for documents executed in counterparts, electronic/digital records and certain records stored or transmitted electronically. Section 58 lists forms of secondary evidence, including certified copies, copies made by mechanical processes, counterparts and oral accounts of contents by a person who has seen the document, subject to its conditions.

The general rule under section 59 is that documents must be proved by primary evidence, except in cases provided by the Act. Section 60 permits secondary evidence in specified circumstances: where the original is with an opponent or a person beyond the court’s reach after notice to produce; where the original is lost/destroyed or cannot be produced for a reason not arising from default or neglect; where the original is not easily movable; where the original is a public document; where a certified copy is permitted; or where numerous accounts/documents are involved and the result can be proved by a skilled examiner. The kind of secondary evidence permitted depends on the applicable clause. Notice to produce is governed by section 64, subject to exceptions.

Public documents and certified copies are addressed in sections 74–76. Electronic and digital records are separately dealt with in sections 61–63; admissibility of an electronic record depends on statutory conditions and any prescribed certificate. *Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer*, (2014) 10 SCC 473, and *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*, (2020) 7 SCC 1, interpret the former Evidence Act’s electronic-record certificate regime. Apply them with the BSA text and current law, noting that the BSA re-enacts the framework with its own wording and section numbering.

**Conclusion.** The original is the best evidence and ordinarily must be produced. Secondary evidence is admissible only after the statutory foundation is laid; electronic records require attention to sections 61–63 and the certificate requirements.

### Q2. Explain privileged communications under the BSA.

Evidence law balances relevance with confidentiality and public interest. The BSA recognises privileges that can exclude relevant evidence in defined circumstances. The provisions include communications during marriage (section 128); affairs of State (section 129); official communications (section 130); information as to commission of offences (section 131); professional communications (section 132); privilege not waived by volunteering evidence (section 133); confidential communications with legal advisers (section 134); title deeds and documents withheld from production (section 135); and production of documents another person could refuse to produce (section 136). Read each provision’s conditions and exceptions.

Under section 132, an advocate generally cannot disclose communications made by or on behalf of a client in the course and for the purpose of professional employment, nor the contents or condition of documents learned in that role, without the client’s express consent. The privilege does not protect communications made in furtherance of an illegal purpose or facts observed by the advocate showing a crime or fraud committed after the engagement began. The obligation continues after the engagement ends. Waiver and the client’s consent are governed by the statute. Privilege belongs to the client and supports full and candid legal advice.

State privilege protects specified unpublished official records where public interest would suffer from disclosure; official communications receive protection under their statutory terms. Marital communications are generally protected from disclosure by a spouse, subject to the exceptions stated in the section. These are not blanket privileges over every document or communication involving a public official, spouse or lawyer.

**Conclusion.** Privilege is a statutory exclusion, not a general claim of secrecy. Identify the exact provision, holder, protected material, waiver and exception before concluding that evidence is inadmissible.

### Q3. “All confessions are admissions, but an admission may not be a confession.” Discuss under the BSA.

An admission is a statement suggesting an inference as to a fact in issue or relevant fact, made by a person and in circumstances covered by the BSA. A confession is a narrower kind of admission in a criminal case: it substantially acknowledges guilt or all essential ingredients of the offence. Thus every confession is an admission, but many admissions—such as admitting presence, ownership or a relationship—do not amount to confession.

Sections 15–25 govern admissions; sections 22–24 specifically address confessions. Section 22 excludes a confession caused by inducement, threat, coercion or promise from a person in authority, where it gives reasonable grounds to suppose that the accused would gain an advantage or avoid an evil in relation to the proceeding. Section 23 generally excludes confession to a police officer and confession in police custody unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate, while preserving the limited discovery rule for so much information as distinctly relates to the fact discovered. Section 24 concerns confession by a co-accused jointly tried for the same offence; it is not substantive evidence against another accused by itself and requires cautious use with other evidence.

A judicial confession must be voluntary and true. A retracted confession may support conviction only if the court is satisfied it is voluntary and reliable; prudence ordinarily calls for corroboration. An extra-judicial confession is admissible but must be scrutinised for voluntariness and reliability. A confession does not become inadmissible merely because it is made outside court, but statutory exclusions control police and custodial confessions.

**Conclusion.** Confession is a subset of admission with special criminal-law safeguards. The court must distinguish an incriminating admission from a complete acknowledgment of guilt and apply the statutory exclusions strictly.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. Explain dying declarations under the BSA.

Section 26 recognises statements by a person who is dead or cannot be found, including statements about the cause of death or circumstances of the transaction resulting in death, when the cause of that person’s death is in question. The maker need not have expected death. A declaration may be oral, written or communicated by signs, if reliably proved. A declaration recorded by a Magistrate is desirable but not an absolute condition. The court must assess fitness, opportunity to observe, consistency, voluntariness, prompting and whether the statement was accurately recorded. A reliable dying declaration may by itself sustain conviction; corroboration is not an inflexible rule. *Khushal Rao v State of Bombay*, AIR 1958 SC 22, and *Laxman v State of Maharashtra*, (2002) 6 SCC 710, state the relevant principles under the predecessor law, applicable subject to the BSA text.

### Q5. Explain the burden and standard of proof.

Sections 104–120 BSA distribute the burden of proof. A person asking the court to give judgment based on asserted facts generally must prove those facts; the burden lies on the party who would fail if no evidence were given. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, while civil claims are ordinarily proved on a balance of probabilities. The burden may shift where a party must prove a particular fact, a fact is especially within knowledge (section 109), or a statutory presumption applies. The accused may rely on prosecution evidence and need not prove innocence. A reverse burden does not remove the prosecution’s duty to establish foundational facts. *Kali Ram v State of Himachal Pradesh*, (1973) 2 SCC 808, emphasises the presumption of innocence and benefit of reasonable doubt.

### Q6. Explain examination and cross-examination of witnesses.

Sections 142–168 BSA govern examination of witnesses. Examination-in-chief is by the party calling the witness; cross-examination is by the adverse party; re-examination clarifies matters arising in cross-examination. Cross-examination may test veracity, identity, position and credit, subject to statutory limits protecting witnesses from improper or indecent questions. Leading questions are ordinarily allowed in cross-examination but restricted in chief/re-examination except with permission or on introductory matters. A party may, with court permission, cross-examine its own witness under section 157 where the witness is hostile or adverse. The judge controls admissibility and may ask questions under section 168 to discover or prove relevant facts, while preserving fairness.

## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Electronic records

BSA sections 61–63 recognise electronic and digital records and prescribe admissibility requirements. Section 63 sets conditions for computer output and requires the certificate specified by the provision. Preserve the source, integrity and chain of custody; do not rely on a screenshot alone without establishing admissibility. Read the BSA wording rather than importing the Evidence Act’s section 65B citation.

### Professional privilege

BSA section 132 protects specified client-lawyer communications and documents made in professional employment, subject to client consent and statutory exceptions such as illegal purpose or post-engagement crime/fraud. Privilege belongs to the client and continues after the engagement.

### May presume, shall presume and conclusive proof

Under section 2 of the BSA, “may presume” leaves the court discretion to regard a fact as proved or call for proof; “shall presume” requires the court to treat it as proved unless disproved; “conclusive proof” bars evidence to disprove the fact once the foundational fact is established, subject to the statute.

### Hostile witness

A witness is not automatically erased from the record merely because the party calling them treats them as hostile. The court may permit cross-examination by that party under section 157 BSA. Credible parts of the testimony may still be relied upon with caution.
