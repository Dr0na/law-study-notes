# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Questions and Answers — Law of Evidence

These are **practice predictions**, not an official question paper or answer key. They are based on the recent SPPU papers and recurring syllabus topics. The latest paper available when this guide was prepared follows this choice pattern:

- **Part A:** Answer any **2 of 4** long questions (15 marks each).
- **Part B:** Answer any **3 of 5** questions (10 marks each).
- **Part C:** Write short notes on any **2 of 4** topics (5 marks each).

SPPU may change the pattern. Check the instructions printed on your examination paper.

## How to write answers in SPPU format

Use a clear answer structure. Do not repeat the same statutory citation in every paragraph. The answers below name the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** and explain the relevant rule without section numbers, as requested.

- **15 marks:** Give an introduction, explain the main rules under numbered headings, discuss exceptions and safeguards, use relevant cases with their principles, and end with a conclusion. Develop each part of the question.
- **10 marks:** Give a short introduction, explain the main rules in numbered points, add an example or relevant case, and conclude briefly.
- **5 marks:** Define the topic, state the core rule, explain a few key points, and end with one sentence.

Write in plain language, but keep legal terms such as *primary evidence*, *privilege*, *onus of proof* and *res gestae*. Explain each term the first time you use it.

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. “Documents are proved either by primary evidence or by secondary evidence.” Explain.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

#### Introduction

Documentary evidence is used to prove the contents of a document. The BSA prefers the original document, but it allows certain copies or other forms of proof when the original cannot reasonably be produced.

#### 1. Primary evidence

**Primary evidence** is the document itself produced for the court to inspect. For example, if a party relies on a signed agreement, the original signed agreement is ordinarily primary evidence.

The BSA also explains how primary evidence works for documents made in parts or counterparts, and for electronic records. In some situations, separate electronic files or properly kept electronic records can count as primary evidence. The court may examine whether the record is disputed and whether it came from proper custody.

#### 2. Secondary evidence

**Secondary evidence** is an authorised substitute for the original. It can include a certified copy, a copy made by a process that ensures accuracy, a copy compared with the original, an oral account by a person who saw the document, an admission about its contents, or the report of a skilled person who examined numerous records.

A photocopy is not automatically accepted. The party relying on it must show that it is a kind of secondary evidence the law permits and that the circumstances allow its use.

#### 3. When may secondary evidence be used?

Secondary evidence may be used when, for example, the original is held by the opposing party and is not produced after proper notice; it has been lost or destroyed without the relying party’s neglect; it cannot conveniently be moved; it is a public document; a certified copy is permitted; or the case concerns many records that cannot conveniently be examined one by one.

The reason the original is missing must be proved. The type of copy allowed depends on the reason. For example, when the original is a public document, the law generally requires a certified copy.

#### 4. Proving a document

The party must prove more than the paper’s existence. Where disputed, the party may also need to prove who made or signed it, that it is genuine, and that it contains what the party claims. Documents that the law requires to be attested may need an attesting witness, subject to legal exceptions.

In *H. Siddiqui v A. Ramalingam*, (2011) 4 SCC 240, the Supreme Court stressed that a party must lay the proper foundation before relying on secondary evidence. A copy cannot be used merely because it is convenient.

#### Conclusion

Primary evidence is the normal method of proving a document. Secondary evidence is allowed only after the party proves a recognised reason for not producing the original and meets the conditions for the particular substitute.

### Q2. Explain privileged communications under the BSA.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

#### Introduction

Evidence may be relevant but still protected from disclosure. **Privilege** is a legal protection for certain communications or documents. It supports privacy, public interest and the proper administration of justice. A claim of privilege must fit a rule recognised by law; confidentiality by itself is not enough.

#### 1. Communications during marriage

A person generally cannot be forced or permitted to disclose a communication made by their spouse during marriage without the maker’s consent. The law recognises exceptions, including proceedings between spouses and prosecutions for an offence by one spouse against the other.

#### 2. State and official communications

Unpublished official records about affairs of State may be protected unless the required government permission is given. A public officer may also be protected from disclosing an official communication made in confidence when disclosure would harm the public interest. Police officers and Magistrates generally cannot be forced to reveal the source of information about an offence, subject to the law.

These are limited protections. They do not make every government document secret or every official communication privileged.

#### 3. Professional communications

An advocate must generally keep confidential communications made by or for a client for the purpose of professional legal work. This includes relevant documents and legal advice. The duty continues after the professional work ends.

The protection does not cover a communication made to help an illegal purpose. It also does not cover an advocate’s observation of a crime or fraud committed after the advocate’s work began. The client may give express consent to disclosure. Merely giving evidence does not automatically waive the client’s privilege.

Confidential communications with a legal adviser also receive protection, subject to the applicable rules. The law further protects some title deeds and documents that another person could legally refuse to produce.

#### 4. Applying privilege

Before deciding that material is protected, identify:

1. the kind of communication or document;
2. who holds the protection;
3. whether consent or waiver applies; and
4. whether a statutory exception removes the protection.

In *State of Punjab v Sodhi Sukhdev Singh*, AIR 1961 SC 493, the Supreme Court considered the protection for unpublished State records and the public-interest basis for the claim.

#### Conclusion

Privilege is a defined legal protection, not a general right to keep relevant evidence secret. The court must balance the specific protection with the need to decide the case fairly.

### Q3. “All confessions are admissions, but an admission may not be a confession.” Discuss.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

#### Introduction

An **admission** is a statement that suggests an inference about a fact in issue or another relevant fact. A **confession** is a narrower kind of statement made by an accused person that substantially admits guilt. Therefore, every confession is an admission, but many admissions are not confessions.

#### 1. Admission

An admission may be oral, written or electronic. It can be made by a party, an authorised agent or another person in a position covered by the BSA. For example, “I was at the shop that evening” may be an admission about presence, but it does not by itself admit a crime.

Admissions are usually relevant against the person who made them. In limited situations, an admission may be used by or on behalf of that person. An admission is not automatically conclusive proof; the court considers its meaning, context and other evidence.

#### 2. Confession

The BSA does not separately define confession. In *Pakala Narayana Swami v Emperor*, AIR 1939 PC 47, the Privy Council explained that a confession must admit the offence or substantially all the facts that make up the offence. A statement that is merely damaging or suspicious is not necessarily a confession.

#### 3. Rules that protect an accused

1. **Involuntary confession:** A confession caused by an inducement, threat, coercion or promise from a person in authority may be excluded when it gives the accused a reason to expect an advantage or avoid harm in the case.
2. **Confession to police:** A confession made to a police officer generally cannot be used to prove guilt.
3. **Confession in police custody:** It is generally excluded unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate.
4. **Discovery of a fact:** When information from an accused in custody leads to discovery, only the part that distinctly relates to the fact discovered may be proved. In *Pulukuri Kottaya v King-Emperor*, AIR 1947 PC 67, the Privy Council explained this limited rule.
5. **Co-accused’s confession:** A confession by one accused may be considered in a joint trial under the conditions set by law. It is weak evidence and should not by itself be treated as proof of another accused’s guilt. *Kashmira Singh v State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1952 SC 159 explains the need for caution.

A confession later withdrawn by its maker is called a **retracted confession**. The court checks whether it was voluntary and reliable. Corroboration is a rule of prudence, not an automatic statutory condition in every case.

#### Conclusion

An admission may concern only one fact, while a confession substantially admits guilt. Confessions therefore receive special safeguards, especially when made to police or in custody.

### Q4. Discuss the admissibility and relevancy of electronic evidence under the BSA.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

#### Introduction

Electronic evidence includes emails, messages, photographs, CCTV footage, call records, audio, video, server logs and other digital records. The BSA recognises electronic and digital records as evidence, but the party must still establish that the record is relevant and properly proved.

#### 1. Legal recognition

An electronic record cannot be rejected only because it is electronic. It can have the same legal effect as another document, subject to the BSA’s rules for proving its contents.

#### 2. Computer output and its conditions

When information is produced as a **computer output**—for example, a printout, copied file or exported CCTV clip—the party must show that the statutory conditions are met. In simple terms, the relevant device or system was regularly used for the activity; information of that kind was entered in the ordinary course; the system was operating properly, or any fault did not affect accuracy; and the output reproduces the information entered or stored.

Where several computers or devices formed part of the same activity, the law can treat them together as one system for this purpose.

#### 3. Certificate

When a computer output is submitted for admission, the BSA requires a certificate to accompany the record. The certificate identifies the record, explains how it was produced, gives relevant device details, and addresses the required system conditions. It is to be signed by the person in charge of the device or relevant activity and by an expert, in the form prescribed by the BSA.

This requirement concerns the statutory method of proving the electronic output. It does not mean that every screenshot, email or device is automatically genuine or accurate. The court may still consider who created the record, whether it was altered, how it was preserved and whether it relates to the issue in the case.

#### 4. Case law and safeguards

*Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer*, (2014) 10 SCC 473, and *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*, (2020) 7 SCC 1 explain the certificate rule under the former Evidence Act. They remain useful when read carefully with the BSA’s own wording and certificate form.

Preserve the original device or file where possible, record how it was collected, avoid editing it, and keep the certificate and supporting details. These steps help the court assess authenticity and reliability.

#### Conclusion

Electronic records are recognised as documentary evidence. To rely on a computer output, meet the BSA’s proof and certificate requirements and show that the record is authentic, intact and relevant.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q5. Distinguish between burden of proof and onus of proof. Explain the difference in civil and criminal cases.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

#### Introduction

The **burden of proof** tells the court which party must prove a claim or defence. The **onus of proof** is the practical need to produce evidence on a particular point as the case develops.

#### 1. Burden of proof

The party who asks the court for a decision based on facts they assert generally has to prove those facts. The burden normally remains on the party assigned by law. In a criminal case, the prosecution must prove the elements of the offence beyond reasonable doubt. The accused does not have to prove innocence.

#### 2. Onus of proof

The onus is the immediate need to bring evidence to answer the other side’s case. It can shift during the trial. For example, if one side produces credible evidence of a fact, the other side may need to answer it. That shift does not necessarily change the overall legal burden.

#### 3. Civil and criminal standards

In a civil case, the party making the claim usually succeeds if the claim is **more likely true than not**. This is called the **balance of probabilities**. In a criminal case, guilt must be proved **beyond reasonable doubt**, because a person’s liberty is at stake.

The accused may have to prove a statutory exception or a fact placed on them by law, usually on a balance of probabilities. But the prosecution must still prove the basic offence. A fact being especially within a person’s knowledge does not allow the prosecution to avoid proving its case.

In *Woolmington v Director of Public Prosecutions*, [1935] AC 462, the House of Lords stated the general rule that the prosecution must prove guilt. The Supreme Court in *Kali Ram v State of Himachal Pradesh*, (1973) 2 SCC 808 explained that a reasonable doubt benefits the accused.

#### Conclusion

The legal burden is the continuing duty to prove a case; the evidential onus may move as evidence is led. The standard of proof is higher in criminal cases than in civil cases.

### Q6. What is the difference between relevancy and admissibility of facts?

**Model answer — 10 marks**

#### Introduction

**Relevancy** is the legal connection between a fact and an issue in the case. **Admissibility** is whether the court is allowed to receive that evidence under the law.

#### 1. Relevancy

A fact is relevant when the law recognises that it helps prove or disprove a fact in issue or another relevant fact. For example, evidence that the accused had a reason to harm the victim may be relevant to motive.

#### 2. Admissibility

Even relevant evidence must satisfy the rules for being received by the court. A confession to a police officer may appear relevant, but the BSA generally excludes it. A copy of a document may relate to the dispute, but the party must first show why secondary evidence is allowed.

#### 3. Main difference

1. Relevancy asks: **Does this fact have a legally recognised connection to the issue?**
2. Admissibility asks: **May the court receive and consider this evidence under the rules?**
3. Relevance is necessary, but it does not always make evidence admissible.
4. Evidence may be excluded because of privilege, a confession rule, hearsay limits, or failure to meet proof requirements.

#### Conclusion

The court first considers whether evidence is relevant and then whether it is admissible. A party should explain both why the fact matters and why the evidence can legally be used.

### Q7. Explain the doctrine of estoppel under the law of evidence.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

#### Introduction

**Estoppel** prevents a person from denying a position that they led another person to believe and act on. The BSA recognises estoppel by conduct and special rules for certain relationships. Promissory estoppel is a related equitable doctrine developed by courts.

#### 1. Estoppel by conduct

The main elements are:

1. A person makes a declaration, does an act, or omits to speak when the law and circumstances call for it.
2. They intend another person to rely on it, or knowingly allow that reliance.
3. The other person believes it and acts on that belief.
4. The first person is then prevented from denying that position against the person who relied on it, in proceedings covered by the rule.

**Example:** A knowingly leads B to believe that A owns land and encourages B to buy it. If B acts on that belief, A may be prevented from later denying the position against B.

#### 2. Special forms and limits

The law also prevents certain tenants or licensees from denying the title or authority of the person who let or licensed the property to them at the start. It also has rules for an acceptor of a bill, a bailee and a licensee.

**Promissory estoppel** may prevent a party, including government acting within its lawful authority, from withdrawing a clear promise on which another person relied. In *Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills Co. v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1979) 2 SCC 409, the Supreme Court applied the doctrine in an appropriate case involving a government promise.

There is no estoppel against a statute. Estoppel cannot require an unlawful act or override legislation or the Constitution.

#### Conclusion

Estoppel protects reliance and prevents unfair denial. It does not create a right in every case or allow anyone to act contrary to law.

### Q8. What is a public document? How is it proved?

**Model answer — 10 marks**

#### Introduction

A **public document** is an official record of an act of government, a court, a public officer or an official body. Public records of private documents are also treated as public documents. Other documents are generally private documents.

#### 1. Examples

Examples include court records, government notifications, official registers and records kept by a public officer in the course of official duty. A private contract does not become a public document just because it is shown to a government office; the official record of that contract may be public.

#### 2. Certified copies

A person entitled to inspect a public document may usually obtain a **certified copy** from its public custodian. The responsible officer certifies that it is a true copy and signs it with the required official details. Where authorised, the copy is sealed.

#### 3. Proof in court

A certified copy may be produced to prove the contents of the public document. The court may presume that a properly certified copy is genuine, subject to the law and the circumstances. This is a practical exception to producing the original public record in court.

The party should identify the document, show that it belongs to a public category, and produce a copy certified by the proper custodian. If authenticity or accuracy is disputed, the court may require further proof.

#### Conclusion

Public documents are official records. Their contents are commonly proved through certified copies, but the copy must be properly certified and relate to a document the law treats as public.

### Q9. What is a presumption? Explain its main types with examples.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

#### Introduction

A **presumption** is a conclusion the court draws after a basic fact is proved. It can save time, reflect ordinary experience, or apply a rule made by law. The party relying on a presumption must first establish the facts that trigger it.

#### 1. “May presume”

The court has a choice: it may treat the fact as proved unless disproved, or ask for further proof. This is generally a discretionary presumption. For example, the court may draw an inference from ordinary human conduct or the usual course of business.

#### 2. “Shall presume”

The court must treat the fact as proved unless the other side disproves it. This is a mandatory but rebuttable presumption. For example, the law directs a presumption in a dowry-death case when the required facts about cruelty or harassment soon before death are proved.

#### 3. “Conclusive proof”

Once the starting fact is proved, the law does not allow evidence to disprove the resulting fact, subject to the exact terms of the statute. The rule concerning legitimacy of a child born during a valid marriage is an example, with the statutory exception relating to absence of access.

#### Conclusion

“May presume” gives the court discretion; “shall presume” requires a rebuttable assumption; “conclusive proof” bars rebuttal once its legal conditions are met. Always prove the foundational facts first.

## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Q10(a). Order of examination of a witness

**Model answer — 5 marks**

The usual order is:

1. **Examination-in-chief:** questions asked by the party who called the witness.
2. **Cross-examination:** questions asked by the opposing party to test the witness’s account and reliability.
3. **Re-examination:** further questions by the party who called the witness, to explain matters raised in cross-examination.

Re-examination should normally clarify matters raised in cross-examination. If the court allows a new matter to be introduced, the opposing party may ask further questions on that matter. This order helps the court hear the witness’s account, test it, and clear up issues that remain.

### Q10(b). Test Identification Parade

**Model answer — 5 marks**

A **Test Identification Parade (TIP)** is an investigative procedure in which a witness is asked to identify a person among others with similar appearance. It tests the witness’s memory and helps investigators check whether the witness can recognise the person they say they saw.

A TIP is generally not substantive evidence by itself. The witness’s identification in court is the substantive evidence; the earlier parade may support the court’s assessment of that identification. In *Budhsen v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1970) 2 SCC 128, the Supreme Court explained this distinction. A TIP should be held fairly and without suggestive procedures. A failure to hold one is assessed with the facts of the case; it does not automatically decide guilt or innocence.

### Q10(c). Relevance of motive and preparation

**Model answer — 5 marks**

**Motive** is the reason a person may have acted. **Preparation** is a step taken in advance to make an act possible. Both may be relevant when they help the court understand or test the alleged conduct.

For example, a prior threat may help show a possible motive, while buying a tool shortly before an offence may show preparation. Neither fact proves guilt by itself. The court considers them with the other evidence. Motive can be especially helpful in a case based mainly on circumstances, but the absence of a known motive does not automatically defeat a case supported by reliable evidence.

### Q10(d). Doctrine of *Res Gestae*

**Model answer — 5 marks**

*Res gestae* means “things done.” It refers to facts so closely connected with the event in issue that they form part of the same transaction. A spontaneous statement made during an event may be relevant because the speaker had little time to invent or alter the account.

The court considers the connection in time, place and continuity. A later statement, made after an opportunity to think or fabricate, is not automatically part of the transaction. In *Sukhar v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1999) 9 SCC 507, the Supreme Court stressed that the statement must be spontaneous and closely connected with the occurrence. The doctrine is a limited exception to the hearsay rule.
