# Module 5: Witnesses, Competency and Privilege

## Introduction

This module explains who may testify, which communications are protected, and the rules on accomplice evidence and the number of witnesses.

---

## 1. Competency to testify — section 124

A person is generally a **competent witness** if they can understand the questions and give sensible answers. A person is not automatically disqualified because they are young, old, ill or have a disability. The judge checks the person’s actual ability to understand and answer. A child may testify if the child can understand the questions and answer them sensibly.

Competency and compellability are different. Competency means the person can give evidence. Compellability means the law can require them to answer. A privilege may protect some information from disclosure.

### A. *Voir Dire* for a Child Witness

*Voir dire* is the court’s preliminary check of whether a child understands the questions and can give rational answers. The court assesses capacity, not age alone. A competent child may testify; corroboration is a rule of prudence in appropriate cases, not an automatic legal requirement.

---

## 2. A witness who cannot speak — section 125

A person who cannot speak may give evidence by writing, signs or another method the court can understand. The evidence is treated as oral evidence. The court arranges an interpreter or special educator where needed and checks that the method works reliably.

---

## 3. Spouses and judges — sections 126–127

Spouses can testify in the cases covered by the Act, but section 128 separately protects certain communications made during marriage. Judges and Magistrates are generally protected from being forced to answer questions about their judicial work or information learned in court, except where the law and a proper court order allow it.

---

## 4. Privileged communications — sections 128–136

### A. Meaning of Privilege

**Privilege** is a legal protection that means certain information cannot be forced out or used in court, even if it might be relevant. The reason may be privacy, public interest or the proper working of justice.

*   **Marital communications, section 128**

    A spouse generally cannot be forced or allowed to reveal a communication made by the other spouse during marriage. The Act has exceptions, including certain cases between spouses or where one is prosecuted for an offence against the other.

*   **Affairs of State, section 129**

    Some unpublished government records cannot be disclosed without the permission required by the Act.

*   **Official communications, section 130**

    A public officer may be protected from disclosing a confidential official communication if public interest would suffer.

*   **Information about offences, section 131**

    Police officers and Magistrates generally cannot be forced to reveal who gave them information about an offence, subject to the Act.

*   **Professional communications, section 132**

    An advocate must not reveal a client’s communication or information learned while acting professionally without the client’s express permission. This duty continues after the work ends. It does not protect a communication made to help an illegal plan or facts showing a later crime or fraud.

*   **No automatic waiver, section 133**

    A client does not automatically lose professional privilege just by giving some evidence.

*   **Legal-adviser communications, section 134**

    Confidential communication with a legal adviser is protected, subject to the Act’s exceptions.

*   **Documents, sections 135–136**

    The Act protects certain title deeds and documents that another person could legally refuse to produce.

Do not assume that something is privileged just because it is private or confidential. Identify the exact section, who holds the privilege and whether an exception applies.

### Memory Aid: Privilege Map (sections 128–136)

“Marriage, State, Official, Informant, Advocate, Waiver, Adviser, Documents” (M-S-O-I-A-W-A-D). Use it to recall the sequence of protected communications and documents, then check the specific section and its exceptions.

## 5. Answer that may expose a witness to a charge — section 137

A witness is not automatically excused from answering a relevant question just because the answer might show that the witness committed an offence. Section 137 requires the witness to answer, but gives protection about how a compelled answer may later be used, subject to the statutory exception for a false answer. This rule must also be read with the constitutional protection against being forced to testify against oneself.

### A. Privilege Against Self-Incrimination

Article 20(3) of the Constitution protects an **accused person** from being compelled to be a witness against themselves. Section 137 BSA concerns a witness’s answer to a relevant question and the statutory protection on later use. These rules are related but not identical; section 137 does not remove the accused’s constitutional protection.

---

## 6. Accomplices and number of witnesses — sections 138–139

An **accomplice** is someone who took part in the offence. Section 138 allows an accomplice to testify, subject to law. Courts are careful because an accomplice may hope for a benefit or try to blame another person. They usually look for supporting evidence as a matter of caution.

Section 139 says the law does not require a fixed number of witnesses to prove a fact. One trustworthy witness may be enough; several unreliable witnesses do not automatically prove a case.

*   *Vadivelu Thevar v State of Madras*, AIR 1957 SC 614

    A witness may be fully reliable, unreliable, or need supporting evidence; one fully reliable witness can be enough.

*   *Rameshwar v State of Rajasthan*, AIR 1952 SC 54

    Supporting a child witness is a safety practice, not an absolute legal requirement in every case.

### A. Accomplice Corroboration: Rule of Prudence

Under section 138, a conviction is not illegal merely because it rests on uncorroborated accomplice testimony. However, courts ordinarily seek **corroboration in material particulars** as a rule of prudence. Supporting evidence should connect the accused with the offence. Corroboration is a reliability safeguard, not an absolute statutory condition for a valid conviction.

### Memory Aid: “Accomplice 138; Count 139.”

Section 138 deals with an accomplice; section 139 says no particular number of witnesses is required.

---

## 7. How courts assess a witness

The judge considers whether the person could see or hear the event, whether they remember it, whether their account is consistent, whether they may be biased, and how they respond to cross-examination. Small differences may happen naturally. A major contradiction may make the account less reliable. The judge considers the whole testimony.

### Quick Recap

Check if the person can testify, then assess whether the testimony is reliable. Check for privilege before requiring an answer. The number of witnesses is less important than the quality of their evidence.
