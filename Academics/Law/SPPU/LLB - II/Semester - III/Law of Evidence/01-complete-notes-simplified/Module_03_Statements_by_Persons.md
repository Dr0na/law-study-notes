# Module 3: Statements by Persons and Expert Opinion

## Introduction

This module covers statements that may be used when a person cannot testify, the limited uses of earlier judgments, expert opinions and character evidence.

---

## 1. Statements by a person who cannot testify — section 26

The usual rule is that the person who made a statement should come to court, take an oath and answer questions. Section 26 makes certain statements relevant when the maker has died, cannot be found, cannot testify, or cannot be brought to court without unreasonable delay or expense. The party relying on the statement must show that the section’s conditions are met.

### A. Dying Declaration

A **dying declaration** is a statement about the cause of a person’s death or the events that led to it, when that person’s death is in question. The person need not have expected to die. The statement may be spoken, written or communicated in another reliable way.

**Latin maxim:** *Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentire* means “a person about to die is not presumed to lie.” It is the traditional explanation for admitting a dying declaration. The court must still examine whether this particular statement is reliable.

The judge checks whether the person could understand and answer, whether the statement was voluntary, whether it is consistent, whether the person had a chance to observe, and whether it was recorded accurately. A Magistrate recording the statement is a safeguard, but it is not an absolute legal requirement. A reliable dying declaration can by itself support a conviction.

*   *Khushal Rao v State of Bombay*, AIR 1958 SC 22

    A reliable dying declaration can be enough to prove a case.

*   *Laxman v State of Maharashtra*, (2002) 6 SCC 710

    A doctor’s certificate of fitness is a useful safeguard, but not an absolute requirement if the judge is otherwise satisfied the person could make the statement.

### Memory Aid: Reliability Checks: “M-V-C-O-R” (Maker, Voluntary, Consistent, Observed, Recorded)

Check the maker’s ability, voluntariness, consistency, opportunity to observe, and accuracy of recording. These are practical checks, not a closed statutory test.

Section 26 also covers certain statements made in the ordinary course of business, against the maker’s interest, about public rights or customs, about family relationships, and in documents connected to a relevant transaction. Match the statement to the correct category.

## 2. Statements in special circumstances — sections 27–33

Section 27 also allows evidence given by a witness in an earlier court proceeding to be used later in the situations listed in the section—for example, if the witness has died or cannot be found. A key safeguard is that the opposing party must have had the right and a real chance to cross-examine the witness in the earlier proceeding, and the issues must be substantially the same.

Other statements are relevant because of how or where they were recorded. The BSA includes specified entries in books of account, public and official records made as part of a person’s duty, maps and plans, official statements about public facts, statements about foreign law in authorised sources, and certain documents about rights or customs.

An entry in a business account can be relevant, but normally cannot by itself make someone liable to pay. A record is not automatically reliable just because it is written down; the statutory conditions and the circumstances still matter.

Section 33 requires enough of a statement, conversation or document to be shown to make its meaning clear. A party should not quote only one sentence if the surrounding words change what it means.

## 3. When earlier judgments matter — sections 34–38

A previous judgment is not automatically proof of every fact written in it. The BSA gives judgments limited uses:

*   Certain decisions about probate, marriage, admiralty or insolvency may establish a legal status, such as whether a person was married.

*   A judgment about a public matter may be relevant, but is not always final proof of the matter.

*   A judgment may be relevant where the fact that the judgment exists is itself important.

*   Fraud, collusion or lack of jurisdiction may be shown where the Act allows it.

This is different from **res judicata**, a civil procedure rule that can stop the same issue being tried again after a final court decision.

### A. *Res Judicata* and Judgments *In Rem*

1. **Res judicata** is principally a rule under section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. It prevents re-litigation of an issue finally decided between the same parties by a competent court. It is not the general rule created by BSA sections 34–38.

2. A judgment **in rem** determines a legal status or condition against the world where the law gives it that effect. BSA section 35 makes certain final judgments in probate, matrimonial, admiralty or insolvency jurisdiction relevant.

3. Other previous judgments have only the uses specifically allowed by the BSA; a judgment is not automatically proof of every fact stated in it.

## 4. Expert opinion — sections 39–45

### A. Expert Opinion

An **expert** is a person with special knowledge or training who helps the court understand a technical subject. Experts may give opinions on science, foreign law, handwriting, fingerprints, electronic evidence and other areas covered by the BSA.

An expert gives help; the expert does not decide the case. The judge checks the expert’s training, information, method, reasoning and answers under cross-examination. If experts disagree, the judge compares how each reached their view. A confident opinion with no clear reasons may deserve little weight.

*   *State of H.P. v Jai Lal*, (1999) 7 SCC 280

    Expert evidence helps the judge, but the judge must assess the reasons behind it.

*   *Magan Bihari Lal v State of Punjab*, (1977) 2 SCC 210

    Handwriting opinions need careful review and should be considered with the other evidence.

The BSA also contains rules about opinions on handwriting, custom, relationship and the reasons supporting an opinion.

### Memory Aid: “Expert assists; judge decides.”

In an answer, identify the expert’s field and explain that the court evaluates the reasons and supporting material rather than treating the opinion as conclusive.

---

## 5. Character evidence — sections 46–50

**Character evidence** is evidence about a person’s general reputation or disposition. It is not simply proof that the person did one particular thing in the past.

*   In a civil case, character is usually not used to prove that a person acted in a certain way, unless character itself matters or the Act allows it.

*   In a criminal case, the accused’s previous good character may be relevant.

*   Bad character is generally not relevant unless the accused has raised good character or another legal exception applies.

*   A victim’s character or past sexual experience cannot be used to prove consent in a sexual-offence case, under the applicable statutory rules.

*   Character may matter when the court decides damages in cases covered by the Act.

### Quick Recap

Section 26 covers specific statements when the maker cannot testify. Sections 27–33 cover records and statements made in special circumstances. Prior judgments and character evidence have limited uses. Expert opinion assists the judge but does not replace the judge’s decision.
