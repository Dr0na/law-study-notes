# Module 4: Documents and Electronic Evidence

## Introduction

This module explains how documentary and electronic records are proved, when copies may be used, and how written terms are treated in evidence.

---

## 1. What counts as a document?

### A. Meaning of Document

The BSA treats information written, marked, recorded or stored on a physical or electronic medium as a document. Examples include a contract, letter, map, photograph, email, CCTV file, audio recording, video or database record.

Showing a document to the court does not always prove it is genuine or that everything in it is true. A party may need to prove where it came from, who made or signed it, what it contains and why it matters.

---

## 2. Primary and secondary evidence — sections 56–60

### A. The Best Evidence Rule

The **best evidence rule** requires the contents of a document to be proved by primary evidence unless the BSA permits secondary evidence. In practice, produce the original where required; use a copy only after showing that a statutory condition for secondary evidence is met.

*   **Primary evidence, section 57**

    The document itself shown to the court, subject to the explanations in the section.

*   **Secondary evidence, section 58**

    The types of substitutes the Act allows, such as certified copies, certain mechanical copies, copies checked against the original, or a witness’s account of a document the witness saw.

Section 59 gives the basic rule: use primary evidence unless the BSA allows secondary evidence. Section 60 allows a copy or other substitute in situations such as:

1. The original is with the other party, and that party does not produce it after proper notice.

2. The original has been lost or destroyed, without carelessness by the party relying on it.

3. The original cannot conveniently be brought to court, such as writing carved into a building.

4. The original is an official public document or the Act allows a certified copy.

5. Many accounts or records must be proved using the method permitted by the Act.

The party relying on a copy must explain why the original is missing and show that the copy is reliable. A photocopy is not automatically accepted. Section 64 deals with notice to produce, subject to exceptions.

### Memory Aid: Secondary-Evidence Conditions: “Other party, Lost, Cannot move, Public, Many records” (O-L-C-P-M)

These are examples of situations in which the Act may allow secondary evidence; the precise statutory condition must still be met.

## 3. Electronic and digital records — sections 61–63

Electronic evidence includes text messages, emails, CCTV, phone records, server logs, photographs, audio and video. **Section 61** says a record cannot be rejected only because it is electronic; it has the same legal effect as another document, subject to the BSA. **Section 62** says the contents of an electronic record are proved under section 63. **Section 63** sets the conditions for computer outputs and requires the certificate prescribed in that section and its Schedule when applicable. Check the current statutory certificate requirements before preparing one.

The court may ask: Is this the real file? Has it been changed? Who created or sent it? What device or system stored it? Was the file preserved properly? A screenshot may help, but it is not automatically conclusive or admissible by itself. Preserve the original device or file and its related information where possible.

*Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer*, (2014) 10 SCC 473, and *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*, (2020) 7 SCC 1, interpreted the certificate rule under the former Evidence Act. Use those cases with the BSA’s own sections 61–63 for current proceedings; do not cite former section 65B as though it were the BSA section.

### Memory Aid: Electronic Record: “Identify, Authenticate, Certify.”

Identify the relevant record, establish its source and integrity, and meet the certificate requirements in section 63 where they apply.

The **Information Technology Act, 2000** also supports electronic transactions. Section 4 recognises electronic records where the law requires writing; section 5 recognises electronic signatures where a signature is required; and section 10A recognises contracts formed electronically. These rules give legal recognition to electronic form. The BSA still controls how an electronic record is proved as evidence in court.

---

## 4. Signatures and attested documents — sections 65–73

A signature or handwriting may be proved by a person familiar with it, an expert, an admission, or comparison by the court, as the Act allows. Some documents must be signed in front of witnesses called **attesting witnesses**. Section 68 generally requires an attesting witness to prove such a document when one is available and able to testify. The BSA provides exceptions. A will is also governed by succession law, so check both statutes.

---

## 5. Public and private documents — sections 74–77

**Public documents** include official records of government bodies, courts and public officers made while doing their official work. Other documents are private. Where the BSA permits, a properly certified copy can prove the contents of a public document. A private document is normally proved by the original and by showing that it was signed or made as claimed, unless a secondary-evidence rule applies.

---

## 6. Presumptions about documents — sections 78–93

For some documents—such as official gazettes, certified copies, court records and certain electronic records—the court may presume that the document is genuine if the Act’s conditions are met. This does not always mean that every statement inside the document is true.

---

## 7. Written terms and oral evidence — sections 94–95

### A. The Parol Evidence Rule (Exclusion of Oral Evidence by Documentary Evidence)

The **parol evidence rule** is the principle reflected in sections 94–95: when the terms of a contract, grant or disposition of property have been reduced to writing, the document is generally used to prove those terms. Oral evidence cannot ordinarily contradict, vary, add to or subtract from them.

The rule has statutory exceptions. For example, evidence may be allowed to show fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution or a separate agreement on a matter on which the document is silent, where the BSA permits it.

If a contract, grant or property transfer has been put into writing, section 94 generally requires the document (or an allowed copy) to prove its terms. Once the written terms are proved, section 95 generally stops the parties from using oral statements to change, contradict or add to those terms.

The Act allows evidence in some situations, including to show fraud, intimidation, illegality, lack of proper signing or capacity, a consistent side agreement about a matter the document does not cover, a condition that had to happen first, or a later lawful change. These are exceptions to the rule, not permission to ignore a written agreement.

Sections 96–103 deal with unclear descriptions and words in documents. The court reads the whole document and may use outside facts only in the ways the Act permits.

**Quick checklist:** Identify the document → show that it is genuine → prove its contents → use the original unless a legal exception allows a copy → meet the electronic-record rules → check any signature and attestation requirements.

### Memory Aid: “I-G-C-O-E-S”

Identify, show Genuineness, prove Contents, prefer Original, meet Electronic-record rules, check Signatures. This is a study checklist, not a substitute for the applicable statutory provisions.
