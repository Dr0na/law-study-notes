# Module 2: Relevance, Admissions and Confessions

## Introduction

This module explains when facts and statements may be considered by a court. It covers the statutory links between facts, admissions, confessions and the safeguards that govern them.

---

## 1. Relevance and admissibility

**Relevance** means a legal connection between a fact and an issue in the case. **Admissibility** means the court is permitted to receive that evidence under the law. A fact may look useful but fail the legal test for relevance. Evidence may also be relevant but excluded by a special rule.

Section 3 BSA is the starting rule: parties may prove facts in issue and facts that the BSA declares relevant, and ordinarily no others. The judge decides whether evidence may be received.

### A. Logical Relevance and Legal Relevance

1. **Logical relevance** means common sense suggests that a fact may help prove another fact.

2. **Legal relevance** means the BSA recognises the connection between those facts.

A fact may be logically relevant but still inadmissible if no rule of law permits it. In an answer, identify the relevant BSA provision rather than relying only on common sense.

---

## 2. Connections that make facts relevant — sections 4–14

### A. Doctrine of *Res Gestae* — Section 4

*Res gestae* means “things done.” Section 4 makes relevant facts that form part of the same transaction as a fact in issue or relevant fact.

1. A statement made spontaneously during or immediately around an event may form part of the transaction.

2. Closeness of time, place and continuity matters. A later account with a real opportunity for fabrication is not automatically part of the transaction.

3. This is a recognised exception to the hearsay rule because the statement is closely connected with the event.

**Example:** A person cries out during an assault, “He is hitting me.” If made as part of the continuing event, the cry may be relevant under section 4.

**Case:** *Sukhar v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1999) 9 SCC 507, explains that a statement must be spontaneous and sufficiently connected with the occurrence.

### B. Occasion, Cause and Effect — Section 5

Facts that explain what led to an event or resulted from it may be relevant. **Example:** damage to a door may help explain how someone entered a room.

### C. Motive, Preparation and Conduct — Section 6

**Motive** is a reason for acting; **preparation** is a step taken in advance; **conduct** is what a person did before or after an event. These facts may support other evidence but do not prove guilt by themselves. Flight, for example, may have an innocent explanation.

### D. Facts Explaining or Identifying — Section 7

Facts that identify a person or thing, fix time or place, or explain a relevant fact may be admitted.

#### Test Identification Parade (TIP)

A **test identification parade** is an investigation step used to check whether a witness can pick out a person they say they saw. The parade itself is not usually substantive evidence; the witness’s identification in court is substantive evidence, and the TIP may support it. *Budhsen v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1970) 2 SCC 128, explains this distinction.

### E. Conspiracy / Common Design — Section 8

Where there is reasonable ground to believe that two or more persons conspired together, an act, statement or writing by one conspirator about their common intention may be relevant against the others if made after that intention was first formed and while it continued.

**Limit:** A statement made after the common design has ended is not automatically relevant under this rule.

**Case:** *Mirza Akbar v King-Emperor*, AIR 1940 PC 176, explains the requirement that the statement relate to the common intention while it continued.

### F. Facts Otherwise Relevant — Section 9

Section 9 covers facts inconsistent with a fact in issue or relevant fact, and facts that make its existence or non-existence highly probable or improbable.

#### Plea of *Alibi*

*Alibi* means “elsewhere.” An accused who says they were somewhere else when the offence occurred relies on facts that may be inconsistent with their presence at the scene. Once the prosecution has established the accused’s presence at the scene, the accused must establish the alibi by positive evidence. This does not lessen the prosecution’s burden to prove the offence.

**Case:** *Binay Kumar Singh v State of Bihar*, (1997) 1 SCC 283, explains the nature of an alibi and the accused’s burden to establish it.

### G. Other Relevant Facts — Sections 10–14

1. **Section 10 — damages:** facts that help the court assess the amount of damages.
2. **Section 11 — right or custom:** facts relevant to the existence of a right or custom.
3. **Section 12 — state of mind or body:** facts that show a relevant mental or physical condition.
4. **Section 13 — accidental or intentional:** facts that help determine whether an act was accidental or intentional.
5. **Section 14 — course of business:** facts showing a usual course of business when relevant.

### Memory Aid: “T-O-M-E-C-L-D-R-B” (sections 4–14)

**T**ransaction; **O**ccasion; **M**otive, preparation and conduct; **E**xplanation and identification; **C**onspiracy; **L**ikelihood; **D**amages; **R**ight/custom; **B**ody or mind, accident/intent and business. The sequence helps recall the topics; use the Act for the exact rule in each section.

## 3. Admissions — sections 15–21 and 25

### A. Admission

An **admission** is a statement—spoken, written or electronic—that suggests something about a fact in issue or another relevant fact. It must be made by a person and in circumstances covered by the BSA.

Admissions may come from a party, an authorised agent, a person with an interest in the matter, a predecessor in interest, or a person referred to by a party, as described in sections 16–18. The rules say when an admission can be proved against a person and when it can be proved by or for that person. Section 21 protects certain civil admissions made on the condition that they will not be used as evidence.

An admission is **not conclusive proof** by itself. Section 25 allows the court to consider it, and in some cases it may create an estoppel, but the other side may explain or challenge it.

**Example:** A says in an email, “I borrowed ₹20,000 from B and will repay it next month.” That statement may be an admission about the loan. The court still considers the context and other evidence.

## 4. Confessions — sections 22–24

### A. Confession

A **confession** is a statement by an accused person that admits guilt or substantially admits the essential facts of the offence. The BSA does not give a separate definition; courts look at what the words mean in context.

1. **Inducement, threat or promise — section 22**

   A confession may be excluded if pressure from a person in authority gives the accused a reason to expect an advantage or avoid harm in the case.

2. **Confession to police — section 23**

   A confession made to a police officer generally cannot be used to prove guilt.

3. **Confession in police custody — section 23**

   It is generally excluded unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate.

4. **Discovery exception**

   If information from an accused in custody leads to finding a fact, only the part that clearly relates to what was found may be proved. The whole confession does not become admissible.

5. **Co-accused — section 24**

   A co-accused’s confession may be considered only in a joint trial for the same offence and under the section’s conditions. It is weak evidence and should not usually be the only reason for conviction.

A confession must be voluntary. If the accused later takes it back, the court checks it carefully and normally looks for supporting evidence.

### B. Discovery of Fact — Section 23(2)

This is the **discovery rule** (formerly section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act). When information from an accused in police custody leads to discovery of a fact, only the part of the information that **distinctly relates to the fact discovered** may be proved.

**Example:** “I hid the knife under the bridge” may be proved to the extent it distinctly relates to the knife found there. Any unrelated confession in the same statement remains excluded.

**Case:** *Pulukuri Kottaya v King-Emperor*, AIR 1947 PC 67, is the leading authority on the limited scope of the discovery rule.

### C. Judicial and Extra-Judicial Confessions

1. **Judicial confession**

   Made before a Magistrate or court in judicial proceedings, subject to applicable safeguards.

2. **Extra-judicial confession**

   Made outside court to another person. The court assesses whether it was voluntary, truthful and reliable.

3. **Retracted confession**

   A confession later withdrawn by its maker. The court treats it with caution and looks for assurance in the circumstances and other evidence.

Each statement must still satisfy the BSA’s admissibility rules.

---

## 5. Admission and confession compared

| Admissions | Confessions |
|---|---|
| Broad type of statement; may concern one relevant fact | Narrower statement that substantially admits guilt |
| Can arise in civil or criminal cases | Usually made by an accused in a criminal case |
| Example: admitting presence or ownership | Example: admitting the essential acts of the offence |
| Mainly sections 15–21 | Special rules in sections 22–24 |

Every confession is an admission, but not every admission is a confession.

---

## 6. Important cases

*   *Pakala Narayana Swami v Emperor*, AIR 1939 PC 47

    A confession must admit the offence or substantially all its essential facts; an incriminating statement alone may not be enough.

*   *Aghnoo Nagesia v State of Bihar*, AIR 1966 SC 119

    A confession to police is excluded, apart from the part that may be proved under the discovery rule.

*   *Pulukuri Kottaya v King-Emperor*, AIR 1947 PC 67

    Only the information that distinctly relates to the discovered fact is admissible.
