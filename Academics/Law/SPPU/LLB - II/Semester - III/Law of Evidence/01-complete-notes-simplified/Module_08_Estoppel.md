# Module 8: Estoppel and Mistakes in Handling Evidence

## Introduction

This module explains estoppel, special rules for particular relationships, promissory estoppel and the effect of wrongly admitting or rejecting evidence.

---

## 1. Estoppel — section 121 BSA

### A. Doctrine of Estoppel by Conduct — Section 121

**Estoppel** prevents a person from denying a position they led another person to believe and act on. Section 121 provides that where a person, by declaration, act or omission, intentionally causes or permits another to believe a thing to be true and to act on that belief, that person (and their representative) cannot later deny it in proceedings between them, subject to the section’s terms.

The doctrine has these elements:

1. **Representation:** a declaration, act or omission conveys a position.

2. **Intention or knowledge:** the representor intended or permitted the other person to rely on it.

3. **Reliance and action:** the other person believed the representation and acted on that belief.

4. **Inconsistent denial:** the representor is prevented from denying the represented fact in proceedings covered by the section.

**In simple terms:** one party cannot knowingly lead another to act on a position and then take the opposite position against them. Estoppel prevents denial; it does not by itself create title or override a statute.

**Example:** A knowingly lets B believe that A owns a piece of land and encourages B to buy it. If B relied on that, A may be prevented from later denying the position against B.

### Memory Aid: “Representation, Intention, Reliance, Change” (R-I-R-C)

Identify the representation or conduct, the intention or knowledge that it would be relied on, the other person’s reliance, and the resulting change of position. Then check that applying estoppel is legally permitted.

Estoppel is a rule of evidence and fairness. It does not create a claim in every case. It also cannot be used to defeat a statute or the Constitution.

## 2. Special rules — sections 122–123

*   **Tenant and licensee, section 122**

    A tenant generally cannot deny that the landlord had title when the tenancy began. A licensee generally cannot deny that the person who gave the licence had authority to do so when it began. The section has limits and exceptions.

*   **Acceptor, bailee and licensee, section 123**

    An acceptor of a bill of exchange generally cannot deny the drawer’s authority. A bailee or licensee generally cannot deny that the person who gave them the goods or permission had authority at the start. The section also contains exceptions.

Apply these rules only to the relationships and situations named in the Act.

## 3. Promissory estoppel

**Promissory estoppel** is a court-developed fairness rule. It may stop a person or government from going back on a clear promise if someone relied on it and it would be unfair to withdraw it. Unlike ordinary estoppel, it can involve a promise about future conduct.

There is **no estoppel against a statute or the Constitution**. A public authority cannot be forced to do something illegal or outside its powers. Courts consider whether the promise was clear, whether the person making it had authority, whether someone relied on it, and whether public interest or the law requires a different result.

### A. Promissory Estoppel: Equitable Doctrine

**Promissory estoppel** may prevent a party, including government acting within its lawful authority, from withdrawing a clear promise where another person relied on it and withdrawal would be inequitable. It is an equitable doctrine, not a licence to compel an unlawful act.

**Case:** *Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills Co. v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1979) 2 SCC 409, recognises promissory estoppel against government in appropriate circumstances. The doctrine remains subject to statutory power and overriding public interest.

### B. No Estoppel Against Statute or Constitution

The maxim **“there is no estoppel against the statute”** means a representation cannot require a public authority or court to act contrary to legislation. Estoppel also cannot validate an act prohibited by the Constitution or waive a constitutional mandate where waiver is not permitted.

*   *Union of India v Anglo Afghan Agencies*, AIR 1968 SC 718

    A government representation may be enforced in an appropriate case.

*   *Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills Co. v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1979) 2 SCC 409

    A person may rely on an authorised government promise in appropriate circumstances.

*   *Kasinka Trading v Union of India*, (1995) 1 SCC 274

    Government may change a concession where law and public interest justify it.

## 4. Estoppel compared with related rules

| Estoppel | Res judicata | Waiver |
|---|---|---|
| Stops denial of a fact after another person relied on it | Stops re-trying a matter finally decided by a competent court | A person chooses to give up a known right |
| Based on a representation and reliance | Based on a final court judgment and procedure rules | Based on a voluntary choice |

They can all prevent unfair inconsistency, but they are different legal rules.

## 5. Wrongly admitting or rejecting evidence — section 169

Section 169 says a court decision is not reversed only because the trial court wrongly let evidence in or kept evidence out, if the mistake did not affect the result. The appeal court asks:

*   If the trial court admitted evidence it should not have admitted, was there enough other proper evidence for the decision?

*   If the trial court rejected evidence it should have admitted, would that evidence have changed the result?

This avoids a new trial over a harmless mistake. But if the error caused real unfairness or could have changed the result, the appeal court must address it.

### Quick Recap

Estoppel protects a person who relied on another’s representation, but cannot require illegal action. Section 169 separates harmless evidence mistakes from mistakes that could change the decision.
