# Module 1: Introduction to Evidence

## Introduction

This module introduces the purpose of evidence law and the basic ideas used throughout the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA). It explains what counts as evidence, how relevance differs from admissibility, and how courts assess proof.

---

## 1. What does evidence law do?

Substantive law tells us what rights and offences exist. Procedure law tells us how a case moves through court. **Evidence law tells the court what facts it may consider, how those facts must be shown, and who must prove them.** It helps the judge decide cases using information that has been checked, not guesses.

The current law is the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)**. It replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 for proceedings covered by the BSA from 1 July 2024. The BSA generally applies to court cases, including court-martial proceedings. It does not apply to affidavits or arbitration proceedings (section 1). A case already underway when the new law began may be covered by a saving rule, so check the dates.

The BSA mainly explains how facts are proved. It does not create most rights or offences. In a criminal case, read it along with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.

---

## 2. Why the law changed

For many years, evidence law came from the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. Parliament replaced it with the BSA to restate the rules in a current statute and expressly address electronic and digital records. The basic job remains the same: help courts hold fair hearings and decide cases on relevant, properly tested evidence. The BSA is arranged around relevance, proof, documents, burden of proof, estoppel, witnesses and witness examination.

---

## 3. Relevance and admissibility

*   **Relevant** means the law connects a fact to something the court has to decide.

*   **Admissible** means the court is allowed to receive the evidence, and any required conditions are met.

A fact can seem useful but still not be legally relevant. Even relevant evidence may be kept out by a rule. For example, a confession made to a police officer is generally not allowed to prove guilt under section 23.

---

## 4. Important words in section 2

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Fact, section 2(f) | Something a person can sense, or a mental state the person knows about |
| Fact in issue, section 2(g) | The main fact the court must decide |
| Relevant fact, section 2(k) | A fact connected to the main issue in a way the BSA recognises |
| Evidence, section 2(e) | Witness statements and documents, including electronic or digital records, shown to the court |
| Document, section 2(d) | Information written, marked, recorded or stored on a physical or electronic medium |
| Proved, section 2(j) | The court believes the fact, or thinks it is likely enough to be true |
| Disproved, section 2(c) | The court believes the fact is false, or thinks it is likely enough to be false |
| Not proved, section 2(i) | The court is not sure enough either way |

**Example:** In a theft case, “Did A take the phone?” is a fact in issue. A person who saw A take it gives direct evidence. A location record or a recovered phone may be a relevant clue, but it still must be properly proved.

---

## 5. Main kinds of evidence

*   **Oral evidence**

    What a witness says in court.

*   **Documentary evidence**

    Papers and electronic records, such as contracts, messages or CCTV files.

*   **Direct evidence**

    A witness personally saw, heard or sensed the fact.

*   **Circumstantial evidence**

    Clues that help the court work out what happened. A full set of reliable clues can prove a case. In *Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v State of Maharashtra*, (1984) 4 SCC 116, the Supreme Court explained the safeguards for deciding a case on such clues.

*   **Primary and secondary evidence**

    The original document and the kinds of copies or substitutes the law allows.

*   **Hearsay**

    A witness repeats what someone else said to prove that it was true. It is usually not allowed unless a legal exception applies.

### A. Panchsheel Principles for Circumstantial Evidence

In *Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v State of Maharashtra*, the Supreme Court restated five safeguards, often called the **Panchsheel principles**, for deciding a case based on circumstantial evidence:

1. Each circumstance must be firmly established.

2. The established circumstances must fit only the conclusion of guilt.

3. The circumstances must be conclusive in nature and tendency.

4. They must exclude every reasonable explanation other than guilt.

5. The chain must be complete enough to show that, in all human probability, the accused committed the act.

**In simple terms:** Each clue must be proved; all the clues must point in the same direction; and together they must leave no reasonable innocent explanation.

---

## 6. Presumptions

A presumption is a conclusion the court is allowed or required to draw after a basic fact is shown.

*   **May presume, section 2(h)**

    The court can accept the fact for now or ask for more proof.

*   **Shall presume, section 2(l)**

    The court must accept the fact unless someone disproves it.

*   **Conclusive proof, section 2(b)**

    Once the starting fact is proved, the law does not allow evidence to disprove the resulting fact.

Usually, the party relying on a presumption must first prove the basic facts needed to trigger it.

---

## 7. How strong must the proof be?

In a criminal case, the prosecution must normally prove guilt **beyond reasonable doubt**. This means the evidence must leave no reasonable doubt; it does not mean every imagined doubt. In a civil case, a claim is usually proved if it is **more likely true than not**. An accused relying on a legal exception usually has to show that the exception is more likely than not. The prosecution still has to prove the basic offence.

---

## 8. A simple way to analyse an evidence question

1. What main fact must the court decide?

2. Which other facts help prove or disprove it?

3. Does the BSA allow this kind of evidence?

4. Has the party shown that it is genuine and properly proved?

5. Who has to prove the fact, and how strong must that proof be?

### Memory Aid: “Issue, Link, Law, Proof, Burden” (ILLPB)

Identify the issue, link the supporting facts, check the legal rule, confirm proof, then identify the burden and standard. Use the questions above in order; the mnemonic is only a prompt, not a separate legal test.

**Cases:** *Kali Ram v State of Himachal Pradesh*, (1973) 2 SCC 808, explains that reasonable doubt in a criminal case benefits the accused. *Sharad Birdhichand Sarda* explains how courts assess circumstantial evidence. These cases were decided under the earlier Act; cite the current BSA sections in present-day answers.

### Quick Recap

Evidence law asks: what may the court consider, how must it be proved, and who has to prove it?
