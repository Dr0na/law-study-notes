# Module 7: Oral Evidence and Questioning Witnesses

## Introduction

This module covers direct oral evidence, the order of examining witnesses, cross-examination, credibility, memory and the judge’s powers.

---

## 1. Oral evidence should be direct — sections 54–55

### A. Oral Evidence and the Rule of Directness

**Oral evidence** is testimony given by a witness in court. Under section 55, oral evidence must be **direct**: a witness who says they saw or heard a fact should have perceived it themselves.

### B. Hearsay Rule and its Exceptions

**Hearsay** is an out-of-court statement repeated to prove that its contents are true. It is generally excluded because the original maker is not ordinarily before the court to give evidence on oath and face cross-examination.

The BSA recognises specific exceptions, including *res gestae* (section 4), admissions (sections 15–21), certain statements by unavailable persons (section 26) and former testimony (section 27). Identify the applicable exception and establish its conditions.

---

## 2. The usual order of questioning — sections 140–143

1. **Examination-in-chief**

   The party who called the witness asks questions to get their evidence.

2. **Cross-examination**

   The other party asks questions to test the account and the witness’s reliability.

3. **Re-examination**

   The first party asks follow-up questions to explain matters raised in cross-examination.

If a new topic comes up in re-examination, the other side may be allowed to ask more questions about it. The judge controls the process and can ask questions to clarify important facts.

### Memory Aid: “Chief, Cross, Re-examine” (C-C-R)

The party calling the witness begins; the opposing party tests the account; the first party may clarify matters raised in cross-examination.

---

## 3. Character witnesses and leading questions — sections 145–146

Section 145 covers witnesses called to speak about a person’s character. Section 146 explains **leading questions**. A leading question suggests the answer the questioner wants. For example, “You saw the red car, didn’t you?” is leading. These questions are generally restricted during examination-in-chief and re-examination on disputed matters unless the judge allows them. They are generally allowed in cross-examination, subject to the judge’s control.

---

## 4. Documents and earlier statements — sections 147–148

If a witness is asked about a document, the document must be produced or its contents proved under the documentary-evidence rules. To show that the witness has changed their story, the questioner must point to the specific part of the earlier written statement. If the witness denies saying it, the difference must be proved in the proper way.

A police statement is not itself proof that the statement is true. It can only be used in the way allowed by the applicable criminal-procedure law.

---

## 5. Cross-examination and protection of witnesses — sections 149–155

Cross-examination can test what the witness saw or heard, their memory, truthfulness, possible bias and consistency. The judge can stop questions that are insulting, needlessly offensive, indecent, scandalous or asked without a reasonable basis. The judge decides whether a witness must answer a question under the Act.

Cross-examination is an important way to test evidence. It is not permission to bully or humiliate a witness.

### Memory Aid: Cross-Examination Tests “S-M-T-B-C”

what the witness **S**aw or heard, **M**emory, **T**ruthfulness, **B**ias and **C**onsistency. The court controls improper questions under the Act.

---

## 6. Hostile witness — section 157

A **hostile witness** is a witness who does not support the party who called them, or appears unwilling to tell the truth. The judge may let that party ask cross-examination questions. The witness’s evidence is not erased; the judge may accept the parts that seem reliable and reject the rest.

*Sat Paul v Delhi Administration*, (1976) 1 SCC 727, confirms that reliable parts of a hostile witness’s evidence may still be used.

---

## 7. Challenging or supporting credibility — sections 156–158

The BSA permits a witness’s credibility to be challenged in specified ways, including showing bias, a bribe or a prior inconsistent statement. It also allows some facts to support a witness’s account. The judge looks at the whole testimony, not just one sentence or inconsistency.

### A. Impeaching the Credit of a Witness

**Impeaching credit** means challenging whether a witness should be believed. Section 158 permits specified methods, including evidence that the witness is unworthy of credit, proof of bribery or corrupt inducement, and proof of a former statement inconsistent with the present testimony, subject to the section’s requirements.

---

## 8. Supporting testimony and earlier statements — sections 159–161

The BSA allows a witness’s account to be supported by certain circumstances noticed at the time, or by an earlier statement about the same fact. It also explains how the parties may test or support statements admitted under section 26 or 27. The earlier statement must meet the legal conditions; repeating a story does not automatically prove it true.

---

## 9. Refreshing memory — sections 162–164

A witness may look at a note made when an event happened or soon after, while it was still fresh in their memory. They may also use another person’s note if they read it at that time and knew it was correct. In some situations, an expert may refer to professional books. The witness can testify to facts recorded in the note if sure the record was correct, even if the details are no longer remembered. The other side may inspect the note and ask questions about it.

### A. Refreshing Memory and Recorded Recollection

Under section 162, a witness may refresh memory by consulting a writing made at the time or sufficiently soon after the event. This does not mean that a document can simply be read into evidence: the witness must testify in accordance with the BSA, and the opposing party may inspect and cross-examine on the writing.

---

## 10. Producing documents — sections 165–167

A witness summoned to bring a document must bring it if it is in their possession, even if someone objects to its use. The judge decides whether it can be admitted. The Act also explains what happens when a party asks the other side to produce a document and how a document that was withheld may be used.

---

## 11. The judge’s powers — section 168

The judge may ask questions or order a document produced to discover or prove relevant facts. The judge must still base the decision on relevant, properly proved facts, respect privilege, and avoid questions that the law would not allow a party to ask. The judge must remain fair and cannot act as the lawyer for either side.

### Quick Recap

The normal order is chief, cross-examination, then re-examination. Cross-examination tests the account. The BSA also provides rules for earlier statements, memory aids, documents and the judge’s questions.
