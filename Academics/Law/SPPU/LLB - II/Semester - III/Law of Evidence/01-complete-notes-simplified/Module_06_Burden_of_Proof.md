# Module 6: Burden of Proof, Presumptions and Judicial Notice

## Introduction

This module explains who must prove facts, how presumptions affect that burden, and the standards courts use in civil and criminal cases.

---

## 1. Who must prove a fact?

### A. Meaning of Burden of Proof

The **burden of proof** tells us which side must persuade the court that a fact is true. The main rules are in sections 104–120 BSA.

*   **Section 104**

    A person asking the court to decide a right or liability based on facts they assert must prove those facts.

*   **Section 105**

    The burden lies on the person who would lose if neither side gave any evidence.

*   **Section 106**

    The person who wants the court to believe a particular fact usually has to prove it, unless a law puts the burden elsewhere.

*   **Section 107**

    A person offering evidence must prove the facts needed to make that evidence admissible.

The **legal burden** is the duty to prove a case or an important part of it. It usually stays with the side the law assigns it to. The **onus of proof** is the practical need to bring evidence about a fact at a certain point in the case. It can move from one side to the other as evidence is presented.

### B. Legal Burden and Evidential Onus

1. **Legal burden (persuasive burden)**

   The duty to establish a claim or defence to the required standard. It generally remains on the party on whom the law places it.

2. **Evidential burden (onus of proof)**

   The practical duty to produce evidence on a particular issue. It may shift as evidence is introduced.

In a criminal case, the prosecution generally retains the legal burden of proving every element of the offence beyond reasonable doubt. A statutory presumption or defence exception may place a limited burden on the accused, but it does not erase the prosecution’s burden to prove the offence.

In a criminal case, the prosecution normally has to prove guilt **beyond reasonable doubt**. The accused does not have to prove innocence. If the accused relies on a legal exception, the accused may have to show that the exception probably applies, but the prosecution still has to prove the basic offence.

**Example:** A says B borrowed money and did not repay it. A must prove the loan. If B says the money was repaid, B usually has to prove repayment.

### Memory Aid: Sections 104–107: “Assert, Lose, Believe, Admit” (A-L-B-A)

The person asserting a right or liability proves it; ask who would lose if neither side gave evidence; the person asking the court to believe a particular fact proves it; and the person relying on evidence proves the facts needed for its admissibility.

## 2. Special rules about burden — sections 108–116

*   **Legal exceptions, section 108**

    An accused who relies on a general or special exception must show the facts that bring the case within it. Usually, the accused must show this on a balance of probabilities, not beyond reasonable doubt.

*   **Facts especially known to a person, section 109**

    A person may have to prove a fact that is especially within their own knowledge. This does not let the prosecution avoid proving its case. *Shambhu Nath Mehra v State of Ajmer*, AIR 1956 SC 404, explains this limit.

*   **Life or death, sections 110–111**

    The Act sets rules for a person known to be alive within thirty years, and a person not heard from for seven years by people who would naturally hear from them. The rule does not automatically prove the exact date of death.

*   **Relationships, section 112**

    Special rules apply to relationships such as partners, landlord and tenant, and principal and agent.

*   **Ownership, section 113**

    Possession may support a presumption of ownership unless the other side proves otherwise.

*   **Active confidence, section 114**

    A person who was trusted to act for another may have to show that the transaction was fair and honest.

*   **Certain offences, section 115**

    The presumption applies only when the exact conditions in the section are met.

*   **Legitimacy, section 116**

    The Act creates a strong presumption that a child born during a valid marriage, or within the statutory period after it ends while the mother remains unmarried, is legitimate. The Act states the limited condition that can rebut it.

## 3. Presumptions for certain offences — sections 117–120

The BSA has special presumptions: section 117 concerns abetment of suicide by a married woman; section 118 concerns dowry death; section 119 lets the court presume certain facts from ordinary experience; and section 120 concerns absence of consent in specified rape prosecutions. The prosecution must first prove the basic facts required by the relevant section. Only then does the presumption apply.

For a dowry-death presumption, the court asks whether the statutory conditions are met, including cruelty or harassment linked to a dowry demand **soon before** the death. “Soon before” means there must be a close connection; it does not necessarily mean immediately before. *Kaliyaperumal v State of Tamil Nadu*, (2004) 9 SCC 157, discusses the earlier version of this rule.

## 4. Judicial notice and admitted facts — sections 51–53

**Judicial notice** means the court accepts certain basic facts without asking parties to prove them in the usual way. These include laws in force and other matters listed in sections 51–52.

Under section 53, parties may agree that a fact is true. The court can then treat proof as unnecessary, although it may still ask for proof if needed.

Section 2 explains three kinds of presumption:

*   **May presume**

    The court may accept the fact or ask for more proof.

*   **Shall presume**

    The court must accept the fact unless someone disproves it.

*   **Conclusive proof**

    Once the starting fact is proved, the law does not allow the other fact to be disproved.

### A. Presumptions of Fact and Presumptions of Law

1. A **presumption of fact** is ordinarily permissive and drawn from experience; the court may infer a fact from proved circumstances.

2. A **rebuttable presumption of law** is directed by statute. Depending on its wording, the court must presume the fact unless it is disproved.

3. **Conclusive proof** prevents evidence from disproving the resulting fact once the foundational fact is established, subject to the statute’s terms.

    Always identify the foundational facts first. A presumption does not arise merely because a party invokes its name.

## 5. Standards of proof and important cases

*   Criminal case: guilt must be proved beyond reasonable doubt.

*   Civil case: the claim must be more likely true than not. This is called the **balance of probabilities**.

*   Accused relying on an exception: usually has to show that it probably applies.

*Woolmington v DPP*, [1935] AC 462, states the prosecution’s general duty to prove guilt. *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v State of Gujarat*, AIR 1964 SC 1563, explains the accused’s burden for a legal exception and the continuing importance of reasonable doubt. *Anil Rishi v Gurbaksh Singh*, (2006) 5 SCC 558, explains that the party asserting a fact generally carries the burden.

### Memory Aid: standards

“Criminal: beyond reasonable doubt; Civil: more likely than not.” For a defence exception, remember that the accused’s burden is generally the balance of probabilities, while the prosecution retains the burden to prove the offence.

### Quick Recap

Ask who is making the claim, who would lose without evidence, whether a special presumption applies, and how strong the proof must be. The special-knowledge rule does not shift the whole criminal case to the accused.
