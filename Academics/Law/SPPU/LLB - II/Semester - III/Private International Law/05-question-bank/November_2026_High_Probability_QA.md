# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Q&A — Private International Law (LDSE 0708)

Reasoned predictions from the syllabus and April/October 2025 papers; not guaranteed. Current 70-mark format: Part A 2×15, Part B 3×10, Part C 2×5. PIL rules in India are partly uncodified and topic-specific. Distinguish the law governing the merits from jurisdiction and recognition/enforcement.

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain why Private International Law is called conflict of laws. Discuss its nature, sources and theories.

Private International Law (PIL), or conflict of laws, is the domestic law a court applies when a private dispute contains a foreign element—for example, parties domiciled in different countries, a marriage abroad, a foreign contract or property situated overseas. It is not itself a single international code. It helps answer three questions: which court has jurisdiction; which law governs each issue; and whether a foreign judgment or award will be recognised or enforced.

PIL is municipal law applied by a forum court, although it draws on statutes, precedent, treaty obligations and comparative approaches. It differs from public international law, which primarily governs relations among States and international organisations. Indian PIL sources include the Constitution, CPC sections 13–14 and 44A, the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, personal laws, other statutes and judicial decisions. India has not codified a comprehensive conflicts code.

Theories include territorialism (apply the law of the place), comity (respect foreign legal acts subject to forum limits), vested-rights theory (recognise rights validly created under a competent foreign law), and modern proper-law/most-significant-relationship approaches. A forum does not mechanically apply foreign law: it first characterises the issue, identifies a connecting factor, selects the governing law and considers renvoi, public policy and mandatory forum rules where relevant.

**Conclusion.** “Conflict of laws” describes the choice-of-court, choice-of-law and recognition questions raised by a foreign element. The court applies its own conflicts rules, which may direct it to apply foreign substantive law.

### Q2. Explain characterization and renvoi, including the stages and theories.

**Characterization** (classification) is the process of assigning a legal issue to a category—such as contract, marriage, succession, property or procedure—so the forum can select the relevant choice-of-law rule. It matters because different categories use different connecting factors. A court may characterise under lex fori (law of the forum), lex causae (law governing the issue) or a comparative approach; Indian courts generally begin with the forum’s own legal concepts while seeking a fair and coherent result.

Stages: (1) characterise the facts and legal issue; (2) identify the relevant conflicts rule and connecting factor (domicile, situs, place of contracting/performance or habitual residence); (3) select the proper law; (4) determine whether renvoi arises; and (5) consider public policy and mandatory rules.

**Renvoi** arises when the forum’s conflicts rule refers to foreign law and the foreign conflicts rules refer the matter back to the forum (remission) or onward to a third State (transmission). The remission approach accepts the reference back; renvoi proper may follow a foreign court’s whole law including conflicts rules; the foreign-court theory asks what decision a court of the foreign country would make. Courts may reject renvoi where it creates circularity or undermines certainty. *Re Annesley* [1926] Ch 692 and *Re Ross* [1930] 1 Ch 377 are classic English authorities illustrating renvoi in succession.

**Conclusion.** Characterization determines which choice rule applies; renvoi then addresses whether the selected foreign law means its internal law alone or includes its conflicts rules. Both should be discussed only where the issue and connecting factor make them relevant.

### Q3. Explain domicile and its importance. Can a minor change domicile?

Domicile is the legal connection between a person and a country regarded as their permanent home for specified legal questions. It is not identical to nationality or residence. It may determine personal status, succession to movables, capacity and jurisdiction in some matrimonial matters.

Fundamental principles: every person has a domicile; a person cannot have two domiciles for the same purpose at the same time; a domicile continues until another is acquired; and domicile requires both residence and intention to make a home there indefinitely. Domicile of origin is acquired at birth under the applicable rule and revives if a domicile of choice is abandoned before a new one is acquired. A domicile of choice requires actual residence plus an intention to reside permanently or indefinitely; temporary absence does not necessarily end it.

A minor ordinarily has a dependent domicile determined under the applicable personal/conflicts law, rather than independently acquiring a domicile of choice. Rules differ by legal system and may depend on the child’s parents, guardian, age and actual residence. A minor’s domicile can change when the legally relevant parental/guardian connection changes, but the child’s own intention generally does not have the same independent effect as an adult’s. Do not state that every minor always follows the father; the applicable law and facts matter. A corporation’s domicile is generally associated with its place of incorporation/registered seat, subject to the issue and governing rule.

**Conclusion.** Domicile is a connecting factor requiring both residence and intention for a choice domicile. A minor’s position is governed by dependence rules, not the adult test applied mechanically.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. State the conditions for recognition and enforcement of a foreign judgment in India.

CPC section 13 provides that a foreign judgment is conclusive on matters directly adjudicated between the same parties, except where: it was not pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction; it was not given on the merits; it appears on the face of proceedings to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or refusal to recognise Indian law where applicable; proceedings oppose natural justice; it was obtained by fraud; or it sustains a claim founded on breach of Indian law. Section 14 creates a rebuttable presumption of jurisdiction when a certified copy is produced. In a reciprocating territory, a qualifying money decree may be executed under section 44A as if passed by an Indian district court, subject to exclusions and section 13. Non-money decrees and decrees from non-reciprocating territories generally require a suit in India on the foreign judgment/cause of action. In matrimonial cases, *Y. Narasimha Rao v Y. Venkata Lakshmi*, (1991) 3 SCC 451, applies jurisdictional and merits safeguards based on the parties’ matrimonial law.

### Q5. Explain material and formal validity of marriage in PIL.

Formal validity concerns the form or ceremony of marriage and is generally governed by the law of the place where it was celebrated (lex loci celebrationis), subject to statutory exceptions and public policy. Material validity concerns capacity and essential conditions—age, consent, prohibited degrees and existing marriage—and may be governed by each party’s personal law/domicile law under the relevant conflicts rule. Indian cases must also apply the governing personal statute, such as the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 or Special Marriage Act, 1954, where applicable. Jurisdiction to grant divorce is a separate issue from the law governing validity. Recognition of a foreign divorce decree is tested under CPC section 13 and the law governing matrimonial status; *Y. Narasimha Rao* is the leading Indian authority.

### Q6. Explain enforcement of foreign arbitral awards in India.

Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 governs enforcement of foreign awards under the New York Convention (sections 44–52) and Geneva Convention (sections 53–60). An applicant files the original/certified award, arbitration agreement and necessary evidence/translation under section 47. Section 48 provides limited grounds to refuse enforcement, including incapacity/invalid agreement, lack of notice or inability to present the case, award beyond submission, improper tribunal/procedure, award not binding or set aside/suspended, non-arbitrability and conflict with Indian public policy. The court does not rehear the merits. Once enforceable, the award is deemed a decree under section 49. Distinguish enforcement of an award from recognition/enforcement of a foreign court judgment.

## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Domicile of a corporation

A corporation has a legal domicile usually associated with its incorporation or registered seat, but the relevant connecting factor depends on the issue (jurisdiction, capacity, insolvency or tax). Its place of business may differ from domicile. Apply the relevant statute and conflicts rule.

### Transfer of immovable property

Rights in immovable property are generally governed by the law of the place where the property is situated (lex situs). Indian immovable property is governed by Indian law, including applicable transfer and registration statutes. The situs rule promotes certainty and local control.

### Double actionability in tort

Traditional English common law required a tort to be actionable both under the law of the forum and the law of the place where it occurred, subject to a flexible exception. The UK Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1995 modifies the rule for many torts. India’s approach must be determined from applicable Indian law and precedent; do not treat UK legislation as directly binding in India.

### Renvoi

Renvoi occurs when the forum refers an issue to foreign law and the foreign conflicts rule refers it back to the forum or onward to another State. The court must decide whether “foreign law” includes that country’s conflicts rules. It may accept remission, apply whole foreign law or reject renvoi to preserve certainty.
