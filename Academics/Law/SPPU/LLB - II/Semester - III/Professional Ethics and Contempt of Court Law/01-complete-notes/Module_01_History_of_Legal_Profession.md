# Module 1: History of the Legal Profession in India

## 1. Ancient period

Ancient Indian dispute resolution included royal courts and local bodies such as village assemblies and guilds. Dharma texts discussed adjudication, evidence, procedure and the roles of judges and advisers. The modern advocate as a regulated, independent professional with a statutory right of audience did not exist in the same form. Historical accounts should therefore distinguish legal advisers and representatives from the present Bar.

## 2. Medieval period

Under the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal administration, justice was administered through imperial, provincial and local institutions, with Islamic law applied in relevant forums and local customs continuing in other settings. Qazis and legal scholars (including muftis) performed judicial or advisory functions. The system was plural and varied across regions and communities; it should not be presented as a uniform modern bar structure.

## 3. British rule

The East India Company and Crown developed formal courts and legal professions. Key milestones include:

- **Regulating Act, 1773** and the establishment of the Supreme Court at Calcutta in 1774, followed by Supreme Courts at Madras and Bombay;
- **Charter Act, 1833** and the creation of a legislative framework that supported modern codification;
- **Legal Practitioners Act, 1846**, which broadened eligibility to practise in courts;
- **High Courts Act, 1861**, creating High Courts and consolidating superior court structures;
- **Legal Practitioners Act, 1879**, consolidating categories and regulation of legal practitioners;
- **Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926**, providing Bar Councils at certain High Courts and a more organised profession;
- **All India Bar Committee (1951–53)**, whose recommendations supported a unified All-India Bar and integrated regulation.

Colonial professional structures expanded court-based advocacy and codified procedure, but were shaped by colonial administration and unequal access to legal education and institutions.

## 4. After Independence

The **Advocates Act, 1961** consolidated the law relating to legal practitioners and established State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India (BCI). It recognised a single class of legal practitioners called advocates, created enrolment rolls, regulated the right to practise, established disciplinary jurisdiction, and authorised rules on professional conduct and legal education. The Act operates alongside constitutional court powers, court rules and BCI rules.

Key professional developments include legal aid, public interest litigation, specialisation, growth of law firms and in-house work, continuing professional education initiatives, and digitisation of court processes. Access, affordability, ethics and independence remain central concerns.

## 5. Globalisation and the profession

Globalisation has expanded cross-border transactions, arbitration, international commercial work, comparative legal practice and demand for specialised expertise. It has also raised questions about foreign lawyer practice, legal services markets, confidentiality across digital systems, conflicts of interest and maintaining public-interest obligations. Foreign law firms and lawyers may practise in India only within the applicable statutory, regulatory and Supreme Court framework; do not assume unrestricted market access.

## 6. Conclusion

The profession evolved from locally varied systems of adjudication and advice into a statutory, self-regulated Bar under the Advocates Act. The modern advocate has both a client-facing role and a public responsibility to the administration of justice.
