# Module 8 — E-Courts in India and Digital Transformation of the Judiciary

> **Currency note:** E-filing and virtual-hearing rules vary by court and change over time. This module explains the syllabus concepts. For actual filing, use the current official portal, practice directions and user manual for the relevant court.

## 1. Meaning, evolution and purpose

“E-Courts” describes the use of information and communication technology in court administration and adjudication: electronic case records, filing, process service, cause lists, case-status information, digital payments, video-conferencing facilities and digital display of documents. Technology is infrastructure for judicial work; it does not itself decide rights or replace procedural fairness.

India’s e-Courts Mission Mode Project is a centrally sponsored initiative of the Department of Justice, implemented with the judiciary. Phase I focused on computerisation and basic ICT infrastructure in district and subordinate courts. Phase II expanded services and connectivity. Phase III, approved in 2023, emphasises a digital repository of court records, digitisation, universal access, cloud infrastructure and user-centred services. Treat dates, totals and implementation status as time-sensitive; consult the Department of Justice and e-Courts project pages for current data.

## 2. Core infrastructure and services

- **Hardware and connectivity:** computers, scanners, document displays, secure networks, video-conference equipment, power backup and accessible courtrooms.
- **Case Information System (CIS):** records case registration, parties, events, orders, hearing dates and case status. Data quality depends on accurate entry and maintenance.
- **e-Filing:** electronic submission of pleadings and documents where enabled by the relevant court rules or portal. Filing does not guarantee acceptance; registry scrutiny, court fees, signatures, annexures and formatting requirements still apply.
- **e-Payments and e-Service:** digital payment and electronic transmission of court processes where permitted. Verify valid service and proof of delivery under applicable rules.
- **Virtual hearings:** video-conferencing systems enable remote participation. The court controls whether a matter is heard remotely and how identity, confidentiality, recording and document sharing are managed.
- **Digital records and evidence:** electronic records are governed by applicable evidence and procedure law. For proceedings governed by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, consult its provisions on electronic and digital records; legacy materials may refer to the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. Do not rely on outdated section numbering.
- **Public services:** eCourts services and the National Judicial Data Grid provide case information and aggregate data. Public case data should be interpreted carefully; pendency counts alone do not measure case complexity, fairness or performance.

## 3. Typical e-filing workflow

1. Select the correct court, case category and filing type on the official portal.
2. Prepare pleadings, annexures, index, vakalatnama and affidavits according to current local rules; ensure legibility, pagination and file-size limits.
3. Upload documents and complete party, advocate and case metadata accurately.
4. Pay court fees or other charges through an authorised method and retain acknowledgements.
5. Monitor registry scrutiny; correct defects within the given time and retain the resubmission record.
6. After registration, use the portal for case status, orders and listings; confirm service requirements independently.

This is a learning outline, not a universal procedural checklist. Local rules govern.

## 4. Benefits and limits

### Potential benefits

Digital filing and records can reduce travel and paper handling, make case information available beyond the courthouse, support scheduling and audit trails, and allow remote participation where appropriate. Digitisation can improve retrieval and continuity when records are complete and systems are maintained.

### Risks and constraints

Uneven internet access, devices, language support and digital literacy can exclude litigants. Connectivity failures and poor audio/video can impair participation. Scanned or poorly indexed files may remain difficult to search. Cybersecurity, confidentiality, data minimisation, retention, accessibility, identity verification and unequal access to counsel require safeguards. A virtual hearing should preserve meaningful opportunity to be heard, confidential lawyer-client communication, reliable access to the record and an accurate record of proceedings.

Technology may reduce administrative friction, but it cannot alone resolve structural causes of delay such as vacancies, adjournments, complex procedure or inadequate capacity. Avoid claiming that e-Courts have eliminated backlog; measure effects with current, transparent data and context.

## 5. Digital ethics for legal professionals

Advocates should protect client confidentiality when using shared devices or remote platforms; verify recipients before sharing documents; use secure channels and strong authentication; avoid recording or circulating hearing material unless permitted; disclose technical barriers promptly; and ensure clients understand the technology and its risks. Do not upload privileged or sensitive material to unapproved public AI services. Check citations and primary sources independently when using digital research or AI-assisted tools. The advocate remains responsible for accuracy, candour and compliance with court directions.

## 6. Artificial intelligence and blockchain

AI tools may assist with transcription, translation, document classification, search, scheduling and summarisation. Their output can be incomplete, biased, outdated or fabricated. Human review, source verification, privacy protection, explainability where relevant and clear accountability are essential. AI output should not be treated as legal authority or a substitute for judicial reasoning. Any court-specific rules or advisories must be checked before use.

Blockchain is sometimes proposed for tamper-evident logs or document provenance. It does not by itself establish that the input was true, protect every endpoint, guarantee confidentiality or cure poor governance. The syllabus presents these as technologies to study, not as proof that Indian courts have adopted blockchain for adjudication.

## 7. Comparative perspective

Compare systems by asking: what is digitised; who can access it; what procedural safeguards exist; how records are authenticated and preserved; how remote hearings accommodate vulnerable users; what privacy and cybersecurity controls apply; and how users can challenge errors. Avoid treating any country’s model as directly transferable without considering its laws, resources, language and court structure.

## 8. Exam answer structure

Define e-Courts; briefly describe India’s phased project; explain infrastructure and services; discuss e-filing and virtual hearing processes; analyse access, efficiency and transparency benefits alongside digital divide, fairness and security risks; evaluate AI/blockchain cautiously; conclude that technology must support due process and accountable human decision-making.
