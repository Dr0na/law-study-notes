# Module 2: Norms of Professional Ethics and Duties

## 1. Why professional ethics matter

Advocacy is a public profession that affects liberty, property and access to justice. Ethical rules protect clients, preserve independent courts, promote trust in legal services and make adversarial proceedings fair. Professional duties arise from the Advocates Act, BCI Rules, court rules, fiduciary principles and decisions of courts.

The supplied **P.E. unit_4** notes frame legal ethics as standards regulating a lawyer’s conduct toward self, client, opposing parties and the court. This is a useful organizing framework, but the binding duties come from the Advocates Act, applicable BCI Rules and court rules. The supplied **2024052015** lecture by Senior Advocate R. Venkataramani also highlights constitutional governance, public trust and the risk of commercialization; these themes help explain why professional independence and service obligations matter, but are commentary rather than statutory rules.

## 2. Duties to the court

Part VI, Chapter II, Section I of the BCI Rules requires advocates to maintain dignity and self-respect, respect courts, avoid private communications with judges about pending cases, resist unfair means, appear in prescribed dress, avoid conflicts where related to a judge, and not wear bands/gowns in public outside permitted occasions. Advocates must not mislead the court, knowingly make false statements, suppress binding authority or misuse process. A serious complaint against a judicial officer should be made through proper channels, not through intimidation or abuse.

## 3. Duties to clients

Section II of the BCI Rules sets out duties including:

- accept a brief only when competent and available to represent the client;
- fearlessly uphold lawful interests, while maintaining honesty and fairness;
- avoid withdrawal without sufficient cause and reasonable notice; refund unearned fees when withdrawing;
- disclose conflicts and not act where personal interests or duties conflict;
- maintain client confidentiality, subject to law;
- keep proper accounts of client money and communicate relevant information;
- not stipulate for a fee contingent on the result or share the proceeds of litigation;
- not purchase or traffic in actionable claims;
- not abuse or take unfair advantage of the client's confidence;
- avoid acting as surety for a client or becoming personally interested in the outcome, subject to the rules.

Confidentiality also intersects with sections 126–129 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 for legacy matters and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 for proceedings governed by the new evidence law. The statutory privilege and professional ethical duty must be analysed under the applicable law and exceptions.

## 4. Duties to opponents and colleagues

Advocates should communicate with represented parties through their advocates, honour undertakings, avoid sharp practice, seek consent or court permission when replacing another advocate on a filed vakalatnama, and avoid solicitation or touting. Courtesy does not require compromising the client's lawful interests.

## 5. Duties to the public and society

Advocates should support access to justice, legal aid and the rule of law; avoid discrimination and exploitative conduct; and use professional skill in the public interest where appropriate. The constitutional right to legal representation is supported by Article 22(1), procedural law and fair-trial principles. *A.S. Mohammed Rafi v. State of Tamil Nadu* holds that collective Bar resolutions refusing representation to a class of accused are unlawful and unethical.

## 6. Duty to self and professional development

Competence, diligence, honesty, preparation, sound judgment, courage, clear communication, emotional restraint and continuing learning are essential. Advocates should maintain independence, avoid conflicts, protect client data, and use technology responsibly. A lawyer's duty is not to guarantee outcomes but to provide competent service and candid advice.

## 7. Training and legal aid

The Advocates Act and BCI Rules provide a framework for legal education, training and professional standards. Legal aid is supported by Article 39A and the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. *Hussainara Khatoon (IV) v. State of Bihar*, (1980) 1 SCC 98 recognised free legal services as part of fair procedure for an indigent accused; *Khatri (II) v. State of Bihar*, (1981) 1 SCC 627 clarified the State's duty to provide legal aid from the first production before a magistrate, where necessary.

## 8. Restrictions on other employment

Rule 49 of the BCI Rules generally prohibits a full-time salaried employee from continuing practice as an advocate. The advocate must intimate the State Bar Council and cease practice while in such employment, subject to the rule's wording and applicable exceptions. Other occupations and retainers must be assessed under current BCI rules, court rules and statutory directions; avoid broad claims that every outside activity is automatically prohibited.

## 9. Advertising and solicitation

Rule 36 prohibits advocates from soliciting work or advertising directly or indirectly, subject to the limited website information permitted under the rule and BCI specifications. It restricts promotional claims, touting, interviews, circulars and publicity connected with cases. This is a professional conduct rule; it should be read in its current form, including valid amendments. Legal information or public education must not be confused with client solicitation.

## 10. Robes and dress

Rule 5 of Chapter IV, Part VI of the BCI Rules prescribes professional dress for appearance in courts, subject to court rules and seasonal directions. Rule 8 of Chapter II restricts wearing bands and gowns in public except on permitted ceremonial occasions and places. High Court rules and directions may add local requirements.

## 11. Vakalatnama

A vakalatnama is the written authority by which a party appoints an advocate to act and plead in a proceeding, subject to procedural rules. The advocate should verify the client's authority, scope, signature/verification requirements, fee arrangements and any limitations on authority to compromise. A vakalatnama does not necessarily authorise settlement without the client's instructions. *Himalayan Cooperative Group Housing Society v. Balwan Singh*, (2015) 7 SCC 373 emphasises that counsel cannot surrender substantive rights or make concessions beyond actual authority. Forged or unauthorised vakalatnamas and fictitious compromises constitute grave misconduct: *Narain Pandey v. Pannalal Pandey*, (2013) 11 SCC 435.
