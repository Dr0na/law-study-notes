# Module 7 — The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971

> **Scope note:** These notes summarize the constitutional and statutory framework for study. Contempt jurisdiction is exceptional and fact-sensitive. Read the Act and the prescribed judgments before forming a conclusion about any particular publication or conduct.

## 1. Constitutional and historical setting

The Supreme Court and High Courts are courts of record under Articles 129 and 215 of the Constitution and have power to punish for contempt of themselves. Parliament may define and regulate contempt powers under Article 142(2) for the Supreme Court and Article 225 (subject to constitutional provisions) for High Courts. The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 consolidates and amends the law; it does not replace the constitutional status of the superior courts. The Act defines contempt, limits and regulates its exercise, and provides procedural safeguards and appeals.

The law must be read alongside Article 19(1)(a), which protects speech, and Article 19(2), which permits reasonable restrictions including in relation to contempt of court. Contempt is not a general power to suppress criticism. The purpose is to protect the administration of justice and public confidence in courts, not the personal sensibilities of judges.

## 2. Definitions and classification

Section 2(a) defines “contempt of court” as civil or criminal contempt. Section 2(b) defines **civil contempt** as wilful disobedience to a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. The elements include a clear obligation, knowledge of it, ability to comply, and wilful failure; mere inability or an honest, non-wilful failure is not automatically contempt.

Section 2(c) defines **criminal contempt** as publication (whether spoken, written, by signs, visible representation or otherwise), or any other act, which:

1. scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower, the authority of any court;
2. prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or
3. interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.

The statutory words “tends to” do not remove the requirement to assess the act in context and its real tendency to interfere with justice. Strong, fair criticism of a judgment is not automatically contempt.

## 3. Statutory safeguards and defences

- **Innocent publication and distribution — sections 3 and 4:** protection is available in the circumstances specified by the Act, including lack of reasonable grounds to believe a proceeding was pending (section 3) and fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings (section 4).
- **Fair criticism — section 5:** fair comment on the merits of a case which has been heard and finally decided is not contempt.
- **Complaint about a presiding officer — section 6:** a good-faith complaint concerning a subordinate court judge, made to another subordinate court or the High Court, is protected within the statutory conditions.
- **Subordinate courts — section 10:** a High Court has the same jurisdiction to punish contempt of courts subordinate to it, subject to the Act. It cannot take cognizance where the alleged contempt is an offence punishable under the Indian Penal Code (as the provision then named it); consult current consolidated text and applicable criminal-law transition provisions for present procedure.
- **Truth — section 13(b):** truth may be permitted as a defence if the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and the request to invoke it is bona fide. It is not an automatic defence.
- **No substantial interference — section 13(a):** no sentence may be imposed unless the contempt substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice.
- **Apology — section 12:** the court may discharge the contemnor or remit punishment on a genuine apology. An apology is not a guaranteed defence; sincerity, timing and conduct matter.
- **Good-faith disclosure and limited protections:** sections 7–9 address in-camera proceedings, acts of judicial officers and other matters; read their exact conditions rather than assuming blanket immunity.

## 4. Punishment and limitation

Under section 12(1), punishment may be simple imprisonment for up to six months, a fine up to ₹2,000, or both. The court may discharge the person or remit punishment on apology under section 12(1). A corporation and persons responsible for its conduct may be dealt with under section 12(4)–(5), subject to those provisions.

Section 20 bars initiation of proceedings after one year from the date on which the contempt is alleged to have been committed. The Supreme Court has interpreted “initiation” in its case law; do not treat the provision as a simple rule that every later court step is barred.

## 5. Initiation and procedure

### Supreme Court and High Court

For contempt in the face of the Supreme Court or a High Court, section 14 provides a summary procedure with safeguards: the alleged contemnor must be informed of the charge, given an opportunity to make a defence, and the matter is decided by the court. For other criminal contempt, section 15 allows action on the court’s own motion, on a motion by the Advocate-General, or on a motion by another person with the Advocate-General’s written consent (with a distinct procedure for a subordinate court’s reference). Section 17 requires notice and specifies service and response procedures. Section 18 provides that a case under section 15 is heard by a bench of at least two judges, subject to the statutory exception for a smaller High Court.

### Subordinate courts

Under section 15(2), a subordinate court may make a reference to the High Court. Section 10 gives the High Court jurisdiction. The Code of Civil Procedure’s Order XXXIX rule 2A is a separate mechanism addressing disobedience or breach of an injunction or other order under Order XXXIX rules 1 or 2. It authorizes specified consequences within that civil-procedure framework; it is not identical to criminal contempt under the 1971 Act. The same conduct can raise distinct legal questions, but the elements and procedure must not be conflated.

## 6. Appeals and remedies

Section 19 provides an appeal as of right from an order or decision of a High Court exercising jurisdiction to punish for contempt: to a bench of at least two judges of that High Court if the order is of a single judge, or to the Supreme Court if the order is of a bench. The Act sets short appeal periods (30 days and 60 days respectively) and allows extension for sufficient cause. Section 19(2) addresses suspension of execution pending appeal; sections 19(3)–(4) provide for bail and hearing. Not every interlocutory order in contempt proceedings is appealable under section 19; the statutory conditions and case law matter. The Act does not create a general appeal from every contempt-related observation.

## 7. Contempt by different actors

The Act applies to persons and bodies within its terms; section 12(4)–(5) addresses corporations. Judges and magistrates acting judicially are subject to the specific protection in section 16, which is not a licence for misconduct. Lawyers may face contempt for conduct obstructing justice and separate professional discipline under the Advocates Act, 1961. These are distinct jurisdictions: contempt protects the administration of justice, while professional discipline addresses fitness and professional standards.

## 8. Important principles from prescribed cases

- *In re S. Mulgaokar*, (1978) 3 SCC 339: use contempt power with restraint; judges should ordinarily ignore harmless or ill-informed criticism and act where intervention is necessary to protect justice.
- *L.D. Jaikwal v State of U.P.*, (1984) 3 SCC 405: a calculated, scurrilous attack on a judge cannot invariably be neutralised by a casual apology.
- *In re Arundhati Roy*, (2002) 3 SCC 343: the Court considered a public statement and affidavit in the context of a pending contempt proceeding and imposed a one-day symbolic imprisonment with a fine; the decision is to be read on its facts and statutory setting.
- *Dr. Subramanian Swamy v Arun Shourie*, (2014) 12 SCC 344: section 13(b) permits truth as a defence only where the court is satisfied as to public interest and bona fides.
- *Bal Kishan Giri v State of U.P.*, (2014) 7 SCC 280: the Court addressed an attack on a judicial officer and the adequacy of apology; a genuine apology must reflect remorse and not be a device to escape consequences.
- *In re Hon’ble Justice C.S. Karnan*, (2017) 7 SCC 1: the Supreme Court exercised contempt jurisdiction over a sitting High Court judge and imposed six months’ imprisonment, illustrating the exceptional constitutional and institutional context.
- *Re: Prashant Bhushan*, Suo Motu Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 1 of 2020, judgment 14 August 2020; sentencing order 31 August 2020: the Court found criminal contempt in two tweets and imposed a fine of ₹1, with default imprisonment and temporary suspension from practice in default. The decision remains controversial; state the holding accurately and distinguish legal result from criticism of the judgment.

## 9. Exam answer structure

Define contempt under section 2; distinguish civil and criminal contempt; state the relevant constitutional source; apply the relevant statutory defence or safeguard; explain initiation, burden and procedure; address punishment, apology and appeal; then use a case on the closest principle. Finish by balancing protection of justice with free speech and fair criticism.
