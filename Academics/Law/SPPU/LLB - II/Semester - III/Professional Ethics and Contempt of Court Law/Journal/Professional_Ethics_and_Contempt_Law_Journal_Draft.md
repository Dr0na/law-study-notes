# Part A — Cases on Professional Ethics

## A.1 Lt. Col. S.J. Chaudhary v. State (Delhi Administration)

**Citation:** *Lt. Col. S.J. Chaudhary v. State (Delhi Administration)*, (1984) 1 SCC 722; AIR 1984 SC 618.  
**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Decision:** 9 February 1984  
**Bench:** A.P. Sen and R.B. Misra, JJ.

### Facts of the Case

The matter arose from a criminal trial in which the prosecution had undertaken to produce witnesses on scheduled dates. The trial was repeatedly disrupted because counsel for the accused did not attend, leading to adjournments and delay in examining witnesses. The accused’s right to a fair trial was important, but the Court also had to consider the public interest in an orderly and reasonably prompt criminal process.

### Issues of Facts and Law

Whether an advocate who accepts a brief may absent himself from trial in a manner that prevents the case from proceeding, and what duty the advocate owes to the court and the client once engaged.

### Arguments Advanced

The concern on behalf of the accused was that representation and a fair opportunity to defend must be preserved. The prosecution and the court process required the trial to proceed without repeated avoidable adjournments. The reported judgment is chiefly remembered for the Court’s statement of counsel’s duty after accepting a brief; it does not set out extended adversarial submissions on this ethical point.

### Decision of the Court

The Supreme Court stressed that a lawyer who accepts a brief is bound to attend the hearing and conduct the matter with due diligence. An advocate’s professional obligation is not exhausted by accepting fees or filing an appearance. Unjustified absence and repeated adjournments obstruct the administration of justice and may prejudice the client as well as the public. The Court directed that the trial proceed without avoidable delay and emphasized the responsibility of counsel to cooperate with the court.

### Ratio of the Case

An advocate must honour an accepted engagement, attend court, and assist in the timely administration of justice. A lawyer cannot treat the court’s schedule or the client’s case casually. Legitimate reasons for absence should be communicated and handled responsibly; a pattern of avoidable non-attendance is inconsistent with professional duty.

### Comments on the Decision

This decision illustrates the advocate’s dual responsibility: to represent the client competently and to act as an officer of the court. The duty to the client does not justify conduct that obstructs justice. The case is a practical reminder that diligence, punctuality, preparation, and respect for court time are essential aspects of professional competence.

## A.2 A.S. Mohammed Rafi v. State of Tamil Nadu

**Citation:** *A.S. Mohammed Rafi v. State of Tamil Nadu*, (2011) 1 SCC 688; AIR 2011 SC 308.  
**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Decision:** 6 December 2010  
**Bench:** Markandey Katju and Gyan Sudha Misra, JJ.

### Facts of the Case

Following a murder, the Bar Association at Coimbatore passed a resolution asking its members not to appear for the accused persons. The appellant, an advocate, challenged the resolution. The dispute raised the question whether a collective refusal by advocates to defend an accused could be justified on the ground that the alleged crime was grave or unpopular.

### Issues of Facts and Law

1. Whether a Bar Association may direct its members not to represent a particular accused.
2. Whether an advocate may refuse a brief solely because the accused is unpopular or charged with a serious offence.
3. How the accused’s right to legal representation relates to an advocate’s professional independence.

### Arguments Advanced

The appellant challenged the Bar Association resolution as inconsistent with the accused’s right to a fair trial and access to counsel. The position supporting the resolution was that members should not appear for persons accused of a grave crime, reflecting the association’s collective response. The Court considered whether collective pressure could lawfully prevent representation, while recognizing that an individual advocate may decline a brief for a proper reason.

### Decision of the Court

The Supreme Court held that the Bar Association’s resolution was illegal and contrary to the rule of law. Every accused person is entitled to a fair trial and a meaningful opportunity to defend themselves. A lawyer is not required to accept every brief, but a collective boycott preventing legal representation undermines access to justice. The Court distinguished an individual advocate’s freedom to decline a brief for a genuine personal or professional reason from an organized resolution intended to deny representation to a class of accused persons.

The Court observed that an advocate defending an accused does not thereby endorse the alleged crime. The advocate’s role is to ensure that the prosecution proves its case according to law and that due process is observed. Where no lawyer is willing to appear, the court may take steps to secure representation, including asking an advocate to assist in the interests of justice.

### Ratio of the Case

Bar Associations cannot impose collective boycotts that deny an accused legal representation. The right to defend oneself through counsel is integral to a fair process. An advocate may exercise individual professional choice, but must not participate in conduct that frustrates justice or deprives a person of a fair trial.

### Comments on the Decision

The case clarifies that professional ethics include independence from public pressure, respect for the presumption of innocence, and commitment to access to justice. Representation is a professional service, not approval of a client’s conduct. At the same time, an advocate’s duties of candour, competence, and fairness remain fully applicable.

# Part B — Cases on Contempt of Court

## B.1 P.D. Khandekar v. Bar Council of Maharashtra and Others

**Full name:** *Pandurang Dattatraya Khandekar v. Bar Council of Maharashtra, Bombay and Others*  
**Citation:** (1984) 2 SCC 556; AIR 1984 SC 110; 1984 SCR (1) 414.  
**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Decision:** 20 October 1983  
**Bench:** A.P. Sen, E.S. Venkataramiah and R.B. Misra, JJ.

### Facts of the Case

Disciplinary proceedings were initiated against the appellant advocate under section 35 of the Advocates Act, 1961. The allegations concerned the manner in which legal advice and professional services had been given in transactions involving clients. The State Bar Council disciplinary committee and the Bar Council of India considered the evidence and found professional misconduct. The advocate appealed to the Supreme Court under section 38 of the Act.

### Issues of Facts and Law

1. What conduct constitutes “professional or other misconduct” under section 35 of the Advocates Act?
2. Does every wrong or negligent legal opinion amount to professional misconduct?
3. What standard should be applied when reviewing a disciplinary finding?

### Arguments Advanced

The advocate challenged the disciplinary findings and sought appellate interference under section 38 of the Advocates Act, 1961. The disciplinary authorities’ position, reflected in their findings, was that the proved conduct crossed the threshold of professional misconduct under section 35. The Supreme Court had to examine the meaning and proof of misconduct while respecting the role of the disciplinary bodies.

### Decision of the Court

The Supreme Court explained that professional misconduct is not confined to a closed list. Conduct may amount to misconduct when it would reasonably be regarded by members of the profession of good repute and competence as disgraceful or dishonourable. However, disciplinary guilt must be established by clear evidence. Mere suspicion, an error of judgment, or simple negligence without moral delinquency is not automatically professional misconduct. The Court distinguished an improper legal opinion, given in breach of professional obligations, from a legal opinion that is simply wrong.

The Court also underscored the advocate’s duty to act with utmost good faith and to give sincere, dispassionate advice in the client’s interests. A lawyer must not exploit the client’s trust or act in a manner that diminishes public confidence in the profession.

### Ratio of the Case

Professional misconduct is not limited to enumerated acts, but must be clearly established by evidence and judged by standards of honourable and competent members of the profession. A mere error of judgment or negligence without moral delinquency does not automatically constitute misconduct. Improper advice involving breach of professional duty is distinguishable from advice that is simply wrong.

### Comments on the Decision

This is principally a **professional misconduct** decision under the Advocates Act, not a contempt conviction. It is included in this part because it concerns accountability of advocates and is often studied alongside the advocate’s obligations to courts and the legal system. Contempt jurisdiction and Bar Council discipline are distinct: contempt protects the administration of justice, while disciplinary proceedings regulate professional conduct.

## B.2 Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India

**Citation:** *Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India*, (1988) 3 SCC 255; AIR 1988 SC 107; 1988 SCR (1) 441.  
**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Decision:** 9 October 1987  
**Bench:** Ranganath Misra and K.N. Singh, JJ.

### Facts of the Case

The petitioner, an advocate appearing in person, filed a petition under Article 32 of the Constitution in the form of public interest litigation. The petition used intemperate language and made sweeping allegations against the Supreme Court and the functioning of the judiciary, including allegations that were framed in a manner capable of lowering public confidence in the Court. The petition purported to raise constitutional concerns but its language and presentation became central to the Court’s consideration.

### Issues of Facts and Law

1. Whether pleadings in a constitutional petition may cross the line from legitimate criticism into contempt.
2. Whether the language used amounted prima facie to an attempt to scandalise or lower the authority of the Court.
3. Whether the petitioner’s conduct was compatible with responsible public interest litigation.

### Arguments Advanced

The petitioner presented the filing as a public interest constitutional challenge and advanced allegations about the functioning of the Supreme Court and government. The Court assessed the petition’s language and its apparent effect on public confidence in the judiciary. The reported order does not set out a conventional adversarial argument on a separate contempt charge; it directed that contempt process be drawn up and notice issued.

### Decision of the Court

The Supreme Court dismissed the petition and held that its allegations, read as a whole, appeared calculated to denigrate the Court in the estimation of the public. The Court considered the language unsavoury and beyond the restraint expected in public interest proceedings. It directed the Registry to draw up appropriate contempt proceedings and issue notice to the petitioner. The order at this stage treated the petitioner as **prima facie** guilty and initiated process; it should not be inaccurately described as a final sentence of imprisonment or fine in that judgment.

The Court also directed that further PIL applications by the petitioner should not be entertained. The decision demonstrates the Court’s concern that the PIL jurisdiction be used responsibly and not as a vehicle for personal attacks on institutions.

### Ratio of the Case

Fair and reasoned criticism of judicial decisions is distinct from contempt. Pleadings that use scandalous or abusive allegations with an apparent tendency to lower the authority of the court may attract contempt jurisdiction. The context, language, intention, and likely effect on the administration of justice must be considered; the contempt process must still follow due procedure.

### Comments on the Decision

The case is relevant to criminal contempt under section 2(c) of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, particularly the branch concerning scandalising or lowering the authority of a court. It also illustrates the importance of restraint and accuracy in legal pleadings. Section 13 requires that punishment not be imposed unless the contempt substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice; truth may be permitted as a defence when the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and invoked bona fide.

# Part C — Essay: Use of AI in Judiciary

## Definition

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that ordinarily require human reasoning, such as searching, classification, translation, prediction, and generating text. In the judiciary, AI should be understood as a decision-support technology. It can assist court administration and legal research, but judicial authority and responsibility remain with judges and the law.

## Introduction

Indian courts face a heavy volume of cases, large documentary records, language diversity, and unequal access to legal services. Digital tools can help courts process information and make services easier to access. AI can support transcription, translation, document management, research, and scheduling. Its use must remain consistent with constitutional values, open justice, fair hearing, privacy, and judicial independence.

## Uses of AI in the judicial system (6)

1. **Translation and language access:** Machine-assisted translation can help litigants and lawyers understand orders and materials across Indian languages. Human review is necessary for legal nuance and authoritative versions.
2. **Speech-to-text and transcription:** Automated transcription can create searchable records of hearings and reduce clerical burden, subject to accuracy checks and recording rules.
3. **Case management:** Algorithms can identify filing defects, classify matters, flag deadlines, and assist with listing and workload planning. They should not determine substantive priority without transparent judicial oversight.
4. **Legal research:** Search and summarisation tools can locate statutes and precedents. Every citation and proposition must be checked against an authoritative source because generative systems may produce fabricated or outdated material.
5. **Document review:** AI can help organise voluminous evidence, identify duplicate documents, and extract dates or names. It cannot replace judicial assessment of admissibility, credibility, or weight.
6. **Public information and assistance:** Carefully bounded chat interfaces may explain filing steps, court locations, and procedural information. They must clearly distinguish general information from legal advice and provide accessible alternatives.

## Potential benefits (5)

- **Efficiency:** Automation of repetitive administrative work may free judicial and registry time for substantive matters.
- **Access to justice:** Translation and simplified interfaces may reduce language and procedural barriers.
- **Consistency of administration:** Structured tools can help identify missing documents and routine compliance issues.
- **Searchability:** Digitised and indexed records can improve retrieval of relevant material.
- **Better planning:** Aggregate, privacy-protected data may help courts understand delays and allocate resources.

## Risks and safeguards (7)

1. **Bias and unequal outcomes:** Training data may reproduce social or historical bias. Systems should be tested across affected groups and never treated as neutral merely because they are automated.
2. **Opacity:** Parties should be able to understand when an AI system materially affects a process and challenge consequential errors. Reasons for judicial decisions must remain intelligible.
3. **Hallucinated or incorrect outputs:** Legal research outputs must be verified from primary sources. A judge or advocate remains accountable for material presented to the court.
4. **Privacy and confidentiality:** Sensitive pleadings, evidence, and personal data should not be entered into unapproved public systems. Access controls, data minimisation, and retention limits are essential.
5. **Due process:** No person should be denied a hearing, bail, liberty, or a remedy solely through an unreviewable automated score or recommendation.
6. **Cybersecurity:** Court systems require protection against unauthorised access, data alteration, and service disruption.
7. **Digital exclusion:** Online-first processes must preserve assisted and offline routes for people without reliable devices, connectivity, literacy, or disability access.

## Constitutional and legal framework

The use of AI must respect equality before law and equal protection under **Article 14**, access to justice and fair procedure under **Article 21**, and the open administration of justice. Judicial independence and the duty to give reasoned decisions cannot be delegated to a software vendor. Privacy interests recognised in *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) require careful handling of personal data. The Information Technology Act, 2000 and applicable data-protection law may also be relevant, depending on the system and data involved. Technology does not change the rules of evidence or relieve parties of their legal burdens.

In India, the Supreme Court has deployed technology initiatives such as the e-Courts ecosystem and language tools including SUVAS (Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software). These illustrate assistance and access applications. They do not make an algorithm the adjudicator. Any use should be guided by published policy, accuracy evaluation, auditability, human supervision, and a process to correct mistakes.

## Important case law (3)

### *State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praful B. Desai* (2003) 4 SCC 601

The Supreme Court accepted evidence by video conferencing in appropriate circumstances. The case shows that technology can facilitate procedure when legal safeguards and fairness are maintained.

### *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer* (2014) 10 SCC 473

The Court addressed the statutory requirements for admissibility of electronic records. Digital evidence must satisfy legal rules; technological convenience does not remove evidentiary safeguards.

### *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1

The nine-judge bench recognised privacy as a fundamental right. Judicial technology handling personal information must therefore be designed and used with appropriate necessity, proportionality, and safeguards.

## Recommendations (6)

1. Use AI first for administrative support and information access, not autonomous adjudication.
2. Require human verification of translations, transcripts, citations, and summaries that may affect rights.
3. Publish governance rules covering approved tools, data handling, audit, and responsibility for errors.
4. Conduct independent testing for accuracy, bias, security, and accessibility before deployment and periodically thereafter.
5. Give parties notice and a meaningful way to question material AI-assisted outputs.
6. Train judges, registry staff, lawyers, and students in responsible use and verification.

## Conclusion

AI can make courts more accessible and improve routine administration when it is used carefully. It must remain subordinate to law, judicial reasoning, and human accountability. A fair AI-enabled judiciary requires transparency, privacy, accuracy, inclusive access, and a final decision made by an independent human judge.

# Part D — Essay: Punishment for Contempt of Court

## Definition

Under section 2(a) of the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971**, “contempt of court” means civil contempt or criminal contempt. Civil contempt under section 2(b) is wilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ, or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. Criminal contempt under section 2(c) includes publication or conduct that scandalises or tends to scandalise a court, prejudices or interferes with judicial proceedings, or otherwise obstructs or tends to obstruct the administration of justice.

## Introduction

Contempt jurisdiction protects the administration of justice and the authority necessary for courts to perform their constitutional role. It is not intended to shield judges from fair criticism or to punish mere disagreement. The law balances institutional protection with freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a), subject to the reasonable restriction in Article 19(2). Punishment is governed principally by sections 12 and 13 of the 1971 Act, procedural provisions in sections 14 and 15, and appellate provisions in section 19.

## Statutory punishments under section 12 (3)

1. **Simple imprisonment:** A person found guilty may be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months.
2. **Fine:** The court may impose a fine which may extend to two thousand rupees.
3. **Either or both:** The court may impose imprisonment, fine, or both, subject to the Act and the facts of the case.

The court may discharge the accused or remit punishment if a sincere apology is made to its satisfaction. An apology is not an automatic defence; it must be bona fide and not merely a device to escape liability. In civil contempt, where a fine would not meet the ends of justice and imprisonment is necessary, the Act permits detention in a civil prison for a period not exceeding six months. A company’s liability and responsibility of persons in charge are addressed by section 12(4) and (5).

## Important statutory safeguards (6)

1. **Substantial interference — section 13:** No sentence may be imposed unless the contempt substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice. Truth may be allowed as a defence where the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and invoked bona fide.
2. **Fair hearing — section 14:** In contempt in the face of the Supreme Court or a High Court, the person must receive notice of the charge and a reasonable opportunity to defend themselves, in accordance with the statutory procedure.
3. **Consent for criminal contempt — section 15:** Certain criminal contempt proceedings initiated on a motion require the written consent of the Advocate-General or other law officer specified by the Act. This screening mechanism helps prevent vexatious proceedings.
4. **Limitation — section 20:** Proceedings for contempt cannot be initiated after one year from the date on which the contempt is alleged to have been committed.
5. **Appeal — section 19:** A person aggrieved by an order or decision punishing for contempt may appeal as provided by the Act.
6. **Defences and exclusions:** Sections 3 to 7 address innocent publication, fair and accurate reporting, fair criticism of a judicial act, good-faith complaints against presiding officers, and certain protected in-camera matters.

## Principles guiding sentence (5)

- **Proportionality:** The punishment should match the gravity, intent, impact, and circumstances of the conduct.
- **Necessity:** Courts should use contempt powers only where needed to protect justice, not for personal vindication.
- **Opportunity to purge:** In civil contempt, compliance may remedy the disobedience; the court assesses whether it was wilful.
- **Bona fide apology:** A genuine apology may mitigate or lead to discharge, but should not be coerced or treated as a ritual formula.
- **Fair process:** The alleged contemnor must know the accusation and have a meaningful opportunity to answer it.

## Case law (5)

### *Brahma Prakash Sharma v. State of Uttar Pradesh*, AIR 1954 SC 10

The Supreme Court explained that contempt jurisdiction concerns the administration of justice and not the personal dignity of a judge. The tendency and effect of the alleged act on public confidence and justice must be assessed.

### *E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.N. Nambiar*, (1970) 2 SCC 325

The Court considered statements about the judiciary and held that criticism may attract contempt when it tends to lower the authority of courts or obstruct justice. The case must be read with later emphasis on free expression and statutory safeguards.

### *In Re: S. Mulgaokar*, (1978) 3 SCC 339

The Supreme Court urged restraint in exercising contempt power and recognised the importance of free criticism. The power should be used with great care and only where the public interest in protecting justice requires it.

### *P.D. Khandekar v. Bar Council of Maharashtra*, (1984) 2 SCC 556

This is a professional disciplinary case under the Advocates Act, not a contempt sentence. It is relevant by contrast: Bar Council discipline and court contempt are separate accountability mechanisms, with different purposes and procedures.

### *Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India*, (1988) 3 SCC 255

The Court found that the petition contained language prima facie intended to denigrate the Supreme Court and directed contempt proceedings. The judgment illustrates that the court may initiate process for scandalising allegations, while the statutory threshold and procedural protections remain applicable.

## Critical analysis

Contempt punishment serves a legitimate purpose where disobedience defeats court orders or conduct seriously obstructs proceedings. However, broad use of the “scandalising” branch can chill robust public discussion. The statutory threshold in section 13, fair criticism protections, limitation, screening, and appeal are therefore important. Courts should identify the specific interference with justice, distinguish criticism from obstruction, and give reasons for the chosen sanction. In civil contempt, the focus should be on wilful disobedience and securing compliance. In criminal contempt, the court should protect proceedings and public confidence without suppressing fair comment.

## Conclusion

The Act authorises simple imprisonment up to six months, fine up to two thousand rupees, or both. These powers are constrained by statutory safeguards and constitutional freedoms. The objective is preservation of justice through proportionate, reasoned, and fair action—not punishment of criticism as such.
