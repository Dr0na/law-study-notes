# Prescribed Case Law Digest

This digest follows the cases listed in the SPPU syllabus. It gives short study propositions, not substitutes for the judgments. **Citation note:** the syllabus appears to contain several citation errors, identified below. Use the SCC citation where available and verify a report citation before reproducing it in an examination.

## A. Professional ethics cases (Module 6)

### 1. *Vishram Singh Raghubanshi v State of U.P.*

**Citation:** (2011) 7 SCC 776; AIR 2011 SC 2275.

**Principle:** An advocate’s abusive and intemperate conduct towards a judicial officer can amount to serious professional misconduct. The Supreme Court stressed the need to protect the dignity and independence of the judiciary at every level. Criticism or advocacy does not license personal abuse or intimidation.

**Use for:** duties to court; courtroom decorum; professional misconduct; dignity of subordinate judiciary.

### 2. *Noratanmal Chouraria v M.R. Murli*

**Citation:** (2004) 5 SCC 689; AIR 2004 SC 2440.

**Principle:** Professional misconduct under section 35 of the Advocates Act concerns conduct incompatible with the standards of the profession. The Court considered the particular facts and the connection between the conduct and professional role; a disciplinary finding is not established merely by labelling conduct objectionable. The disciplinary decision in the case was not displaced.

**Use for:** meaning and limits of “professional or other misconduct”; fact-specific disciplinary review.

### 3. *Lt. Col. S.J. Chaudhary v State (Delhi Administration)*

**Citation:** (1984) 1 SCC 722; AIR 1984 SC 618.

**Principle:** An advocate who accepts a criminal trial brief has a duty to attend the trial from day to day and conduct it diligently. Engagement cannot be treated as permission to delay trial through non-attendance.

**Use for:** duty to client and court; diligence; criminal trial advocacy.

### 4. *Chandra Shekhar Soni v Bar Council of Rajasthan*

**Citation:** (1983) 4 SCC 255; AIR 1983 SC 1012.

**Principle:** The Supreme Court considered disciplinary action for conduct involving a conflict in representation and the advocate’s professional obligations. The case is commonly used for the rule that an advocate must maintain fidelity to the client and must not act in a manner inconsistent with the client’s interest and confidence.

**Use for:** conflict of interest; loyalty; professional discipline.

### 5. *Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v Union of India*

**Citation:** (2003) 2 SCC 45; AIR 2003 SC 739.

**Principle:** Lawyers have no right to strike or boycott court work, including through token strikes. Such action obstructs litigants’ access to justice and the administration of justice. A court may take appropriate action where counsel abstain; only an exceptionally grave and rare situation involving the dignity, integrity or independence of the Bar or Bench may justify a protest, and even then courts should not be left without representation.

**Use for:** right to practise; Bar strikes; advocate’s duty to client and justice system.

### 6. *John D’Souza v Edward Ani*

**Citation:** (1994) 2 SCC 64; AIR 1994 SC 975.

**Principle:** The complaint alleged that an advocate retained a client’s mother-in-law’s will for safe custody and refused to return it despite requests. The Supreme Court examined the professional-misconduct finding and the evidence concerning entrustment and refusal. Use it for fiduciary responsibility and the proof required in disciplinary proceedings; avoid reducing it to an unrelated procedural rule.

**Use for:** advocate’s conduct and procedural responsibility; read with the judgment rather than relying on unsourced case summaries.

### 7. *Himalayan Cooperative Group Housing Society v Balwan Singh*

**Citation:** (2015) 7 SCC 373; AIR 2015 SC 2867.

**Principle:** An advocate has no implied authority to surrender a client’s substantive rights or make a concession affecting the merits without the client’s authority. A client should not be prejudiced by an unauthorised concession.

**Use for:** client instructions; authority of counsel; informed consent.

### 8. *Narain Pandey v Pannalal Pandey*

**Citation:** (2013) 11 SCC 435.

**Principle:** The Court upheld serious disciplinary consequences arising from fabrication and misuse of vakalatnamas and compromise documents. Misconduct affecting the integrity of judicial proceedings calls for a response proportionate to the breach and the need to protect public confidence.

**Use for:** vakalatnama; fraud on court; disciplinary powers and proportionality.

### 9. *A.S. Mohammed Rafi v State of Tamil Nadu*

**Citation:** (2011) 1 SCC 688; AIR 2011 SC 308.

**Principle:** A Bar Association resolution that advocates should refuse to represent accused persons is impermissible. The accused’s right to legal representation and an advocate’s professional freedom cannot be defeated by such collective pressure.

**Use for:** right to counsel; duty to represent unpopular clients; limits of Bar Association resolutions.

### 10. *D. Saibaba v Bar Council of India*

**Citation:** (2003) 6 SCC 186; AIR 2003 SC 2502.

**Principle:** In construing the 60-day period for review under section 48AA of the Advocates Act, the Court held that time runs from communication or knowledge of the order, not simply the date it was made. This ensures the review remedy is practical. The case also arose from disciplinary proceedings concerning an advocate’s employment outside practice.

**Use for:** section 48AA review; limitation; accessible disciplinary remedies.

## B. Contempt cases (Module 7)

### 1. *In re Arundhati Roy*

**Citation:** (2002) 3 SCC 343; AIR 2002 SC 1375.

**Principle:** The Court found criminal contempt in statements and an affidavit made in response to contempt proceedings and imposed a symbolic sentence of one day’s imprisonment and a fine. Study the judgment’s treatment of scandalising the court, the context of the statements and apology; do not generalise it into a rule that all sharp criticism is contempt.

**Use for:** criminal contempt; scandalising; apology; speech and judicial authority.

### 2. *Pandurang Dattatraya Khandekar v Bar Council of Maharashtra, Bombay*

**Citation:** (1984) 2 SCC 556; AIR 1984 SC 110.

**Syllabus placement note:** This is a professional disciplinary case under the Advocates Act, not a contempt conviction. The Supreme Court discussed the standard for professional misconduct, evidentiary sufficiency and an advocate’s duty of honest, disinterested treatment of clients. It is relevant to professional ethics and appears in Module 7’s list, but should not be represented as a contempt holding.

**Use for:** professional misconduct; quality of proof; client confidence; cross-reference to Module 6.

### 3. *L.D. Jaikwal v State of U.P.*

**Correct citation:** (1984) 3 SCC 405; AIR 1984 SC 1374.

**Syllabus citation correction:** The syllabus gives AIR 1984 SC 1734; that appears to be a typographical error. Use AIR 1984 SC 1374 or the SCC citation.

**Principle:** The Court rejected the idea that a casual or insincere apology necessarily erases a deliberate, scurrilous attack on a judicial officer. Apology is assessed for genuineness and timing.

**Use for:** apology; criminal contempt; attacks on judges.

### 4. *Charan Lal Sahu v Union of India*

**Citation:** (1988) 3 SCC 255; AIR 1988 SC 107. The order was delivered 9 October 1987.

**Principle:** The Court dismissed an advocate’s PIL, describing its allegations against the Court as an intentional attempt to lower the Court’s prestige, and directed the Registry to draw up contempt proceedings and issue a show-cause notice. This reported decision records initiation, not a final conviction or sentence in the contempt proceeding.

**Use for:** responsible pleadings; scandalous allegations; distinction between same-party proceedings.

### 5. *Bal Kishan Giri v State of U.P.*

**Citation:** (2014) 7 SCC 280.

**Syllabus citation note:** The syllabus’s “AIR 2014 SC (Supp) 469” is not the citation consistently used in reliable case references. Prefer the SCC citation unless the AIR citation is independently checked against the report.

**Principle:** The Court addressed contemptuous conduct directed at a judicial officer and whether the apology was genuine. A belated apology is not a matter of right and must demonstrate real remorse.

**Use for:** apology; contempt of subordinate judiciary; dignity of courts.

### 6. *High Court of Judicature of Bombay v Manisha Koirala*

**Citation:** 2003 Cri LJ 1634 (Bom HC) (also reported as 2003 BomCR (Cri) 1687).

**Principle:** The Bombay High Court considered efforts to bring outside influence to bear in a matter pending before it. The case is used for the proposition that attempting to influence a pending judicial proceeding can obstruct the administration of justice. Read the judgment for the exact circumstances and final disposition.

**Use for:** interference with pending proceedings; criminal contempt; influence and independence of adjudication.

### 7. *Dr. Subramanian Swamy v Arun Shourie*

**Citation:** (2014) 12 SCC 344; AIR 2014 SC 3020.

**Principle:** The Court interpreted section 13(b) of the Contempt of Courts Act: truth may be allowed as a defence when the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and the request to invoke the defence is bona fide. It is a qualified statutory defence.

**Use for:** truth defence; public interest; bona fides.

### 8. *In re S. Mulgaokar*

**Correct citation:** (1978) 3 SCC 339; AIR 1978 SC 727.

**Syllabus citation correction:** The syllabus says AIR 1978 SC 717; commonly reported citation is AIR 1978 SC 727.

**Principle:** The Court emphasised restraint in using contempt jurisdiction against criticism. Judges should not be unduly sensitive; intervention is warranted where the attack is sufficiently serious and public justice requires action. The opinions discuss differing approaches, so avoid presenting every passage as a single unanimous test.

**Use for:** judicial restraint; fair criticism; threshold for contempt.

### 9. *In re Hon’ble Justice C.S. Karnan*

**Citation:** (2017) 7 SCC 1; AIR 2017 SC 3191.

**Principle:** A seven-judge Supreme Court bench convicted a sitting High Court judge of contempt for conduct and communications that scandalised the judiciary and obstructed justice, sentencing him to six months’ imprisonment. The case is exceptional and raises distinct constitutional questions; cite its actual holding rather than extending it to ordinary criticism.

**Use for:** contempt by a judge; Article 129; institutional independence; exceptional procedure.

### 10. *Re: Prashant Bhushan and Anr.*

**Citation:** Suo Motu Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 1 of 2020; judgment dated 14 August 2020; sentencing order dated 31 August 2020.

**Principle and result:** The Supreme Court found criminal contempt in two tweets and imposed a fine of ₹1, with three months’ simple imprisonment and three months’ suspension from practice in default of payment. The default consequences were conditional on non-payment. Separate the holding from the substantial public debate about the judgment.

**Use for:** online speech; criminal contempt; criticism of the judiciary; apology, punishment and free speech.

## Citation and study cautions

1. Syllabus citation for *L.D. Jaikwal* should be corrected to AIR 1984 SC 1374 (or (1984) 3 SCC 405).
2. Syllabus citation for *In re S. Mulgaokar* should be corrected to AIR 1978 SC 727 (or (1978) 3 SCC 339).
3. *Pandurang Dattatraya Khandekar* is relevant to professional ethics, although the syllabus places it under contempt.
4. For *Bal Kishan Giri*, use the SCC citation unless the supplied AIR supplement citation has been verified from a reporter.
5. Distinguish the 1987 *Charan Lal Sahu* order initiating contempt proceedings from the later Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster Act merits litigation under the same party name.
6. Report citations are bibliographic aids; the judgment text controls. Use official or reporter copies for quotations and exact procedural outcomes.
