# Condensed Revision — Modules 1–8

Use this as a recall sheet after reading the complete notes. Section numbers refer to the Advocates Act, 1961 and Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 unless stated otherwise.

## Module 1 — History of the legal profession

- Ancient and medieval India had varied forms of dispute resolution and legal advisers; avoid projecting the modern regulated advocate profession unchanged into those periods.
- British rule introduced colonial courts, formal pleading and legal education; the Legal Practitioners Act, 1879 and Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926 were important steps before independence.
- The Advocates Act, 1961 created a unified class of advocates and a national/state Bar Council framework.
- Globalisation and technology expand cross-border work and research access, while raising questions of competence, confidentiality, conflicts and access.

## Module 2 — Ethics and duties

- Advocates are officers of the court and fiduciaries to clients, while owing duties to the profession and public.
- BCI Rules, Part VI, Chapter II: duties to court (Section I); client (Section II); opponent (Section III); colleagues (Section IV); restrictions in other employment (Section VII). Always consult the current consolidated rules.
- Advertising and solicitation are restricted by Rule 36, subject to the limited website information permitted by BCI.
- Dress is governed by the BCI Rules on dress and robes, read with applicable court and local directions.
- Vakalatnama is authority to act and plead subject to its terms, court rules and client instructions. It does not give counsel unlimited authority to compromise substantive rights.
- Legal aid is part of the profession’s public role; see constitutional Article 39A and applicable legal services legislation.

## Module 3 — Advocates Act authorities

- State Bar Council: establishment/composition ss. 3–4; functions s. 6; rules s. 15.
- Bar Council of India: composition s. 4; functions s. 7; rules s. 49; general provisions include corporate status (s. 5), committees (s. 9), and common provisions (ss. 10–15).
- The BCI sets professional conduct standards, legal education standards, and recognition functions, among others under s. 7.
- The Act provides for elected representation; check the current statutory composition and term provisions rather than relying on shorthand.

## Module 4 — Admission and enrolment

- State roll: ss. 17–22; transfer: s. 18; senior advocates: s. 16; pre-audience: s. 23.
- Enrolment qualifications and disqualifications: ss. 24 and 24A; disposal of applications: s. 25 onward; State Bar Council rules: s. 28.
- Senior designation is made by the Supreme Court or High Court under s. 16 and applicable judicial guidelines.
- Inter-State transfer is governed by s. 18; advocates are entered on one State roll at a time.

## Module 5 — Right to practise

- s. 29 recognises advocates as the one class entitled to practise law; s. 30 grants an advocate whose name is on a State roll the right to practise throughout India, subject to the Act and rules.
- s. 32 permits a court to allow a non-advocate to appear in a particular case; s. 33 generally reserves practice before courts and authorities to advocates; s. 34 enables High Court rules.
- There is no general right to strike/boycott court work: *Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v Union of India*, (2003) 2 SCC 45.
- Professional qualities: integrity, preparation, diligence, sound judgment, courage and respectful advocacy.

## Module 6 — Professional discipline

- s. 35: State Bar Council disciplinary action; s. 36: BCI disciplinary jurisdiction; s. 36B: transfer/time framework; s. 37: appeal to BCI; s. 38: appeal to Supreme Court; s. 40: stay; s. 44: review; s. 48AA: BCI review.
- Outcomes under s. 35(3): dismiss complaint, reprimand, suspend practice, or remove name from State roll.
- Key cases: *S.J. Chaudhary* (diligence); *Himalayan Cooperative* (authority to concede); *Narain Pandey* (fraudulent vakalatnamas); *Mohammed Rafi* (representation); *D. Saibaba* (review limitation).

## Module 7 — Contempt

- Constitutional sources: Arts. 129 and 215; speech protections and restriction: Art. 19(1)(a) and (2).
- Contempt Act s. 2(b): civil contempt = wilful disobedience/breach of undertaking. s. 2(c): criminal contempt = publication/act scandalising, prejudicing proceedings, or obstructing administration of justice.
- Defences/safeguards: innocent publication ss. 3–4; fair criticism s. 5; good-faith complaint s. 6; truth s. 13(b), if public interest and bona fide; substantial interference threshold s. 13(a).
- Procedure: ss. 14–18; appeal s. 19; limitation s. 20; punishment and apology s. 12.
- Order XXXIX r. 2A CPC is a separate civil injunction enforcement provision, not synonymous with contempt under the 1971 Act.
- Cases: *Mulgaokar* (restraint); *Arundhati Roy* and *Jaikwal* (limits/apology); *Swamy* (truth); *Karnan* and *Prashant Bhushan* (exceptional fact-specific applications).

## Module 8 — E-Courts

- E-Courts combines case information systems, e-filing, e-service, digital records, payments and video-conferencing.
- Project phases: computerisation and connectivity developed through Phases I and II; Phase III focuses on a more integrated, accessible digital ecosystem and digitised records. Check official current status before using statistics.
- Benefits: reduced travel/paper friction, access to records, scheduling and remote participation.
- Risks: digital divide, connectivity, accessibility, privacy, cyber-security, authentication, record integrity and fair hearing.
- AI may assist search, translation or administration; outputs require human verification and cannot replace judicial responsibility. Do not treat AI-generated citations as authorities.

## High-yield case citation corrections

- *L.D. Jaikwal*: AIR 1984 SC 1374, not 1734; (1984) 3 SCC 405.
- *In re S. Mulgaokar*: AIR 1978 SC 727, not 717; (1978) 3 SCC 339.
- *Pandurang Dattatraya Khandekar*: professional disciplinary case, notwithstanding its syllabus placement under contempt.
