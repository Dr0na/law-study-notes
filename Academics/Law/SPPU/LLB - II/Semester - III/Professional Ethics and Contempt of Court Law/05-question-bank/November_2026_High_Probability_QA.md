# Part A — 15-mark likely questions and model answers

## Q1. Define professional misconduct. Explain the complaint, inquiry, punishment, appeal and review procedure under the Advocates Act, 1961.

### Answer

### Introduction

An advocate is both a representative of the client and an officer of the court. Professional discipline protects clients, the administration of justice and public confidence in the Bar. The Advocates Act, 1961 does not provide an exhaustive definition of “professional or other misconduct”; section 35 gives the State Bar Council disciplinary jurisdiction when it has reason to believe that an advocate on its roll is guilty of such misconduct.

### Meaning and standard

Misconduct is assessed against the duties imposed by the Act, Bar Council of India (BCI) Rules, court rules and professional obligations. Dishonesty, breach of trust, conflict of interest, unauthorised compromise, abandonment of a brief, solicitation, fabrication of a vakalatnama or conduct that obstructs justice may amount to misconduct depending on the evidence and circumstances. A mere error of judgment or every instance of negligence does not automatically establish professional misconduct. In *Pandurang Dattatraya Khandekar v Bar Council of Maharashtra*, (1984) 2 SCC 556, the Court cautioned that misconduct must be proved and cannot be inferred merely from suspicion or an error of judgment. In *Chandra Shekhar Soni v Bar Council of Rajasthan*, (1983) 4 SCC 255, acting for conflicting interests was held contrary to professional standards.

### Complaint and inquiry under section 35

1. The State Bar Council may act on a complaint or on its own information.
2. If it has reason to believe that an advocate on its roll is guilty of professional or other misconduct, it refers the case to its disciplinary committee.
3. The disciplinary committee gives notice and a reasonable opportunity of hearing to the advocate and complainant, conducts an inquiry and may receive evidence under the Act and applicable BCI Rules.
4. After inquiry, section 35(3) empowers the committee to dismiss the complaint, reprimand the advocate, suspend the advocate from practice for a period, or remove the advocate’s name from the State roll.

The decision must follow fair procedure and be supported by evidence. *Narain Pandey v Pannalal Pandey*, (2013) 11 SCC 435, illustrates the gravity of forged vakalatnamas and false compromise documents because they undermine the integrity of court proceedings.

### BCI jurisdiction and transfer

Under section 36, the BCI may withdraw a disciplinary proceeding pending before a State Bar Council and dispose of it itself or through its disciplinary committee as authorised by the Act. Section 36B provides for transfer to the BCI of proceedings not concluded by the State disciplinary committee within one year from receipt of the complaint. This is a transfer mechanism, not a limitation period for filing complaints.

### Appeals, stay and review

- **Section 37:** an appeal from an order of a State Bar Council disciplinary committee lies to the BCI within 60 days from communication of the order, subject to the Act.
- **Section 38:** an appeal from an order of a BCI disciplinary committee lies to the Supreme Court within 60 days from communication.
- **Section 40:** an appeal does not automatically stay the order; stay may be granted under the statutory conditions.
- **Section 44:** a disciplinary committee may review its order within 60 days; a State committee’s review order requires BCI approval to take effect.
- **Section 48AA:** the BCI or its committees other than the disciplinary committee may review specified orders within 60 days.

In *D. Saibaba v Bar Council of India*, (2003) 6 SCC 186, the Court interpreted section 48AA so that the review period runs from communication or knowledge of the order, making the remedy meaningful.

### Conclusion

The Act combines self-regulation with procedural fairness and appellate supervision. Discipline must protect the profession and litigants while ensuring that serious findings are based on clear evidence and a fair inquiry.

## Q2. Define contempt of court. Explain the constitutional relationship between freedom of speech, defamation and contempt, with relevant case law.

### Answer

### Introduction and constitutional basis

Freedom of speech and expression is protected by Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution. Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions in relation to contempt of court and defamation, among other grounds. Articles 129 and 215 make the Supreme Court and High Courts courts of record with power to punish for contempt of themselves. The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines and regulates contempt jurisdiction. The purpose is to protect the administration of justice, not to shield judges from fair criticism.

### Statutory definitions

Section 2(b) defines **civil contempt** as wilful disobedience to a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court.

Section 2(c) defines **criminal contempt** as publication, whether by words, signs, visible representation or otherwise, or any other act which:

1. scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower, the authority of a court;
2. prejudices or interferes or tends to interfere with the due course of a judicial proceeding; or
3. interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.

### Distinguishing defamation and contempt

Defamation protects an individual’s reputation and is governed by the applicable civil and criminal law. Contempt protects the due administration of justice and the authority of courts in their judicial function. A statement can raise separate questions under defamation law and contempt law, but one does not automatically prove the other. The context, target, truth, intention where relevant, proximity to proceedings, and actual or likely impact on justice must be assessed under the applicable law.

### Safeguards for speech and fair comment

- Section 4 protects fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings.
- Section 5 protects fair comment on the merits of a case that has been heard and finally decided.
- Section 13(a) bars punishment unless the contempt substantially interferes or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice.
- Under section 13(b), truth may be permitted as a defence if the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and the request is bona fide.

In *In re S. Mulgaokar*, (1978) 3 SCC 339, the Supreme Court urged restraint, recognising that courts should not be unduly sensitive to criticism and should use contempt power only when justice requires it. *Dr. Subramanian Swamy v Arun Shourie*, (2014) 12 SCC 344, explains the qualified truth defence under section 13(b). *In re Arundhati Roy*, (2002) 3 SCC 343, is an example of contempt assessed in its particular factual context; it should not be read to make all forceful criticism contempt. *Re: Prashant Bhushan*, Suo Motu Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 1 of 2020, illustrates the application of criminal contempt to online statements and remains a fact-specific decision.

### Conclusion

The constitutional balance requires both protection of robust speech and protection of fair judicial process. Criticism of judgments is not per se contempt; liability depends on the statutory definition, safeguards, context and substantial-interference threshold.

## Q3. Explain the defences available in civil and criminal contempt, punishment, and the procedure for initiating contempt proceedings.

### Answer

### Introduction

The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 distinguishes civil from criminal contempt in section 2. The Act also provides defences and procedural safeguards so that contempt jurisdiction is exercised consistently with fairness and freedom of speech.

### Civil contempt and its answer

Civil contempt under section 2(b) requires **wilful** disobedience of a court order or wilful breach of an undertaking. A respondent may show that the order was unclear, they lacked knowledge of it, compliance was impossible despite reasonable effort, or the breach was not wilful. The court examines the order’s terms, notice, ability to comply and conduct. Mere non-compliance without wilfulness does not automatically satisfy section 2(b).

Order XXXIX rule 2A of the Code of Civil Procedure separately addresses disobedience or breach of an injunction/order under rules 1 or 2. It is a civil-procedure enforcement mechanism and should not be treated as identical to contempt under the 1971 Act.

### Statutory safeguards and defences

1. **Innocent publication — section 3:** protection may apply where a person had no reasonable grounds to believe that a proceeding was pending, subject to the statutory requirements.
2. **Fair and accurate report — section 4:** a fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding is protected.
3. **Fair criticism — section 5:** fair comment on a case heard and finally decided is not contempt.
4. **Good-faith complaint — section 6:** a good-faith complaint about a presiding officer of a subordinate court to the High Court or another competent court is protected within the section’s conditions.
5. **Truth — section 13(b):** the court may permit truth as a defence if it is in the public interest and raised bona fide. *Dr. Subramanian Swamy v Arun Shourie*, (2014) 12 SCC 344.
6. **Substantial interference threshold — section 13(a):** no sentence may be imposed unless the contempt substantially interferes or tends substantially to interfere with justice.
7. **Apology — section 12:** a genuine apology may lead to discharge or remission; it is not an automatic defence. *L.D. Jaikwal v State of U.P.*, (1984) 3 SCC 405.

### Initiation and procedure

- **Contempt in the face of the Supreme Court or High Court — section 14:** the court informs the person of the charge, allows a defence and decides the matter through the statutory summary process.
- **Other criminal contempt — section 15:** the Supreme Court or High Court may act suo motu; on a motion by the Attorney-General/Solicitor-General for Supreme Court matters or Advocate-General for High Court matters; or on another person’s motion with written consent of the appropriate law officer. For contempt of a subordinate court, the High Court may act on reference or as permitted by section 15(2).
- **Notice — section 17:** notice is served with the motion/affidavits or reference, and the person charged is given a chance to respond.
- **Bench — section 18:** a case under section 15 is heard by a bench of at least two judges, subject to the statutory exception.
- **Subordinate courts — section 10:** High Courts may punish contempt of subordinate courts within the Act.

### Punishment and appeal

Section 12 provides simple imprisonment up to six months, a fine up to ₹2,000, or both; the court may discharge or remit punishment on apology. Section 19 provides an appeal as of right from qualifying High Court orders punishing for contempt, subject to the prescribed forum and time limits.

### Conclusion

The applicant must establish the statutory elements and the court must observe procedural safeguards. Defences are fact-dependent; wilfulness in civil contempt and substantial interference in criminal contempt are central checks on the jurisdiction.

## Q4. Discuss an advocate’s duties to the court, client, opponent and colleagues under the BCI Rules. Explain advertising, other employment and vakalatnama restrictions.

### Answer

### Introduction

Professional ethics protect the client, the court and public confidence in legal services. The principal rules are in Part VI, Chapter II of the Bar Council of India Rules, made under the Advocates Act, 1961. The advocate’s duty to the client must be performed consistently with duties to the court and justice system.

### Duty to the court

Under Section I of Chapter II, advocates must maintain dignity and respect for courts, avoid private communications with judges about pending matters, use fair means, appear in prescribed dress, and avoid conduct that obstructs justice. Counsel must not knowingly mislead the court or present false material. The duty includes respectful advocacy and appropriate professional criticism through lawful channels.

### Duty to the client

Section II requires an advocate to accept briefs consistent with competence and availability, fearlessly protect lawful interests, give candid advice, avoid conflicts, preserve confidentiality subject to law, maintain proper accounts of client money, and not withdraw without sufficient cause and reasonable notice. A lawyer cannot guarantee an outcome or sacrifice the client’s rights without authority. In *Himalayan Cooperative Group Housing Society v Balwan Singh*, (2015) 7 SCC 373, the Court held counsel cannot surrender substantive rights or concede the merits without client authority.

### Duty to opponents and colleagues

Advocates should communicate with represented parties through their counsel, honour undertakings, avoid sharp practice, and seek consent or leave when changing counsel on an existing vakalatnama. Courtesy does not require compromising the client’s lawful case. *Chandra Shekhar Soni v Bar Council of Rajasthan*, (1983) 4 SCC 255, underscores the prohibition on acting for conflicting interests without informed consent after disclosure.

### Advertising and solicitation

Rule 36 prohibits solicitation and advertising, subject to limited information permitted on an advocate’s website under BCI specifications. Promotional claims, touting and improper publicity connected with cases are restricted. Public legal education should not become solicitation.

### Other employment

Rule 49 generally prevents an advocate from practising while in full-time salaried employment, subject to the rule’s exact terms and applicable exceptions. The advocate must notify the State Bar Council and cease practice while employed as required. Other occupations must be assessed under current BCI rules and relevant directions.

### Vakalatnama and dress

A vakalatnama records a client’s authority for an advocate to act and plead, subject to its terms and procedural rules. It should be validly executed, and counsel must follow client instructions on settlement or concessions. Prescribed dress is governed by the dress rules in Part VI, Chapter IV and local court directions.

### Conclusion

The advocate is not merely a hired spokesperson: the role combines loyalty, competence, candour and independence. Ethical rules regulate how advocacy is conducted so that both client interests and administration of justice are protected.

## Q5. Explain the right to practise under sections 29–34 of the Advocates Act, 1961, including the court’s power to permit a non-advocate to appear.

### Answer

### Introduction

The Advocates Act, 1961 establishes a unified class of legal practitioners and sets out who may practise law. The right is statutory and regulated; it is not an unrestricted entitlement to appear in every forum without regard to court rules.

### Statutory framework

1. **Section 29:** advocates are the only recognised class of persons entitled to practise the profession of law, subject to the Act.
2. **Section 30:** an advocate whose name is on a State roll has a right to practise throughout India before courts, including the Supreme Court, tribunals or persons legally authorised to take evidence, and other authorities/bodies where the law permits, subject to the Act and rules.
3. **Section 32:** a court, authority or person may permit a non-advocate to appear in a particular case. This is a case-specific discretion, not a general right to practise.
4. **Section 33:** except as otherwise provided by the Act or another law, only an advocate enrolled under the Act may practise before a court or authority.
5. **Section 34:** High Courts may make rules laying down conditions subject to which an advocate may practise in the High Court and subordinate courts.

### Limits and professional responsibilities

The right is subject to enrolment, professional conduct rules, court procedure, restrictions applicable to Senior Advocates and lawful directions of the court. Bar Councils regulate enrolment and professional discipline; courts control proceedings, audience and appearances in matters before them. Section 32 is an exception for an individual case and cannot be used to bypass the statutory enrolment scheme.

Advocates must appear when retained and avoid abandoning clients. In *Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v Union of India*, (2003) 2 SCC 45, the Supreme Court held that advocates have no right to strike or boycott court work; such action harms litigants and justice. In *S.J. Chaudhary v State (Delhi Administration)*, (1984) 1 SCC 722, the Court stressed an advocate’s duty to attend a criminal trial from day to day after accepting the brief.

### Conclusion

Sections 29–34 create a regulated right to practise, preserve a narrow judicial discretion to permit individual appearances, and empower High Courts to govern practice before their courts. The framework protects competent representation and orderly administration of justice.

## Q6. Explain the development of the legal profession in India from the pre-British period to the Advocates Act, 1961.

### Answer

### Introduction

The modern Indian Bar developed through a gradual change from locally varied systems of dispute resolution to a statutory, unified profession. The stages reflect changes in courts, legal education, rights of audience and professional regulation.

### Pre-British period

In ancient and medieval India, disputes were handled through royal, local, community and religious institutions. Under Mughal and regional systems, qazis and legal scholars performed judicial or advisory functions. Persons described as vakils could assist litigants, often as agents. There was no uniform, all-India system of enrolment and discipline comparable to the modern Bar.

### British period

- The Regulating Act 1773 and Charter of 1774 established the Supreme Court at Calcutta; later Supreme Courts operated at Madras and Bombay.
- Company courts developed alongside Crown courts, producing different classes of practitioners and rights of audience.
- The Legal Practitioners Act 1846 widened eligibility to practise in courts. Later legislation, including the Legal Practitioners Acts of 1853 and 1879, regulated practitioners and consolidated parts of the system.
- The High Courts Act 1861 created High Courts, which exercised powers concerning legal practitioners under their charters and rules.
- The Indian Bar Councils Act 1926 established Bar Councils at certain High Courts and advanced limited professional self-government, but did not create one unified national Bar.
- The All India Bar Committee (1951–53) recommended a unified Bar and a national regulatory structure.

### Post-independence and the Advocates Act

The Advocates Act, 1961 consolidated the law relating to legal practitioners and created State Bar Councils and the BCI. It established advocates as the recognised class of persons entitled to practise, provided for State rolls and transfer between rolls, regulated professional conduct and discipline, and authorised rule-making on legal education and practice. It created a framework for one All-India Bar while preserving the role of courts and constitutional institutions.

### Modern developments

Globalisation has increased specialisation, cross-border commercial work, arbitration, legal technology and digital research. These changes also raise ethical concerns involving confidentiality, conflicts, access, competence and commercialization. Technology may improve access and administration but does not remove the advocate’s duties of honesty and independence.

### Conclusion

The profession evolved from plural, informal forms of representation through colonial court structures to the integrated statutory framework of 1961. Its modern development remains tied to access to justice, ethical independence and public confidence.

# Part B — 10-mark likely questions and model answers

## Q7. Explain the composition, election, term, disqualification and functions of a State Bar Council.

Section 3 of the Advocates Act provides for a State Bar Council for each State and specified combinations of States/Union Territories. Its composition includes elected members chosen by advocates on the State roll through proportional representation by single transferable vote, together with specified ex officio law officers. The number of elected members depends on the size of the State roll under section 3; use the current Act for exact thresholds.

Under section 8, the term is generally five years, subject to the Act’s extension and election provisions. Section 8A provides for a Special Committee in the circumstances specified where elections are not completed. Section 10B sets out disqualification grounds for members, including statutory qualifications relating to age, insolvency, unsoundness of mind, conviction and other specified matters; the exact clauses should be read from the current consolidated Act.

Section 6 functions include admitting persons as advocates on its roll, preparing and maintaining that roll, entertaining and determining misconduct matters, safeguarding advocates’ rights, promoting law reform and legal aid, managing funds and elections, and performing assigned functions. Section 15 gives rule-making power on specified State Council matters, subject to the Act and required approvals.

**Conclusion:** State Bar Councils are elected statutory regulators. Their composition and powers balance professional self-government with accountability under the Advocates Act and BCI supervision.

## Q8. Distinguish Senior Advocates from other advocates under section 16 of the Advocates Act.

Section 16 recognises two classes: Senior Advocates and other advocates. The Supreme Court or a High Court may designate an advocate as Senior Advocate, with the advocate’s consent, where ability, standing at the Bar or special knowledge/experience in law merits distinction. Designation is governed by the Act and applicable judicial guidelines.

Senior Advocates are subject to restrictions under BCI Rules and court rules. In general, they do not accept instructions directly from a client and act through an advocate-on-record or another advocate as required by the relevant forum. The restrictions preserve the distinction in professional role and court procedure; they do not make Senior Advocates a separate enrolment class outside the State roll.

Under section 23, Senior Advocates have pre-audience over other advocates, after the law officers listed in the section. Designation does not remove ordinary duties of honesty, competence, decorum and fidelity to the client.

**Conclusion:** Section 16 recognises distinction based on professional merit while imposing role-specific practice restrictions. Seniority or designation does not confer immunity from professional discipline.

## Q9. Explain qualifications and disqualifications for admission as an advocate and disposal of enrolment applications.

Section 24 sets qualifications for admission on a State roll, including citizenship (with the statutory reciprocity condition for foreign nationals), age, a recognised law degree, compliance with BCI rules and payment of prescribed fees/stamp duty. The precise degree and category requirements are specified in section 24 and rules.

Section 24A sets out disqualifications, including conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, conviction under specified untouchability legislation and dismissal or removal from State employment on a charge involving moral turpitude, subject to statutory provisos, exceptions and the period after which disqualification ceases.

Under sections 25–26, the application is made to the State Bar Council within whose jurisdiction the applicant proposes to practise. The enrolment committee considers whether statutory and rule requirements are met. Section 26 provides for inquiry/referral where necessary and safeguards before refusal. Section 26A allows removal of a name in specified circumstances, while section 27 governs a fresh application after refusal in the circumstances stated there. State Bar Council rules under section 28 operate within the Act.

**Conclusion:** Enrolment is a statutory process, not an automatic consequence of holding a degree. Eligibility, disqualification, documentation and fair disposal must all be addressed under the current Act and rules.

## Q10. Explain contempt by judges, magistrates and persons acting judicially under section 16 of the Contempt of Courts Act.

Section 16(1) provides that, subject to other law, a judge, magistrate or other person acting judicially is liable for contempt of their own court or another court in the same manner as any other individual; the Act applies so far as may be. Judicial office therefore does not create a blanket immunity from contempt jurisdiction.

Section 16(2) creates a specific protection: the section does not apply to observations or remarks made by a judge, magistrate or other person acting judicially regarding a subordinate court in an appeal or revision pending before that judicial officer against the subordinate court’s order or judgment. This allows reasoned appellate or revisional criticism within the proceeding.

The provision must be read with the constitutional contempt powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts and with fair procedure under the Act. It does not mean that every judicial error is contempt; contempt concerns conduct within the statutory definition and due process is required. *In re Hon’ble Justice C.S. Karnan*, (2017) 7 SCC 1, is an exceptional case involving a sitting High Court judge and should be described narrowly on its facts.

**Conclusion:** Section 16 confirms accountability while protecting judicial observations made in the proper course of appellate or revisional work.

## Q11. Explain the effect of e-Courts on efficiency, transparency and access to justice, and discuss its limitations.

E-Courts use ICT for case registration and tracking, e-filing, e-service, digital records, online cause lists, payments and video-conferencing. India’s e-Courts Mission Mode Project developed through phases, with Phase III emphasising a more integrated, accessible digital ecosystem and digitisation.

**Benefits:** Electronic filing and services can reduce travel and paper handling; online case status and orders can improve access to information; digital scheduling and records can help case management; virtual hearings can assist remote users and reduce geographic barriers where suitable.

**Limits:** Unequal access to devices, connectivity, language support and digital literacy may exclude vulnerable litigants. Technical failure, poor audio/video, privacy and cybersecurity risks can undermine participation. Digitisation alone does not remove case backlog, which also depends on vacancies, procedure, adjournments and institutional capacity. Fair hearing, accessibility, confidentiality and reliable records must remain priorities.

**Conclusion:** E-Courts can support efficiency and access when implemented with safeguards and inclusive design. They are tools for justice administration, not a substitute for procedural fairness or adequate court capacity.

## Q12. Explain the law on strikes and boycotts by advocates.

In *Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v Union of India*, (2003) 2 SCC 45; AIR 2003 SC 739, the Supreme Court held that advocates have no right to strike or boycott courts, including token strikes. Such action harms litigants, delays cases and obstructs justice. Courts should not routinely adjourn matters merely because of a strike call, and Bar Councils may take disciplinary action as permitted by law.

The judgment recognises only a very narrow and exceptional situation involving the dignity, integrity or independence of the Bar or Bench, with a limited protest and safeguards to prevent disruption. It is not a general licence for strikes. An individual advocate should not abandon a client or fail to attend after accepting a brief. In *S.J. Chaudhary v State (Delhi Administration)*, (1984) 1 SCC 722, the Court stated that counsel accepting a criminal brief must attend from day to day.

**Conclusion:** The advocate’s professional duty to the client and administration of justice prevails over collective abstention from court work.

## Q13. Explain transfer of an advocate’s name between State rolls and the right of pre-audience.

Section 18 of the Advocates Act permits an advocate to apply to the BCI to transfer their name from one State roll to another. On receipt of the application, the BCI may direct the concerned State Bar Council to transfer the name, subject to the statutory conditions. The transfer framework allows an advocate to move their enrolment when changing the State with which their practice is associated, while maintaining the integrity and accuracy of State rolls. The Act does not permit duplicate enrolment on multiple State rolls; an advocate’s name is to be maintained on one State roll at a time.

Section 23 governs pre-audience, meaning priority in addressing the court. The Attorney-General of India has pre-audience over all other advocates; the Solicitor-General follows; then the Additional Solicitor-General(s), Advocate-General(s) in inter se seniority, Senior Advocates, and other advocates according to seniority, subject to the exact statutory order and court rules.

**Conclusion:** Section 18 provides a regulated transfer process, while section 23 orders oral audience priority. Neither provision changes the advocate’s substantive duties or the court’s control of its proceedings.

## Q14. Explain the power of a High Court to make rules concerning an advocate’s right to practise.

Section 34 of the Advocates Act empowers a High Court to make rules laying down the conditions subject to which an advocate may practise in the High Court and courts subordinate to it. This power operates within the Advocates Act, valid BCI Rules and the Constitution. It supports court control over appearances, procedure and orderly conduct of litigation; it does not permit rules contrary to the parent Act.

The distinction is important: Bar Councils regulate enrolment, professional conduct and discipline under the Act, while courts control proceedings and the conditions of appearance before them. The right under section 30 to practise throughout India is expressly subject to the Act and other applicable rules. Section 32 separately allows a court or authority to permit a non-advocate to appear in an individual case.

**Conclusion:** Section 34 reconciles the nationally recognised right to practise with the High Court’s responsibility to regulate practice and proceedings in its jurisdiction.

## Q15. Explain the constitution of committees of the Bar Councils and their role.

Section 9 of the Advocates Act requires every Bar Council to constitute one or more disciplinary committees. Each disciplinary committee consists of three members: two elected by the Council from among its members and one co-opted by the Council from among advocates possessing the prescribed standing at the Bar. The senior-most advocate member chairs the committee. The Act’s detailed eligibility and term provisions should be checked when answering a specific procedural question.

Section 10 provides for other committees, including executive, enrolment, legal aid and other committees as required by the Act and rules. Committees enable the Council to carry out specialised functions. Disciplinary committees hear matters under sections 35 and 36 and may impose the statutory consequences after inquiry and hearing. A committee’s authority remains subject to the Act, natural justice, applicable rules and appeal provisions.

**Conclusion:** Bar Council committees distribute regulatory work and provide a specialised forum for discipline, enrolment and administration under the statutory framework.

# Part C — 5-mark short answers and case comments

## Part C — Case comment 1(a): *Chandra Shekhar Soni v Bar Council of Rajasthan* (1983) 4 SCC 255; AIR 1983 SC 1012.

**Facts:** The advocate was disciplined for professional misconduct, including accepting representation for the opposite side after previously appearing for the complainant in a criminal matter, and related conduct involving an attempt to procure an improper benefit.

**Issue:** Whether the conduct amounted to professional misconduct and whether the disciplinary findings and punishment should stand.

**Decision and ratio:** The Supreme Court upheld the finding that acting for conflicting interests without informed consent after full disclosure is contrary to professional etiquette. It modified the suspension from three years to one year in the circumstances, while strongly disapproving the conduct.

**Comment:** The case protects client confidence and the integrity of the profession. It demonstrates that a conflict can itself undermine public trust and that discipline should be proportionate to the proved misconduct.

## Part C — Case comment 1(b): *Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v Union of India* (2003) 2 SCC 45; AIR 2003 SC 739.

**Facts/issues:** The case considered whether advocates or Bar Associations have a right to strike or boycott court work and what effect such action has on litigants and administration of justice.

**Decision and ratio:** The Supreme Court held there is no right to strike or call for a boycott of courts. Any exceptional protest is extremely narrow and cannot disrupt judicial work or leave clients without representation.

**Comment:** The decision treats advocacy as a public responsibility as well as a profession. It protects litigants’ access to courts and reinforces duties arising once counsel accepts a brief.

## Part C — Case comment 1(c): *In re Hon’ble Justice C.S. Karnan* (2017) 7 SCC 1; AIR 2017 SC 3191.

**Facts/issues:** A seven-judge Supreme Court bench considered contempt proceedings concerning communications and conduct by a sitting High Court judge directed at members of the judiciary.

**Decision and ratio:** The Court convicted Justice Karnan of contempt and sentenced him to six months’ imprisonment. The case involved exceptional facts and institutional issues concerning judicial office and constitutional contempt powers.

**Comment:** The decision confirms that judicial office is not a blanket immunity from contempt, but its exceptional facts should not be generalised to ordinary criticism of judicial decisions.

## Part C — Case comment 1(d): *Re: Prashant Bhushan*, Suo Motu Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 1 of 2020, judgment 14 August 2020; sentencing order 31 August 2020.

**Facts/issues:** The Supreme Court initiated suo motu proceedings concerning two tweets by advocate Prashant Bhushan and considered whether they constituted criminal contempt.

**Decision and ratio:** The Court found criminal contempt and imposed a fine of ₹1, with three months’ simple imprisonment and three months’ suspension from practice in default of payment. Those default consequences were conditional on non-payment.

**Comment:** The decision concerns the boundary between online criticism and criminal contempt. Present the holding and result accurately, while distinguishing legal analysis from the wider public debate about the judgment.

## Q16. Short note: Right of pre-audience.

Section 23 of the Advocates Act determines pre-audience priority. The Attorney-General has pre-audience over all other advocates, followed by the Solicitor-General, Additional Solicitor-General(s), Advocate-General(s) in inter se seniority, Senior Advocates, and other advocates according to seniority, subject to the statutory wording and relevant court rules. Pre-audience concerns order of addressing the court; it does not itself determine the merits or grant additional substantive rights.

## Q17. Short note: Truth as a defence in contempt.

Section 13(b) of the Contempt of Courts Act permits truth to be raised as a defence if the court is satisfied that it is in the public interest and that the request is bona fide. The defence is qualified, not automatic. In *Dr. Subramanian Swamy v Arun Shourie*, (2014) 12 SCC 344, the Supreme Court interpreted this provision. Section 13(a) separately requires substantial interference or tendency substantially to interfere with justice before punishment.

## Q18. Short note: Punishment for contempt.

Under section 12(1) of the Contempt of Courts Act, punishment may be simple imprisonment up to six months, fine up to ₹2,000, or both. The court may discharge the person or remit punishment on a genuine apology. Under section 13(a), no sentence may be imposed unless contempt substantially interferes or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice.

## Q19. Short note: Right of a court to permit a non-advocate to appear.

Section 32 of the Advocates Act preserves the power of a court, authority or person legally entitled to permit a non-advocate to appear in a particular case. It is discretionary and case-specific. It does not create a general right to practise law; sections 29 and 33 establish the general statutory position in favour of enrolled advocates.

## Q20. Short note: *S.J. Chaudhary v State (Delhi Administration)* (1984) 1 SCC 722; AIR 1984 SC 618.

The Supreme Court held that an advocate who accepts a criminal trial brief has a duty to attend the trial from day to day. Failure to attend may breach professional duty and contribute to delay. The case is authority for diligence and the advocate’s responsibility to the client and court.

## Q21. Short note: *A.S. Mohammed Rafi v State of Tamil Nadu* (2011) 1 SCC 688; AIR 2011 SC 308.

The Supreme Court held that a Bar Association cannot pass a resolution directing advocates not to represent accused persons. Such collective pressure is impermissible and interferes with the accused’s right to legal representation and the advocate’s professional independence.
