  •  INTRODUCTION
Intrinsic aids are matters within an Act itself which may help make the meaning clearer. The court may consider the long title, the short title and any preamble. Other useful internal aids may include headings before a group of sections and any schedules attached to the Act. There are also often marginal notes explaining different sections; however, these are not generally regarded as giving Parliament's intention as they will have been inserted after parliamentary debates and are only helpful comments put in by the printer.
Some Acts include sections in which words are expressly defined. For example, the 1963 Animal Boarding Act section 5(2) states: ‘In this Act animal means any dog or cat.’ The Interpretation Act 1978 section 6 also states that unless the contrary intention appears, words importing the masculine gender also include the feminine and words importing the feminine gender also include the masculine. In addition, words in the singular also include the plural and words in the plural include the singular.

  •  INTRINSIC / INTERNAL AIDS
There are some internal aids which helps the court in the interpretation of Court. Those are as follows:
  •  Short title
-The short title of the Act is only its name & is given solely for the purpose of facility of reference.
-It is merely a name given for identification of the Act & generally ends with the year of passing of the Act.
E.g. Indian Contract Act, 1872; Indian Penal Code, 1860.
-In some modern statutes it is sometimes given in a section near the end of the Act with the use of language ‘this Act may be cited as...’
Sometimes in the beginning it is given that ‘This Act may be called...’
E.g. This Act may be called the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
-Even though it is a part of the statute, it has no role to play while interpreting statute.

  •  Long title
-A statute is headed by a long title whose purpose is to give a general description about the object of the Act.
-Normally, it begins with the words ‘An Act to...’
E.g. the long title of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 says: An Act to consolidate & amend the law relating to criminal procedure.
-In the olden days the long title was not considered a part of the statute & was, therefore, not considered an aid while interpreting it.
-But, in recent times there is change in the thinking of the courts & there are numerous occasions when help of long title was taken in interpretation.
-long title helps to the extent of removing confusions & ambiguities. In 
Kedar nath v. State of West Bengal, the State Government was empowered to choose as to refer particular cases to Supreme Court. This was challenged as violative of Art-14 of the Indian constitution. Court rejected this contention & held that it is clear by long title that such power is there with State Government.

  •  Preamble
-Preamble contains the main objects of the Act; it is the key to open the mind of the legislature.
-If the language of an Act is clear & unambiguous, the preamble has no part to play in interpretation.
-If there are more than one interpretation then preamble can be taken as aid to find out true meaning.
-the modern trend generally is not to have a preamble in an Act & it resulted into decline of importance of preamble. In
Attorney general v. H.R.H. Prince Earnest Augustus of Hanover, if there is need to remedied the mischief then it may be permissible to take aids of constructions. But, preamble is not of the same weight as an aid to construction of a section. In
State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali, the State Government was empowered to choose as to refer particular cases to Supreme Court. This was challenged as violative of Art-14 of the Indian constitution. Court rejected this contention & held that it is clear by preamble clearly & unambiguously that such power is there with State Government. In
A.C.Sharma v. Delhi administration, in this case Delhi police has ceased to have power of investigating bribery cases because preamble pointed out to this effect. The Supreme Court held that no preamble can interfere with the clear & unambiguous words of a statute.

  •  Marginal notes
-Marginal notes are those which are inserted at the side of the sections in an Act & express the effect of the sections. It is also called as “side notes”.
-In olden times marginal notes were taken as aid if meaning of enactment was in doubt.
-But, in modern times the court is of the view that marginal notes have no role to play.
-Marginal notes are not parts of a statute because they are not inserted by the legislatures nor inserted as per instructions of it. So, it can be inaccurate.
-Exceptionally marginal notes act as aid if it is inserted by legislature.
E.g. marginal notes are inserted by constituent assembly in Indian constitution.  
  In Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar, marginal note added to Art-286 of the Indian constitution is a part of it. Therefore it can be taken as an aid in interpretation because it furnishes a clue to the purpose & meaning of that Article. In
  Tara Prasad v. Union of India, the Supreme Court held that marginal notes to the sections of the statute cannot take away the effect of the provisions so as to render those provisions incompetent.  

  •  Headings
-Headings are prefixed to sections or a set of sections.
-It is treated by courts as preamble means rules of preamble are applicable here.
- If the language of an Act is clear & unambiguous, the headings have no part to play in interpretation.
-If there is more than one interpretation then headings can be taken as aid to find out true meaning. In 
  Shelly v. London County Council, in this case it was held that a heading to one set of sections cannot act as an aid to interpret another set of sections. In
  Bhinka v. Charan singh, in this case the landlord seeks to evict a person who has no right of possession. The court takes the help of heading & held that here tenant is not a person who has no right of possession. Hence tenant cannot be evicted.

  •  Definition/Interpretation clauses
-Definition or interpretation clauses are generally included in a statute with the purpose of extending the natural meaning of some words.
-It is used to interpret the meaning of words which are not clear.
-The only exception to this rule is that if the court feels that in the context of particular provision if it resulted into absurdity then court will not apply definition.
-definition clause of one Act cannot be used to explain the same word in another statute except if both laws are pari materia. In
  Ramanlal bhailal patel v. State of Gujarat, the use of the word ‘includes’ indicates an intention to enlarge the meaning of the word. The use of the word ‘denotes’ ambit of particular word and the word ‘deemed’ creates fiction.
  State of Haryana v. Raghubir dayal, the use of word ‘shall’ is ordinarily mandatory but it is sometimes not so interpreted if it does not matches with real intention of legislature.
  •  Provisos
-Provisos generally not to be taken absolutely in its strict literal sense but is of necessity limited to the ambit of the section.
-If proviso is in contradicting the main enactment, then proviso will prevail over main enactment because it speaks the last intention.
-The court should made efforts to reconcile the main clause & proviso. In
  R. v. Leeds prison (Governor), the main part of an enactment cannot be so interpreted as to render its proviso unnecessary and ineffective. In
  T.M. Kanniyan v. I. Tax officer, Pondicherry, under Art-240(1) President has power to make rules along with legislature for the union territory. Such power cannot be curtailed by proviso.

  •  Illustrations
-Illustrations are sometimes appended to a section of a statute with a view to illustrate the provision.
-A very large number of Indian Acts have illustrations appended to various sections.
-It is being show of mind of the legislature is a good guide to find out the intention of the framers. In
  Jumma masjid v. Kodimaniandra, in this case it was held that S.43 of the Transfer of property Act applicable to spes successionis and if the other conditions of the section are satisfied the transferee is entitled to claim the property.
  Mahesh chand Sharma v. Raj kumara Sharma, it was observed that illustration is part of the section and it helps to elucidate the principle of the section.

  •  Exceptions & Saving clauses
-Exceptions are generally added to an enactment with the purpose of exempting something which would otherwise fall within the ambit of the main provision.
E.g. 5 exceptions have been added to S.300 of the Indian Penal Code. In 
Collector of Customs v. M/s. Modi Rubber Limited, the Supreme Court held that whenever there is a provision in the nature of an exception to the principal clause thereof, it must be construed with regard to that principal clause.
-Saving clauses are generally appended in cases of repeal and re-enactment of a statute.
-In case of clash between saving clause & main part, the saving clause has to be rejected.
  
  •  Explanations
-Explanations are inserted with the purpose of explaining the meaning of a particular provision & to remove doubts.
-It does not expand the meaning of the provision to which it is added but only ties to remove confusion.
E.g. S.108 of IPC defines ‘Abettor’ has 5 explanations attached to it.
-Sometimes, explanations are inserted not at the time of enactment but at a later stage.
E.g. 2 explanations to S.405 of IPC ‘Criminal breach of trust’ were inserted in 1973 & 1975 respectively.
  In Bihta Co-operative development care marketing union v. State of Bihar, the Supreme Court said that in case of a conflict between the main provision and the explanation attached to it, the general duty of the court is to try to harmonise the two. In 
M.K.Salpekar v. Sunil Kumar Shamsunder Chaudhari, the Supreme Court observed that where a provision is related to 2 kinds of accommodation & explanation refers only residential accommodation, then it cannot be looked into matters other than residential accommodation.

  •  Schedules
-Schedules attached to an Act generally deal with as to how claims or rights under the Act are to be asserted or how powers conferred.
-It may contain some subjects in the form of list.
E.g. Constitution of India provides union n state list relating to legislative powers.
  In LIC of India v. Escorts ltd. Schedules are parts of the statute itself & may be looked into by the courts for the purpose of interpreting the main body of the statute. In
M/s Aphali Pharmaceuticals ltd. v. State of Maharashtra, court held that in case of a clash between main body and schedules of the Act, the main body will prevail and schedules has to be rejected.

  •  Punctuation
-In the ancient times, statutes were passed without punctuations and court was not concerned about it.
-In the modern times statutes contain punctuations and court first look into it.
-If interpreting provisions with punctuation leads to injustice then court read provision without punctuation. In
  Dadaji v. Sukhdeobabu, the Supreme Court held that the punctuation marks by themselves do not control the meaning of a statute where its meaning is otherwise obvious. 
  In M.K.Salpekar v. Sunil Kumar Shamsunder Chaudhari, under the provision a tenant could be evicted if ‘has secured alternative accommodation, or does not reasonably need the house’. It was held that the two expressions separated by comma are independent of each other & are not connected anyway.

Subject: Interpretation of Statutes Topic: External (Extrinsic) Aids to Interpretation Marks: 10 Marks (Recommended length: 3.5 to 4 pages) 

1. INTRODUCTION
The primary objective of the judiciary while interpreting a statute is to ascertain the true "intent of the legislature" (mens legislatoris). When the text of a statute is clear, plain, and unambiguous, the courts apply the Literal Rule. However, when the words are vague, obscure, or capable of multiple interpretations, internal aids are exhausted first. If ambiguity still persists, the courts take recourse to External Aids (also known as extrinsic aids). 
External aids are materials, sources, or documents that are found outside the body of the statute itself but are referred to by judges to understand the background, context, and purpose of the law. 
2. WHEN ARE EXTERNAL AIDS RESORTED TO?
As a general rule, external aids cannot be used to alter, amend, or override the plain and unambiguous language of a statute. They are sought only under the following circumstances: 
  •  When the words used in the provision are ambiguous or unclear. 
  •  When the application of the literal text leads to manifest absurdity or injustice. 
  •  To discover the object, purpose, and historical backdrop of the legislation. 
Key Case Law: 
  •  B. Prabhakar Rao v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1985): The Supreme Court held that "Where internal aids are not forthcoming, we can always have recourse to external aids to discover the object of the legislation. External aids are not ruled out. This is now a well-settled principle of modern statutory construction." 

3. MAJOR EXTERNAL AIDS OF INTERPRETATION
A. Parliamentary History (Legislative History)
Parliamentary history encompasses the entire process a bill goes through before becoming an Act. It includes: 
  •  Statement of Objects and Reasons (SOR): Accompanies the bill to explain why the law is being introduced. 
  •  Reports of Committees: Reports submitted by Law Commissions, Select Committees, or Joint Parliamentary Committees. 
  •  Parliamentary Debates / Speeches: Debates that occurred in the House during the passing of the bill. 
  •  Evidentiary Value: The traditional English view strictly rejected parliamentary history. However, the modern Indian view allows its use for the limited purpose of understanding the historical background and the specific evil the Act sought to remedy, though the speech of a single minister cannot be used to interpret the final legal text. 
  •  Case Law: State of West Bengal v. Subodh Gopal Bose (1954) – The Supreme Court ruled that the Statement of Objects and Reasons can be referred to for the limited purpose of understanding the state of affairs that brought about the legislation. 
B. Historical Facts and Surrounding Circumstances
The court is entitled to put itself in the position of the legislature at the time the Act was passed. This means looking into the socio-economic, political, and cultural environment prevalent when the law was drafted. This is deeply connected to the Mischief Rule (Heydon’s Case), which asks: 
  •  What was the common law before the Act? 
  •  What was the mischief/defect for which the common law did not provide? 
  •  What remedy did Parliament resolve to cure the disease? 
  •  Case Law: R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. Union of India (1957) – The Supreme Court considered the history of the prize competitions and the trade environment to determine whether the Act targeted gambling or skill-based competitions. 
C. Dictionaries
When a specific term or expression is not defined within the definitions clause (Section 2) of the Act, courts naturally refer to standard legal and general dictionaries (e.g., Oxford, Black’s Law Dictionary) to ascertain the ordinary, grammatical, and popular meaning of the words. 
  •  Evidentiary Value: Dictionary meanings are persuasive but not binding. If a word has acquired a specific technical or legal meaning in a particular context, the dictionary meaning yields to it. 
  •  Case Law: State of Maharashtra v. Praful B. Desai (2003) – The Supreme Court utilized dictionary meanings alongside modern developments to interpret the term "presence" in the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) to include video conferencing. 
D. Statutes in Pari Materia (Related Statutes)
Pari Materia means "dealing with the same subject matter." If a provision in an Act is ambiguous, the court can look at other statutes enacted by the same legislature that deal with the same subject, person, or thing. The legislature is presumed to have a consistent and uniform policy across related laws. 
  •  Case Law: State of Assam v. D.P. Barua (1969) – The Supreme Court held that the Assam Agricultural Income Tax Act and the Indian Income Tax Act are in pari materia, and definitions/interpretations from one can assist in clarifying the other. 
E. Textbooks, Legal Treatises, and Scholarly Articles
Courts often refer to standard works of eminent legal jurists (such as Maxwell, Craies, or Mulla) to understand the foundational concepts of law and complex statutory provisions. 
  •  Evidentiary Value: They hold highly persuasive value, especially when dealing with well-established principles of common law, equity, or international law. 
F. Foreign Decisions
Judgments delivered by foreign courts (especially common law jurisdictions like the UK, USA, Australia, and Canada) can be referred to when an Indian statute is modeled after a foreign statute (e.g., the Indian Contract Act or the Sale of Goods Act, which are heavily based on English Common Law). 
  •  Evidentiary Value: Foreign decisions have only persuasive value and are never binding on Indian courts. If an Indian precedent exists, or if Indian socio-economic conditions differ, foreign judgments must be completely disregarded. 
  •  Case Law: Forasol v. Oil and Natural Gas Commission (1984) – The Supreme Court referred to English decisions regarding foreign currency conversions but adapted the final ruling to align with Indian economic contexts. 
G. Later Scientific and Technological Developments (The "Always Speaking" Approach)
Statutes are generally regarded as "always speaking." This means a statute must be interpreted in a way that accounts for scientific, technological, and medical advancements that did not exist at the time the law was enacted, provided they fit within the broad purpose of the text. 
  •  Case Law: Senior Electric Inspector v. Laxminarayan Chopra (1962) – The Supreme Court held that the expression "telegraph line" under the Telegraph Act, 1885, was wide enough to include subsequent inventions like wireless radio networks. 

4. ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS OF EXTERNAL AIDS
Advantages 
Limitations 
Helps prevent absurd results when the literal text fails.
Can introduce subjectivity, allowing individual judges to pick and choose sources.
Bridges the gap between old legislation and modern societal updates.
Legislative history (like long debates) can be contradictory and confusing.
Focuses heavily on the true welfare objective of the lawmakers.
Cannot be used to alter or defeat the plain, clear provisions of an Act.

5. CONCLUSION
In conclusion, external aids serve as excellent guiding tools for the judiciary when traversing ambiguous statutory terrains. In the words of Justice G.P. Singh, external aids act as a "prop or a crutch" to assist the court when internal textual analysis hits a dead end. However, judges must exercise extreme caution, ensuring that extrinsic materials are utilized only to clear up ambiguity, and never to rewrite the clear letters of the law.

  •  INTRODUCTION
Extrinsic aids are matters which may help put an Act into context. Sources include previous Acts of Parliament on the same topic, earlier case law, dictionaries of the time, and the historical setting. It permits to be used where the legislation is ambiguous or obscure or leads to an absurdity, and the material relied on comprises one or more statements by a Minister or other promoter of the Bill and such other parliamentary material as is necessary to understand the statements, and the effect and the statements that were relied on have to be clear.
Extrinsic aids also include international conventions, regulations or directives which have been implemented by English legislation. It is thought that English law should be interpreted in such a way as to be consistent with international law. Section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 expressly states that as far as it is possible to do so, an Act must be read and given effect in a way which is compatible with the rights in the European Convention on Human Rights. This only applies to any case where there is an issue of human rights.

  •  EXTRINSIC/EXTERNAL AIDS
There are some internal aids which helps the court in the interpretation of Court. Those are as follows:
  •  Parliamentary history
-Historical evolution of a provision can be relied upon as an external aid to construction. 
Mohan Lal Tripathi v. District Magistrate, Rai Bareilly (1992)
White paper giving details of the facts leading to enactment of a statute issued by the government is admissible for understanding the background, when the court is called upon to interpret and decide the validity of a particular statute.
-Extensive reference was made to the white paper for the purpose of understanding the background of the Rama Janmabhumi Babri Masjid Dispute and the constitutionality of the acquisition of certain areas of Ayodhya Act, 1993.
In Golak Nath v. State of Punjab, it was held that the historical background and, perhaps, what was accepted or what was rejected by the Constituent Assembly while the Constitution was being framed may be taken into account in finding out the scope and extent of Article 368.
-Parliamentary history means the includes conception of an idea, drafting of the bill, the debates made, the amendments proposed, speech made by mover of the bill, etc. Papers placed before the cabinet which took the decision for the introduction of the bill are not relevant since these papers are not placed before the parliament.
-The Supreme Court in S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab and others  has stated that it is a settled position that debates in the Constituent Assembly may be relied upon as an aid to interpret a Constitutional provision because it is the function of the Court to find out the intention of the framers of the Constitution.
But as far as speeches in Parliament are concerned, a distinction is made between speeches of the mover of the Bill and speeches of other Members.
-Regarding speeches made by the Members of the Parliament at the time of consideration of a Bill, it has been held in K.S. Paripoornan v. State of Kerala and others that they are not admissible as extrinsic aids to the interpretation of the statutory provision. However, speeches made by the mover of the Bill or Minister may be referred to for the purpose of finding out the object intended to be achieved by the Bill.
  •  Debates & proceedings of the legislature
-Speeches of Members of Parliament are recognized as external aids to construction. They are in a similar position to Constituent Assembly Debates. The reports of commissions and enquiry committees, which precede the introduction of a bill, have been used for interpretation.
Indira Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)
In this case, it was observed that constituent assembly debates are external aids. They are not conclusive for interpretation of any expressions. It held that the court cannot ignore the present by going into the past.
State of Travancore v. Bombay Co. Ltd.
In this case it was held that a speech made in the course of the debate on a bill could at the best be indicator of the subjective intent of the speakers but it could not reflect the inarticulate mental process lying behind the majority vote which carried the bill. Nor is it reasonable to assume that the minds of all those legislators were in accord.
  •  Historical facts & surrounding circumstances
-The external aid in the form of historical facts and circumstances surrounding the legislation is necessary and quite useful in understanding the subject matter of the statute and thereby, to arrive at the legislative intent. [D.K. Trivedi & Sons v. State of Gujrat (1986)]
-If the language of the statute is clear and unambiguous, there is no need to refer to any external aid; consequently, the historical facts and surrounding circumstances must give way to the clear language of the statue. [CIT v. Sodra Devi (1957)]
  •  Social, economic & political developments
-The courts are free to apply the current meaning of the statute to the present-day conditions.
State v. S.J. Choudhary (1996)
In this case it was held that the legislature is presumed to anticipate the temporal development and to intend the act to be applied in such a way as to give effect to the tone intention. The statute should be construed as to continuously update the wordings in accordance with the changes in social conditions, Science and Technology. In this case expert opinion about the typewriter on which the questioned document was typed was in question. Since typing had become more common than handwriting and this change is on account of the availability of type writers and their common use, much after the statute was enacted in 1872, it was held that, the opinion of the typewriter expert in this context is admissible under Section 45, Evidence Act, 1872.
-A Statute must be interpreted to include circumstances or situations which were unknown or did not exist at the time of enactment of the statute. Any relevant changes in the social conditions and technology should be given due weightage.
In S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, it was stated – “The interpretation of every statutory provision must keep pace with changing concepts and values and it must, to the extent to which its language permits or rather does not prohibit, suffer adjustments through judicial interpretation so as to accord with the requirement of the fast changing society which is undergoing rapid social and economic transformation … It is elementary that law does not operate in a vacuum. It is, therefore, intended to serve a social purpose and it cannot be interpreted without taking into account the social, economic and political setting in which it is intended to operate. It is here that the Judge is called upon to perform a creative function. He has to inject flesh and blood in the dry skeleton provided by the legislature and by a process of dynamic interpretation, invest it with a meaning which will harmonise the law with the prevailing concepts and values and make it an effective instrument for delivery of justice.”[21]
-Therefore, court has to take into account social, political and economic developments and scientific inventions which take place after enactment of a statute for proper construction of its provision.
  •  Reference to other statutes
-For the purpose of interpretation or construction of a statutory provision, courts can refer to or can take help of other statutes. It is also known as statutory aids.
e.g. the General Clauses Act, 1897.
-The application of this rule helped to avoid any contradiction between a series of statutes dealing with the same subject as it allows the use of an earlier statute to throw light on the meaning of a phrase used in a later statute in the same context.
i. Statutes "in pari materia"
-Statutes dealing with the same subject matter or forming part of the same system are known as statutes in pari materia. According to Sutherland:
-Statutes are considered to be in pari materia to pertain to the same subject matter when they relate to the same persons or thing or have the same purpose or object.
In Nagpur Improvement Trust v. Vasantrao, the schedules to Punjab Town Improvement Act, 1922 and Nagpur Improvement Trust Act, 1936 though not identical, have been held to be in pari materia.
ii. Assistance of latter statutes
-A later statute is normally not used as an aid to construction of an earlier statute.
-If the subsequent legislation proceeds upon an erroneous construction of previous legislation, it cannot alter that previous legislation, but if there is any ambiguity in the earlier legislation, then the subsequent legislation may fix the proper interpretation which is to be put upon the earlier. [Cape Brandy Syndicate v. IRC (1921)]
iii. Incorporation/Reference of earlier statute
Ichchapur Industrial Coop. Society Ltd. v. Competent Authority, ONGC (1997)
In this case it was held that, legislation by incorporation or by reference is a legislative device adopted for the sake of convenience in order to avoid verbatim reproduction of the provisions of the earlier act into the latter.
  •  Contemporanea exposition
-The maxim means best exposition of a statute or any other document is that which it has received from contemporary authority. In
Senior Electric Inspector v. Laxmi Narayan, the Supreme Court did not accept the meaning of the term ‘telegraph line’ used in old statute. It is because this term in old law does not include wireless communications. In 
Raja ram v. Bihar, the court refused to apply the maxim and held that with passage of time an excise officer is a police officer within the meaning of S.25 of Evidence Act and hence old meaning is inapplicable.
M/s J.K. Mills ltd. v. Union of India, the maxim is applicable in construction of ancient statutes but not in interpreting Acts which are comparatively modern statutes.
  •  Other external aids – codifying & consolidating statutes
i. Codifying statutes
-The purpose of a codifying statute is to present an orderly and authoritative statement of the leading rules of law on a given subject, whether those rules are to be found in statute law or common law. 
ii. Consolidating Statutes
-Consolidation is an Act which brings together statutory provisions contained in number of existing statutes, without altering the substance of the law.
-The purpose of consolidating statutes is to present the whole body of statutory law on a subject in complete form repeating the former statute. Codification is the process of incorporation of the law on a particular topic or subject, in statutes.
  •  International conventions
-Bennion states that an international treaty may have three different kinds of status: a domestic Act may directly enact the treaty, or indirectly may do so, or the treaty may remain as only an international obligation. Lord Wilberforce in Buchanan & Co. v Babco Ltd described the interpretation of a treaty imported into domestic law by indirect enactment as "unconstrained by technical rules of English law or by English legal precedent, but on broad principles of general acceptation"  In Salomon v Commissioners of Customs & Excise the Court of Appeal held that where there was cogent extrinsic evidence that an enactment was intended to implement the government's obligations under a convention, then the court may look at the convention in elucidating the Act, although the Act nowhere makes mention of the treaty.
-However, reference can only be made to the convention to resolve ambiguities, or obscurities of language, where the terms of the legislation are not clear. If the terms are not clear, but are reasonably capable of more than one meaning, then the construction which is consonant with the treaty obligations is to be preferred. This is in accordance with the prima facie presumption that Parliament does not intend to act in breach of international law, including specific treaty obligations.

  •  Committee report
-Other than Internal Aids to Interpretation which are parts of the statute itself there are other aids which are not parts of the statute. In
  R. S. Nayak v. A. R. Antulay, the court held that report of the committee, reports of parliamentary committee & reports of commission are permissible external aids to construction.
-The reports of committee is also considered as one of the external aids in
G. Sekar v. Geetha, the Supreme Court held that report of the Law Commission of India may be looked into for the purpose of construction of statute. But the same would not prevail over clear and unambiguous provision contained therein.

  •  Dictionary
-Words used in a statute should be interpreted in the light of ordinary sense. To find out ordinary sense of the words, dictionaries can naturally be of help. In
R v. Peters, it was held that dictionaries are not to be taken as authorative exponents of the meanings of words used in Act of Parliament. But it is well known rule of courts of law that words should be taken to be used in their ordinary sense & we are therefore rely on dictionaries.
-Whenever the need arises to know the ordinary sense of a word, the courts must be careful to take dictionary meaning because it is not always that it provides true meaning in a particular context. In
K.B. Rohmare v. Shanker rao, in this case a member of sugar industry was alleged to be holding an office of profit as he was being paid an honorarium & hence incapable of holding election. The court held that dictionary meaning of honorarium was not of much consequence & it was not an office of profit.
-In case of doubt the court may refer dictionary of standard authors and well-known dictionaries.
-Diverse meanings of words are given in a dictionary. It is very difficult for court to choose the correct meaning. Then court may refer circumstances in which the word is used. In
  State of U.P. v. Kores (India) ltd. in this case the issue before the court was that whether carbon paper is comes under the meaning of word ‘paper’ & need to be taxed. The court held that carbon is a special kind of paper & it cannot be taxed under the said notification.
-When words are not defined in an act itself it is permissible to look into a dictionary to find out the general sense of the word.
-When a plain meaning of the provision brings out what was intended, dictionary meaning is not to be considered. [State of Maharashtra v. Praful B. Desai (2003)
  •  Text book
-Text books may be referred to by the courts to arrive at the true meaning of an enactment. It is not necessary that views in books are the views of courts also.
-There have been instances of both acceptance and rejection by the courts of the opinions expressed in books.
E.g. - Manu, Yajanvalkya, Vijnaneshwar, Jimutvahan, Kautilya & Mulla have been frequently quoted by courts.
- In the landmark judgement of
Keshavanand Bharati v. Kerala, a large number of text books were quoted but most of the judges are of the opinion that the court has to interpret keeping in mind always the whole context of the issues.
The Privy Council in Collector of Madura v. Mootoo Ramalinga Sethupathi (1868), observed: "according to the established rules in our courts, it is not open to the court to go back to the old text in order to see whether the interpretations placed on them by the recognised commentators are correct or not."
-It is the discretion of the courts to accept or reject the meaning given in the textbooks.
  •  Historical background
-The court is at liberty to look into the history of the law & legislation & to seek help from other historical facts to get the true meaning of an enactment. In
Express newspaper pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, the Supreme Court clearly stated that history of legislation and other like external sources may be looked into by a court in case of ambiguity. 
-The court should be careful while interpreting words & it may not infer what perhaps in the mind of legislature.
-The subject matter of law & facts existing at the time has to be considering finding out object and purpose of the legislature. In
Hariprasad v. Divakar, the court accepted that meaning of ‘retrenchment’, that is to say, discharge of surplus staff in a running industry.

  •  Legislative history
-The modern views seem to be that it is not permissible as an aid to interpretation. Because whatever was in mind of the legislators has been already expressed through words. But in
  A. K. Gopalan v. Madras, the court disallowing a speech to be considered as an aid made in the course of the debate on a bill could be best indicative of intent. It also not reasonable to assume that it reflects minds of all those who voted in majority. 
-The legislative debates on the floor of the houses, reports of the select committees & statements of objects & reasons are inadmissible aids. In
  Aswini Kumar v. Arbinda Bose, the statements of objects & reasons disclose the circumstances and things to be achieved under a bill. Hence, it cannot be taken as aid because it may control the meaning of the actual words used therein. In 
  S. P. Gupta v. President of India, court observed that legislative history of a constitutional provision used in exceptional cases to find out the true meaning of words if words used are unclear in nature.

  •  Speech
-It is said that speeches in constituent assembly helps to find out true intention of makers of Indian constitution but it does not apply to other statutes. In 
  Keshavanand Bharati v. Kerala, it was held that speeches made by members of a legislature in the course of debates relating to law cannot be used as aids for interpreting any provisions therein.
-The question of interpretation of expression ‘backward class of citizens’ used in Art-16(4) of Indian constitution solved in
  Indira sawhney v. Union of India, it was held that it is permissible to refer to the speech of Dr. B. R. Ambedkar to understand the context, background and object behind such expression.

  •  Object & reason
-Statement of objects & reasons can be used for understanding the background. In
  M. Ismail Faruqqui v. Union of India, it was held that white paper issued by government stating detailed facts helps understand the background of the Ram janmabhumi- Babri masjid dispute. In
  S. S. Bola v. B. D. Sardana, the court held that if words used in a statute are clear and unambiguous showing the intention of the legislature, then court shall not take assistance of objects and reasons to find out the meaning of words used therein.

  •  Recommendation of Law commission
-The court can look into the recommendations of the Law commission while interpreting a provision which has been amended on its basis. In
  M. Kumari v. P. B. Khare, the reports of law commission has to be referred. It is depend on the facts and circumstances of each case.

  •  Previous legislation
-Where a code consolidates the law on a particular subject in such cases history of previous Act may be looked at to determine the construction in case of ambiguity. In
  Gulab chand v. Kudilal, where a statute is only modifying the form of any previous law and to determine whether it is retrospective in operation, it may be necessary to refer to the prior state of law.

  •  Previous decision
-It is fundamental principle that doctrine of precedent has to be followed to bring equality in decision making. The only ratio decidendi that has the force of law. In
  Greaves v. tofield, if an Act of Parliament uses the same language which was used in a former Act referring same subject, then the safe rule of constructions is to uses those words in the sense which the decisions have attached to them.

  •  Foreign decision
-Foreign decisions are not helpful in interpreting post-independence laws.
E.g. - the Special marriage Act, 1954 made by Indian Parliament after independence and it cannot be interpreted by English decisions.
The Supreme Court is not bound by decisions of foreign courts. [M.C Mehta v. Union of India]
Foreign decisions and textbooks have only persuasive value. [General Electric Co. v. Union of India (1987)]
Foreign decisions as observed in B.R. Enterprises v. State of UP (1999), can be referred to, to take stock of the view as to with what vision the judges judged and what they meant and understood, while dealing with the same subject matter as is concerned in the statute to be interpreted.
  •  Acts in pari materia
-If words used in different statutes with the same subject-matter can be referred in interpretation of words used in those statutes. In
  Ram Kishore v. Shyam Sunder Lal, where there are different statutes in pari materia, though made at different times, they ought to receive a uniform construction.

  •  Public policy
-Legislation is intended to follow the principles of public policy & not to transgress it. In
J. D. Bhate v. The commissioner, Nagpur, the scope of the expression ‘public purpose’ is obviously not static and must change with varying concept, time, state of society and its needs. 

22) Practice – Conveyance, administrative
-Generally administrative practice is not recognised as an aid to interpretation. In
  State v. Chhadami lal, the court held that in case of a clash between a law and an administrative practice, the law is bound to prevail.
-Practice of eminent conveyance has frequently been given much respect by the courts. In
Pilkington v. I. R.C. the court accepted the views and practice of eminent conveyance while referring that it helps to interpret provisions correctly.
23) Change in Social Conditions
Anuj Garg v. Hotel Assn. Of India (1930)
In this case under Section 30, Punjab Excise Act, 1914, the Act prohibited the employment of any man or woman under 25 years of age to be employed in any part of premises wherein intoxicating drugs were consumed by public. But having regard to the present social conditions and equality of men and women under the constitution, the same was declared invalid. In the same way change in social attitude has brought about a change in the interpretation of the word "person". In some European countries the word person did not include a woman but in 1929, the Privy Council included a woman in the word person and this made her competent to vote.
24) Doctrine of "stare decisis"
The expression means to abide by the decisions. Article 141 of the Constitution recognizes this Doctrine. It is binding on all Courts in India except the Supreme Court because the Supreme Court of India is free to depart from its previous decisions for valid reasons, like the House of Lords in England. [Bengal Immunity Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1955)]
According to this rule, the principle of the law which has become settled by a series of decisions, is generally binding on the courts and should be followed in similar cases. This rule is based on expediency and public policy.
