STATUTE AFFECTING JURISDICTION OF COURTS

INTRODUCTION
Jurisdiction isn’t merely a procedural detail; it’s the cornerstone of due process, ensuring cases are heard by the appropriate court. This involves understanding three core types: territorial jurisdiction (location of the cause of action or defendant’s residence), subject-matter jurisdiction (the type of case a court can hear), and pecuniary jurisdiction (monetary value of the claim).
The Indian Constitution and statutes like the Civil Procedure Code (CPC) and the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) allocate jurisdictional powers among various courts, from the Supreme Court to District Courts. The concept of exclusion of jurisdiction, where a statute explicitly or implicitly restricts a court’s power, is equally crucial. Presumptions regarding jurisdiction, where jurisdiction is assumed unless challenged, also play a significant role in litigation strategy.
SPECIFIC STATUTES AFFECTING JURISDICTION: A DETAILED ANALYSIS
The Civil Procedure Code (CPC), 1908: The Foundation of Civil Jurisdiction
The CPC is the cornerstone of civil court jurisdiction. Sections 15-20 define territorial jurisdiction, while pecuniary jurisdiction determines which court level can hear a case based on the claim’s value. Subject-matter jurisdiction allocates cases based on the nature of the dispute. Landmark cases interpreting these provisions offer crucial insights into their practical application.
1. Territorial Jurisdiction: This type of jurisdiction decides where a case should be filed. As per Section 16 of the CPC, lawsuits involving immovable property must be filed in the court where the property is located. However, there are exceptions for contract-related disputes or cases involving multiple locations. Ensuring the right court hears a case prevents unnecessary delays and inconvenience to the parties involved.
2. Pecuniary Jurisdiction: Not all courts handle cases of the same financial scale. Pecuniary jurisdiction ensures that cases are assigned to courts based on their monetary value, preventing small cases from being heard in higher courts and vice versa. This system helps distribute the judicial workload fairly and avoids unnecessary burdens on lower courts.
3. Subject-Matter Jurisdiction: Different courts specialize in different types of cases. Family courts handle matrimonial disputes, while commercial courts deal with business-related issues. Ensuring cases are heard in the right courts prevents misinterpretation of legal provisions and improves efficiency.
4. Original and Appellate Jurisdiction: Original jurisdiction means a court has the power to hear a case first-hand. In contrast, appellate jurisdiction allows higher courts to review decisions made by lower courts. While subordinate courts usually handle original cases, higher courts—like High Courts and the Supreme Court—mainly deal with appeals, ensuring errors are corrected and justice is served.
Indian courts have played a significant role in clarifying jurisdictional rules. Some key cases include:
K.K. Modi v. K.N. Modi (1998): Established that civil courts cannot interfere in matters governed by special laws unless explicitly allowed.
Dhannalal v. Kalawatibai (2002): Affirmed that civil courts have jurisdiction unless explicitly barred by law.
State of Tamil Nadu v. Ramalinga Samigal Madam (1985): Clarified that revenue courts, not civil courts, have jurisdiction over land revenue disputes.
The Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), 1973: Exploring Criminal Jurisdiction
The CrPC governs criminal court jurisdiction. Sections 177-188 define territorial limits based on the offense location, while subject-matter jurisdiction is determined by the crime’s nature and severity. The CrPC also outlines procedures for transferring cases between courts when jurisdictional issues arise.
Territorial and Subject-Matter Jurisdiction: The CrPC primarily determines jurisdiction based on the location of the offense and the nature of the crime. Understanding these distinctions is paramount for initiating proceedings in the correct court.
Concurrent and Exclusive Jurisdiction: The CrPC addresses situations where multiple courts might have concurrent jurisdiction or where a specific court has exclusive jurisdiction over particular offenses.
Special Statutes and Their Impact: Carving Out Specific Jurisdictions
Specialized statutes often create unique jurisdictional arrangements, deviating from general principles. These statutes often establish specialized courts or tribunals with exclusive jurisdiction over specific matters. Understanding these provisions is essential for professionals dealing with disputes under these legal frameworks.
The Companies Act, 2013: This Act designates the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) as primary adjudicatory bodies for corporate disputes, limiting the role of traditional civil courts.
The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: While not directly defining court jurisdiction, this Act impacts it by providing for judicial oversight of arbitral awards, making it vital for handling arbitration-related disputes.
The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016: This Code designates the NCLT as the primary adjudicating authority for insolvency proceedings, further impacting the jurisdiction of other courts in related matters.
GENERAL PRINCIPLES
Defining Jurisdiction: Territorial, Subject-Matter, and Pecuniary
Jurisdiction, the authority of a court to adjudicate, is a multifaceted concept. Territorial jurisdiction defines the geographical area over which a court exercises power. Subject-matter jurisdiction concerns the types of cases a court can hear, such as civil, criminal, or family matters.
Pecuniary jurisdiction relates to the monetary value of the claim, determining which court level has competence. The allocation of jurisdictional powers among the Supreme Court, High Courts, District Courts, and specialized tribunals is primarily determined by the Constitution and statutes like the CPC and CrPC.
The Extent of Exclusion: Explicit and Implicit Limitations
Exclusive jurisdiction grants sole authority to specific courts or tribunals over particular types of cases. Statutory exclusion of jurisdiction must be explicit or clearly implied through judicial interpretation. The Family Courts Act, 1984, exemplifies explicit exclusion, barring civil courts from matters handled by family courts. Implicit exclusions arise when statutes implicitly limit jurisdiction by creating specialized tribunals with comprehensive powers. The burden of proving exclusion rests on the party asserting it.
Exclusion must be explicitly expressed or clearly implied
 The provisions excluding jurisdiction of civil courts and provisions conferring jurisdiction on authorities other than civil courts are strictly construed.
 There is a strong presumption that civil courts have jurisdiction to decide all questions of civil nature and exclusion of jurisdiction of civil courts is not to be readily inferred and such exclusion must either be explicitly expressed or clearly implied.
 The rule that the exclusion of jurisdiction of civil courts is not to be readily inferred is based on the theory that civil courts are courts of general jurisdiction and the people have a right, unless expressly or impliedly debarred to insist for free access to the courts of general jurisdiction of the state.
 Principles as to jurisdiction of courts not limited to civil courts alone, but applies to all courts of general jurisdiction including criminal courts.

Presumptions Regarding Jurisdiction: The Default Position
Generally, courts are presumed to possess jurisdiction unless specifically challenged. This presumption is strong for courts of general jurisdiction like District Courts but weaker for specialized tribunals. The burden of proving jurisdiction shifts to the plaintiff when a court’s competence is questionable, highlighting the need for diligent research into applicable statutes and case law.

Categorizing Jurisdictional Disputes: Three Key Classes
Jurisdictional disputes often fall into three categories: concurrent remedies (where both statutory and common law remedies exist), implicit common law remedies (where a right is conferred without a specified remedy), and exclusive statutory remedies (where the statute provides the sole remedy). The Supreme Court’s decision in Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. Kamalakar Shantaram Wadke (1973) exemplifies the concept of exclusive statutory remedies.
Breach of Statutory Duties and Nullity: Jurisdictional Considerations
Breaches of statutory duties raise complex jurisdictional questions, particularly regarding whether a statutory duty creates a private cause of action. The presence of a penalty clause doesn’t automatically negate civil liability. The question of nullity, where a tribunal’s decision is challenged, involves examining whether the tribunal acted ultra vires or violated principles of natural justice.
The Rule of Conclusive Evidence: Impact on Jurisdictional Determinations
The doctrine of conclusive evidence impacts jurisdiction by declaring certain facts as conclusively proven, removing them from judicial scrutiny. This affects jurisdictional determinations if the conclusive evidence directly impacts the court’s ability to determine its jurisdiction.

Statutes affecting ‘Judicial Review’
 Judicial review has been held to be a part of the basic structure of the Indian Constitution.
 In India some of the statute excludes the judicial review concept through incorporation of the word ‘Statutory Finality’.
Supreme Court on statute affecting judicial review  Kihota Hollohan v. Zachilhu, AIR 1993 SC.
Owing to large scale defections in the Indian legislature, lawmakers introduced Anti-Defection Law vide the Constitution (Fifty Second Amendment) Act, 1985. Paragraph 7 of Schedule 10 provides
“Notwithstanding anything contained in the constitution, no court has any jurisdiction in respect of any matter connected with the disqualification of a member of a House on ground of defection”
Court observed that if the power of judicial review is weakened, then it would adversely affect the system of justice whereby tribunals and other quasi- judicial bodies could violate rules at will.
The purpose of finality clause was clarified to prevent any further appeal but its effect on the power of judicial review remains unfettered.
State of Rajasthan v. Union of India, AIR 1977 SC
Article 356(5) provides that order passed by the President under Art. 356 complete immunity from judicial scrutiny.
Held: Reviewable by the Courts if it was based on irrelevant considerations, ulterior motives or was mala fide.
N. P Ponnuswami v. The Returning officer, AIR 1951
No election shall be called in question except by an election petition’’ in Article 329 (b), and the point to be decided is whether questioning the action of the Returning Officer in rejecting a nomination paper can be challenged only after the culmination of election process.
HM Trivedi v. VVB Raju, AIR 1973 SC
It was held that entry of a person’s name in the electoral roll of a constituency prepared under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, could neither be challenged before the civil court nor before an election tribunal on the ground that the person concerned was not ordinary resident in that constituency.
It was held that the authorities under the Act could conclusively decide the question of ordinary residence.
Union of India v. Jyoti Prakash Mitter AIR 1971 1093 SC.
The question of jurisdiction of Higher Courts were raised again in this case. When the finality of the President’s decision with regard to question of age of a judge arose.
Article 217(3) specified that the President’s order shall be final with regard to the determination of age of a judge of a High Court and excluded it from the purview of judicial review.
The Supreme Court discussed the effect of the President’s decision on the independence of Judiciary and observed that “Notwithstanding the declared finality of the order of the President the court has jurisdiction in appropriate cases to set aside the order, if it appears that it was passed on collateral considerations or the rule of natural justice were not observed, or that the president’s judgment was coloured by the advice or representation made by the executive or it was founded on no evidence.
CONCLUSION
The concept of jurisdiction is vital for maintaining the orderly functioning of the judiciary. Under the CPC, various types of jurisdictions—territorial, pecuniary, subject-matter, and others—ensure that cases are heard by the appropriate court. Understanding these different jurisdictions helps litigants avoid procedural pitfalls and ensures that disputes are resolved by the correct forum. By following the provisions laid out in the CPC, the legal system maintains its efficiency and fairness in administering justice.

DOCTRINES
INTRODUCTION 
Constitutional doctrines serve as fundamental principles that guide the interpretation and application of the Indian Constitution. These doctrines have evolved through judicial pronouncements and provide essential frameworks for resolving constitutional disputes.  
DOCTRINE OF SEVERABILITY 
Also known as the doctrine of separability, it protects fundamental rights by invalidating only the unconstitutional portions of a law while preserving the valid parts. The entire law is not struck down unless valid and invalid parts are inseparably connected. 
  •  In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), the Court held that only disputed provisions should be void, not the entire Act.  
  •  In State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951), eight sections of the Bombay Prohibition Act were declared invalid while the rest remained operative. 
  •  General Motors Traders v. State of Andhra Pradesh: Only the repugnant provisions were voided.
  •  Exception: When valid and invalid provisions are inseparable, the entire statute is void. For instance, in Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras, a broad law restricting freedom of speech was struck down entirely because its unconstitutional and constitutional parts were inseparable.
Related Articles: Based on Article 13(1), which renders laws inconsistent with fundamental rights void to the extent of inconsistency. 
DOCTRINE OF ECLIPSE 
This doctrine applies when laws violate fundamental rights, rendering them unenforceable but not void ab initio. Such laws remain dormant against citizens but continue to operate against non-citizens who lack fundamental rights protection. 
  •  First introduced in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), where a law empowering government takeover of motor transport business was held eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g). Pre-constitutional laws violating Fundamental Rights were considered dormant and revived after the inconsistency was addressed.
  •  Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay: The doctrine applies only to pre-constitutional laws.
  •  Limitation: Post-constitutional laws violating Fundamental Rights are void ab initio, as held in Deep Chand v. State of UP.
Related Articles: Contained in Article 13(1) and applies only to pre-constitutional laws. 



DOCTRINE OF TERRITORIAL NEXUS 
This doctrine establishes that state laws generally apply within state boundaries unless there's sufficient connection between the state and the subject matter. It prevents arbitrary extraterritorial application of state laws. 
  •  In A.H. Wadia v. Income Tax Commissioner (1948), the Court held that extraterritoriality cannot invalidate laws of supreme legislative authority. In State of Bombay v. RMDC (1952), sufficient territorial nexus was found to justify taxation. 
  •  Wallace Bros. v. Income Tax Commissioner: A foreign company with operations in India was taxed on its Indian income, as a sufficient nexus existed.

  •  State of Bombay v. RMDC: A tax imposed on a newspaper printed outside Bombay but circulated widely within the state was upheld due to its territorial connection.

Related Articles: Derives power from Article 245, which addresses territorial limits of legislative power. 
DOCTRINE OF COLOURABLE LEGISLATION 
Known as "Fraud on the Constitution," this doctrine prevents indirect legislation on subjects outside legislative competence. It ensures legislatures cannot achieve through indirect means what they cannot do directly. 
  •  In R.S. Joshi v. Ajit Mills (1977), the Supreme Court observed that colourable legislation occurs when a legislature lacks competence but attempts to legislate by assuming the appearance of competency. 
  •  K.C.G. Narayan Dev v. State of Orissa: The court scrutinised legislative intent to evaluate the validity of a law.

  •  State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh: The Bihar Land Reforms Act was invalidated as it bypassed constitutional compensation requirements, reflecting indirect legislative overreach.

Related Articles: Usually applied to Article 246, which demarcates legislative competence through the three lists. 
DOCTRINE OF PITH AND SUBSTANCE 
This doctrine determines the true nature and essential character of legislation when there's a question about legislative competence. Courts examine the substance rather than the form of the law to determine its validity. 
  •  In Prafulla v. Bank of Commerce (1946), The Bengal Money Lenders Act, though incidentally affecting promissory notes (a Union subject), was upheld as its main subject was money lending (a State subject). The Supreme Court held that a state law on money lending remains valid even if it incidentally affects promissory notes. 
  •  State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla: Regulation of amplifiers, though linked to communication (a Union subject), was upheld as it primarily addressed public health and noise pollution (State subjects).

Related Articles: Applied under Article 246 (legislative powers) and Article 254 (repugnancy between central and state laws). 
DOCTRINE OF WAIVER
The Doctrine of Waiver refers to the deliberate relinquishment of a right or advantage. However, in India, Fundamental Rights cannot be waived as they are considered essential for public welfare and constitutional integrity.
  •  Definition: Individuals cannot waive their Fundamental Rights, as these are guaranteed for societal benefit.
  •  Basheshar Nath v. Income Tax Commissioner: The Supreme Court ruled that Fundamental Rights cannot be waived, as they are designed to protect public interests.
  •  Mutiah M. Ct v. Commissioner of Income Tax: Fundamental Rights serve as a safeguard against individuals’ unawareness or lack of resources, emphasising their non-negotiable nature.
DOCTRINE OF LACHES
The Doctrine of Laches discourages delayed legal claims, promoting timely action to ensure justice and prevent prejudice to the opposing party.
  •  Definition: Courts may deny relief for undue delay in claiming rights, as “equity aids the vigilant, not those who slumber on their rights.”
  •  Ravindra Jain v. Union of India: The Supreme Court ruled that remedies under Article 32 could be denied if the petitioner delayed unreasonably.
  •  Significance: By encouraging prompt action, this doctrine ensures fairness in legal proceedings and prevents misuse of judicial resources.
DOCTRINE OF PLEASURE 
  •  Originating from English law, this doctrine allows the President or Governor to dismiss civil servants at pleasure. However, constitutional safeguards prevent arbitrary dismissal. 
  •  In State of Bihar v. Abdul Majid (1954), the Court clarified that English common law wasn't adopted entirely.  
  •  In Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1965), the doctrine was held to be based on public policy rather than feudal concepts. 
Related Articles: Article 155 (Governor's tenure), Article 310 (civil servants' tenure), and Article 311 (restrictions and safeguards). 
DOCTRINE OF REPUGNANCY
The Doctrine of Repugnancy addresses inconsistencies between laws enacted by Union and State legislatures on matters listed in the Concurrent List. When such conflicts arise, Union law prevails.
  •  Definition: If a State law conflicts with a Union law on a Concurrent List subject, the Union law supersedes the State law under Article 254.
  •  Zaveri Bhai v. State of Bombay: The Bombay legislature enhanced penalties under the Essential Supplies Act. When Parliament amended the Act with different penalties, the State law was repealed due to repugnancy.
  •  Exceptions:
  •  If the State law receives Presidential assent, it prevails within the state. However, Parliament retains the power to override it through subsequent legislation.
  •  Significance: This doctrine ensures uniformity in critical areas while allowing states some legislative flexibility under specific conditions.
DOCTRINE OF JUDICIAL REVIEW
The Doctrine of Judicial Review empowers the judiciary to assess the constitutionality of laws and executive actions, ensuring compliance with constitutional principles.
  •  Definition: Courts can invalidate laws and actions that violate constitutional provisions, acting as guardians of the Constitution.
  •  Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala: Judicial review was upheld as part of the Constitution’s basic structure, preventing its alteration by constitutional amendments.
  •  Significance: This doctrine ensures that laws and executive actions align with constitutional mandates, protecting citizens’ rights and preserving the Constitution’s supremacy.
DOCTRINE OF BASIC STRUCTURE 
The Basic Structure Doctrine establishes that while Parliament has the power to amend the Constitution, it cannot alter its fundamental framework. The constituents include parliamentary democracy, fundamental rights, secularism, federalism, and judicial review. 
  •  In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court first ruled that Parliament cannot alter the basic structure of the Constitution.  
  •  This doctrine was reaffirmed in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), where the Court invalidated provisions of the 39th Amendment Act that placed election disputes involving the Prime Minister outside judicial jurisdiction. 
  •  Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Reinforced that Parliament cannot destroy or damage the Constitution’s basic features while amending it.
Related Articles: The doctrine operates as an implied limitation on Article 368 (amendment power). 
DOCTRINE OF SEPARATION OF POWERS 
This doctrine signifies the division of powers among the executive, legislature, and judiciary, ensuring that no single organ dominates the others. It prevents concentration of power and maintains checks and balances in governance. 
  •  In Ram Jawaya v. State of Punjab (1955), the Supreme Court held that while the Indian Constitution doesn't recognize absolute separation of powers, functions are sufficiently differentiated.  
  •  In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), the Court declared separation of powers as part of the basic structure. The Supreme Court held that adjudication of disputes is exclusively a judicial function, which the legislature cannot usurp.
  •  Golaknath v. State of Punjab: Jurisdictional boundaries of the three branches were demarcated to prevent overreach.

Related Articles: Article 50 of the Directive Principles mandates separation of judiciary from executive in public services. 
DOCTRINE OF INCIDENTAL OR ANCILLARY POWERS 
This doctrine allows legislatures to make laws on matters incidental to their primary legislative subjects. It supplements the Doctrine of Pith and Substance by addressing the power to legislate on connected matters. 
  •  In State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1958), the Supreme Court stated that the power to legislate on a topic includes power to legislate on reasonably ancillary matters. 
  •  R.M.D. Charbaugwala v. State of Mysore: Gambling is a state subject, but the court held that the power to tax gambling activities is separate and not incidental.
  •  Prafulla Kumar v. Bank of Commerce: The doctrine was applied to recognise ancillary powers of the state legislature while addressing incidental effects on Union subjects.

Related Articles: Articles 4 and 169 provide specific instances of incidental powers. 
DOCTRINE OF HARMONIOUS CONSTRUCTION 
This doctrine requires interpreting seemingly contradictory provisions in a manner that gives effect to all provisions without rendering any redundant. It promotes constitutional harmony and coherence. 
  •  The doctrine evolved through Shankari Prasad v. Union of India,1951 and was systematized in CIT v. Hindustan Bulk Carriers (2003), which laid down five principles.  
  •  In Re-Kerala Education Bill (1951), the Court applied harmonious construction between fundamental rights and directive principles. 
  •  O.N. Mohindroo v. Bar Council of Delhi: Conflicting entries between the Union and Concurrent Lists regarding legal practice before the Supreme Court and High Courts were harmoniously interpreted to divide jurisdiction.
  •  Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala: The court harmonised the Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy, ensuring they coexist without undermining each other.

Related Articles: Applied across constitutional provisions to resolve apparent conflicts. 
CONCLUSION 
These constitutional doctrines form the bedrock of Indian constitutional jurisprudence, providing interpretive tools that maintain constitutional balance and protect democratic values. Through judicial evolution, these doctrines continue to adapt to contemporary challenges while preserving the Constitution's foundational principles. They demonstrate the dynamic nature of constitutional law and the judiciary's role in safeguarding constitutional supremacy.
The doctrines under the Indian Constitution are essential tools for upholding the rule of law and ensuring constitutional governance. They reflect the evolving nature of legal interpretations and the judiciary’s commitment to justice, equality, and democratic principles. Together, these doctrines form a robust framework for interpreting and applying the Constitution, ensuring its relevance in a changing socio-political landscape.
