# Landmark Cases — Interpretation of Statutes

## General rules and purpose

### *Heydon's Case* (1584) 3 Co Rep 7a
**Principle:** Four-part mischief inquiry: prior law, defect, remedy and the construction that suppresses mischief and advances remedy.

### *Kanai Lal Sur v. Paramnidhi Sadhukhan*, AIR 1957 SC 907
**Principle:** Begin with the statutory words; where language is clear, the court must give effect to it rather than substitute a different policy.

### *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills Ltd. v. Collector of Central Excise*, (1990) 3 SCC 447
**Principle:** Applies *noscitur a sociis* to read a word in the company it keeps, where context warrants.

### *Amar Chandra Chakraborty v. Collector of Excise*, (1972) 2 SCC 442
**Principle:** Explains the requirements and application of *ejusdem generis*.

### *CIT v. Hindustan Bulk Carriers*, (2003) 3 SCC 57
**Principle:** Interpretation should harmonise provisions and give effect to all parts of the statute where possible.

## Internal/external aids and legislative intent

### *K. P. Varghese v. ITO*, (1981) 4 SCC 173
**Principle:** Parliamentary material, including a Minister's speech, may be considered to understand the background and object where appropriate; it does not displace statutory words.

### *R. S. Nayak v. A. R. Antulay*, (1984) 2 SCC 183
**Principle:** Discusses limits and use of legislative history as an aid to construction.

## General Clauses Act and repeal

### *State of Punjab v. Mohar Singh Pratap Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84
**Principle:** GCA section 6 applies to repeal unless a different intention appears; repeal and re-enactment must be examined as a whole.

### *Fibre Boards (P) Ltd. v. CIT*, (2015) 10 SCC 333
**Principle:** Analyses the meaning of “repeal” and the interaction of the GCA with statutory deletion/amendment; cite with attention to the specific statutory context.

## Jurisdiction

### *Dhulabhai v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1969 SC 78
**Principle:** Sets out principles for determining when civil court jurisdiction is excluded by a special statute; exclusion is not lightly inferred, and adequacy of statutory remedies and the tribunal's compliance matter.

### *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261
**Principle:** Judicial review by High Courts under Articles 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Article 32 forms part of the Constitution's basic structure; tribunal decisions remain subject to constitutional review.

## Constitutional interpretation and precedent

### *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225
**Principle:** Parliament's power under Article 368 does not extend to destroying the Constitution's basic structure.

### *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India*, (1980) 3 SCC 625
**Principle:** Reaffirmed limited amending power and the importance of balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

### *I. C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643
**Principle:** Adopted prospective overruling in Indian constitutional adjudication; later constitutional doctrine developed through subsequent cases.

### *Bengal Immunity Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar*, AIR 1955 SC 661
**Principle:** Constitutional interpretation considered the structure and purpose of Article 286 and the mischief of multiple taxation on inter-State trade.

## Penal, taxing and remedial construction

### *Nathulal v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1966 SC 43
**Principle:** Mens rea is ordinarily presumed in criminal offences unless expressly or by necessary implication excluded.

### *State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George*, AIR 1965 SC 722
**Principle:** Context and statutory purpose may show that mens rea is excluded in a regulatory offence.

### *A. V. Fernandez v. State of Kerala*, AIR 1957 SC 657
**Principle:** Tax liability must be founded on clear charging language; no tax may be imposed by implication.

### *Commissioner of Customs v. Dilip Kumar & Co.*, (2018) 9 SCC 1
**Principle:** Clarifies interpretation of exemption notifications: claimant must establish eligibility, and genuine ambiguity in the exemption is resolved in favour of Revenue.

### *B. Shah v. Presiding Officer, Labour Court*, (1977) 4 SCC 384
**Principle:** A beneficial construction was adopted to advance the protective purpose of maternity benefit legislation.

### *S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. Neeta Bhalla*, (2005) 8 SCC 89
**Principle:** Criminal vicarious liability of company officers requires satisfaction of statutory conditions and specific pleadings; office alone is insufficient.

### *Sharif-ud-Din v. Abdul Gani Lone*, (1980) 1 SCC 403
**Principle:** Whether a statutory requirement is mandatory or directory depends on legislative intent, purpose and consequences, not wording alone.

## Use notes

Use cases for their specific holdings. Verify citations and exact propositions before reproducing in formal work; a case listed in a source handout may belong to a different syllabus or jurisdiction.
