Aim for a developed answer of about **450–650 words** (roughly 5–7 handwritten pages, depending on handwriting).
- **10 marks (Part B):** Give a brief introduction; state the key provisions; explain the principal elements/steps in numbered points; include **2–3 directly relevant authorities** where the topic has settled case law; finish with a concise conclusion. ## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain the meaning and purpose of statutory interpretation. Is it different from construction?

**Model answer — 15 marks**

Statutory interpretation is the judicial process of ascertaining the meaning and legal effect of enacted words in their context. It is necessary because language may be general, ambiguous, incomplete or applied to unforeseen facts. Interpretation seeks the meaning Parliament expressed; it does not authorise a court to replace the statute with its preferred policy.

The starting point is the text read as a whole. The court considers ordinary meaning, grammar, definitions, context, scheme and purpose. In *Kanai Lal Sur v Paramnidhi Sadhukhan*, AIR 1957 SC 907, the Supreme Court stressed that clear statutory language must be given effect. In *Padma Sundara Rao v State of Tamil Nadu*, (2002) 3 SCC 533, the Court cautioned that a court cannot supply a casus omissus except in a narrow case where it is necessary to make the statute workable and the legislative intention is clear. *Hindustan Bulk Carriers v State of U.P.*, (2003) 3 SCC 57, emphasises harmonious construction so provisions are not rendered redundant.

Writers sometimes distinguish interpretation (ascertaining the meaning of words) from construction (drawing legal consequences from the text in context). In practice, courts often use the terms together because determining meaning and applying it to facts are connected. Both begin with the enacted language and are limited by it. The literal, golden and mischief approaches, harmonious reading and statutory purpose are tools, not licences to legislate.

Article 141 makes law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India. Interpretation therefore requires both faithful reading of the statute and adherence to binding precedent.

**Conclusion.** The distinction can aid analysis, but should not be overstated. Interpretation and construction must remain anchored in statutory text, context and binding authority.

### Q2. Explain the mischief rule and its application in India.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

The mischief rule originates in *Heydon’s Case* (1584) 3 Co Rep 7a. It asks: (1) what was the law before the Act; (2) what defect or mischief existed; (3) what remedy Parliament chose; and (4) the reason for that remedy. The statute is then read to suppress the mischief and advance the remedy, consistently with the words used.

Indian courts use purpose and legislative context when text is ambiguous or a literal reading would defeat the statute’s evident object. In *Bengal Immunity Co. v State of Bihar*, AIR 1955 SC 661, the Supreme Court used the mischief rule in interpreting constitutional provisions on inter-State sales. In *K.P. Varghese v ITO*, (1981) 4 SCC 173, the Court considered the object and context of the Income-tax provision and rejected a literal construction that would produce an unjust and unintended result. Legislative history, including the Statement of Objects and Reasons, may assist in identifying the background but cannot override clear enacted language.

The rule is subject to limits: courts cannot add words merely because they consider the outcome desirable; purpose must be derived from the Act’s text, structure and admissible context. *Padma Sundara Rao v State of Tamil Nadu*, (2002) 3 SCC 533, reiterates that courts cannot fill every omission.

**Conclusion.** The mischief rule is a purposive aid used to resolve meaning in context. It advances the remedy chosen by the legislature without transferring legislative power to the court.

### Q3. Explain internal and external aids to interpretation.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

Internal aids are found within the statute. They include the long and short titles, preamble, headings, marginal notes, definitions, provisos, explanations, illustrations, schedules, punctuation and the scheme of sections. These aids show how provisions relate and may resolve ambiguity. A definition clause controls where applicable, but context may indicate a different meaning. A proviso ordinarily qualifies the main provision; it should not be read as an independent enactment unless the scheme requires it.

External aids come from outside the statute: dictionaries, earlier law, legislative history, Statement of Objects and Reasons, committee reports, parliamentary materials in appropriate cases, historical facts, contemporaneous administrative practice and international instruments where relevant. They assist in identifying context, the mischief or technical meaning. They cannot be used to contradict clear statutory text or to substitute a rejected proposal for enacted law.

The court first reads the statute as a whole and gives effect to ordinary meaning. Where genuine ambiguity remains, internal and then appropriate external aids may help. In *K.P. Varghese v ITO*, (1981) 4 SCC 173, the Court relied on context and legislative materials to understand the provision’s object. *R.S. Nayak v A.R. Antulay*, (1984) 2 SCC 183, addresses contextual interpretation of statutory language.

**Conclusion.** Aids support, rather than displace, the enacted text. The weight of each aid depends on the statute, the ambiguity and the question before the court.
## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. Explain commencement, operation and expiry/repeal of statutes under the General Clauses Act.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

Section 5 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 deals with commencement of Central Acts: a Central Act not expressed to commence on a particular day comes into operation on the day it receives Presidential assent, subject to any commencement provision. A statute may instead appoint a later date by notification. Commencement is the date it becomes law; operation concerns when its provisions apply to facts or rights, which may be prospective or, where clearly authorised, retrospective. Section 6 sets out the effect of repeal, preserving prior operation, accrued rights/liabilities, penalties and related proceedings unless a different intention appears. Section 6A addresses repeal of an amending Act in specified circumstances. Temporary statutes may expire by their own terms; repeal may be express or implied, but implied repeal is not favoured where both laws can operate together. *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84, explains section 6 and the presumption that accrued rights and liabilities survive repeal.

### Q5. Explain rules for interpreting penal and taxing statutes.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

Penal provisions are construed strictly: a person cannot be punished unless the conduct clearly falls within the statutory words. Strict construction does not require an artificially narrow reading; text, context and purpose still matter. Ambiguity in the definition of an offence or penalty is generally resolved in favour of the accused, but courts cannot ignore clear language. In *Tolaram Relumal v State of Bombay*, AIR 1954 SC 496, the Court applied the rule that where two reasonable constructions of a penal provision are possible, the one avoiding penalty is preferred.

Taxing statutes require clear authority for a charge. No tax can be imposed by implication; liability must fall within the charging provision. Exemptions and deductions are construed according to their wording and statutory scheme. *A.V. Fernandez v State of Kerala*, AIR 1957 SC 657, holds that tax liability must be established within the four corners of the law. The answer should distinguish charging provisions from machinery provisions and apply the relevant text.

### Q6. Distinguish express and implied repeal.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

Express repeal occurs when a later enactment expressly identifies and repeals an earlier Act or provision. Its effect is governed by the repealing clause and General Clauses Act section 6 unless a contrary intention appears. Implied repeal is inferred only where provisions of two enactments are so inconsistent or repugnant that they cannot operate together, or the later law clearly occupies the whole field. Courts presume against implied repeal and attempt harmonious construction. Section 6 preserves accrued rights, liabilities, penalties and proceedings after repeal unless the new law shows a contrary intention. *Municipal Council, Palai v T.J. Joseph*, AIR 1963 SC 1561, discusses inconsistency and implied repeal; *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84, explains the saving effect of section 6.
## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Ejusdem generis

**Model answer — 5 marks**

Where specific words of a class are followed by general words, the general words may be restricted to things of the same genus, if the list forms a distinct class and no contrary intention appears. It is a contextual rule, not automatic. *Amar Chandra Chakraborty v Collector of Excise*, (1972) 2 SCC 442.

### Noscitur a sociis

**Model answer — 5 marks**

A word is known by the company it keeps: surrounding words may help identify its intended meaning. The doctrine applies where context supports it and cannot defeat a clear statutory definition or purpose. *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills Ltd. v Collector of Central Excise*, (1990) 3 SCC 447.

### Reddendo singula singulis

**Model answer — 5 marks**

The rule means “referring each to each”: when a sentence lists multiple subjects and predicates, each may be matched with the appropriate corresponding subject. It is used to make grammatical sense of statutory text, subject to context and legislative intent.

### Retrospective operation

**Model answer — 5 marks**

A statute is presumed prospective unless the legislature clearly provides otherwise or the provision is procedural/clarificatory in a way recognised by law. A law creating a new offence or increasing punishment cannot retrospectively operate against an accused, under Article 20(1). Distinguish retrospective from retroactive effect on antecedent facts.
