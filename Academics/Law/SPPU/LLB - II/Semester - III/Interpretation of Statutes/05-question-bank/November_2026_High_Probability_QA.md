# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Q&A — Interpretation of Statutes (LCC 0703)

For a shorter recap of the same questions, see the [quick revision Q&A](November_2026_High_Probability_Quick_Revision_QA.md).

These are high-probability revision questions based on the SPPU syllabus, supplied notes and the April/October 2025 papers. They are predictions, not an official paper. Prepare every module. The model answers are written for the marks shown, not as short revision definitions.

## SPPU answer format and depth by marks

- **15 marks:** introduction (1), meaning/statutory framework (2), detailed rules and ingredients (6), exceptions/application (3), authorities and their principles (2), conclusion (1) — about 450–650 words.
- **10 marks:** introduction (1), governing rule/provision (2), explanation in 4–6 organized points (4), authority/application (2), conclusion (1) — about 300–450 words.
- **5 marks:** definition (1), governing rule (1), three or four essential points (2), authority/example and conclusion (1) — about 120–200 words.

Do not repeat section numbers in every paragraph. Mention the relevant provision once, then explain the doctrine in simple language while retaining correct legal terms.

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain literal, golden, mischief and harmonious construction.

> **Model answer — 15 marks**
>
> #### 1. Introduction
>
> Interpretation finds the legal meaning of enacted words; it is not permission to rewrite an Act.
>
> #### 2. Literal rule
>
> Clear words receive their ordinary grammatical meaning. *Kanai Lal Sur v Paramnidhi Sadhukhan*, AIR 1957 SC 907, confirms that clear language must be applied even if inconvenient.
>
> #### 3. Golden and mischief rules
>
> The **golden rule** permits a limited adjustment of ordinary meaning to avoid absurdity, contradiction or a result defeating the provision. The **mischief rule** comes from *Heydon’s Case* (1584): identify the previous law, the defect, the remedy and the reason for the remedy, then suppress the mischief and advance the remedy. In *K.P. Varghese v ITO*, (1981) 4 SCC 173, context and purpose prevented an unintended literal result.
>
> #### 4. Harmonious construction
>
> **Harmonious construction** reads apparently conflicting provisions together so that each has effect. One section should not be made useless. *CIT v Hindustan Bulk Carriers*, (2003) 3 SCC 57, supports this approach. Courts cannot supply every omission: *Padma Sundara Rao v State of Tamil Nadu*, (2002) 3 SCC 533, limits a **casus omissus** to a narrow, necessary situation.
>
>
> Under the literal rule, ordinary usage is only the starting point. A statutory definition governs where it applies, and words used in a specialised trade or legal setting may carry a technical meaning. Read the whole sentence, including qualifiers, and check whether adjoining provisions use the expression consistently. For example, if a licensing provision fixes a minimum age of twenty-one, the court must ordinarily apply that threshold even to an applicant who is only a day short. The inconvenience of a clear cutoff supplies no reason to change it.
>
> | Rule | Question the court asks | Illustration |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Literal | What meaning do the enacted words bear in context? | Apply an expressly fixed age requirement according to its terms. |
> | Golden | Would ordinary meaning produce a manifestly absurd result, and is another permissible reading available? | Read a reference to a notice served before a hearing in a way that gives effect to an express adjournment provision. |
> | Mischief | What defect in the prior law did Parliament address, and which available reading advances the remedy? | Where an anti-evasion provision reasonably bears two meanings, prefer the one that reaches the evasion it was enacted to prevent. |
> | Harmonious | Can apparently conflicting provisions operate together? | Read a general power of inspection subject to a specific protection for privileged documents. |
>
> The difference between the golden and mischief rules lies in the inquiry. The golden rule focuses on the consequence of a particular reading. The mischief inquiry looks back to the previous law and identifies the gap that prompted the legislation. In *K.P. Varghese*, the Court read the former tax provision in light of its aim of addressing understatement of consideration. A difference between market value and the declared consideration did not by itself establish understatement.
>
> Harmonious construction requires more than choosing whichever section seems more important. Consider their fields of operation, any express exception and any priority clause. In the inspection example, the general power still applies to ordinary documents, while the specific protection applies to privileged material. Each provision does work. If the language establishes an express priority, give that priority its defined effect. The sequence in an exam problem is to identify the disputed words, show the competing readings, explain the legal consequence of each, and justify the reading supported by the Act.
>
> #### 5. Conclusion
>
> Begin with the text, use context and purpose when genuine difficulty exists, and keep every construction within the enacted language.

### Q2. Explain internal and external aids to interpretation.

> **Model answer — 15 marks**
>
> #### 1. Introduction
>
> Aids are materials used by the court to understand legislative intention when the text requires context.
>
> #### 2. Internal aids
>
> Internal aids are within the Act: title, preamble, headings, definitions, sections read as a whole, provisos, explanations, illustrations, schedules, marginal notes and punctuation. A definition normally controls; a proviso generally qualifies the main provision; headings and the preamble may resolve ambiguity but cannot defeat clear operative words.
>
> #### 3. External aids
>
> External aids include earlier law, legislative history, Statement of Objects and Reasons, committee reports, parliamentary materials where admissible, dictionaries, historical and social conditions, other statutes and international conventions. They identify the mischief, background and technical meaning. They cannot contradict enacted words. *K.P. Varghese* used context and legislative material; *R.S. Nayak v A.R. Antulay*, (1984) 2 SCC 183, illustrates contextual reading.
>
>
> **Reading the operative text.** Start with the provision in dispute and the definition clause. A definition using “means” ordinarily fixes the scope of a term, whereas “includes” commonly enlarges it. Check whether the definitions are subject to a contrary context. A special definition in one chapter may apply only to that chapter. Related provisions can show whether a word refers to the same class of persons throughout the enactment.
>
> **Functions of internal aids.** The long title indicates the Act’s subject and the preamble may identify its broader purpose. A heading places a provision within a topic, but its generality cannot narrow clear operative language. An explanation may remove doubt about a term or show how the provision applies. An illustration gives an enacted example of the rule in operation and should be read consistently with the rule itself. A schedule forms part of the enactment when the Act incorporates it. For instance, if the charging section adopts a schedule of rates, both provisions are needed to calculate the liability. Punctuation and marginal notes carry limited weight where grammar and statutory context point elsewhere.
>
> **Limits of a proviso.** A proviso generally creates an exception or qualification within the field of the principal provision. If a licensing section requires all traders to register but exempts traders below a stated turnover, the proviso relieves those traders of that obligation. It does not create a registration requirement for persons outside the principal section. Its actual effect still depends on its wording and place in the scheme.
>
> **Weight of external aids.** Earlier enactments and committee reports may identify a defect in the prior law. A dictionary supplies possible senses, and the court chooses the one that fits the provision. Statutes dealing with the same subject may help where the terms and scheme are comparable. Legislative material must be used for the purpose for which it is admissible. In *K.P. Varghese*, the Court considered the speech introducing the measure to identify the problem of understated consideration when construing the former tax provision.
>
> **Application.** If the scope of “market” in a municipal Act is disputed, first read the definition, licensing provisions and relevant schedule together. A dictionary might offer several meanings. Earlier law or a committee report might show why the legislature regulated a particular kind of trading venue. Adopt the meaning that fits the enacted provisions, explaining the precise assistance each source supplies.
>
> #### 4. Conclusion
>
> Aids support the text; the court uses them to resolve real ambiguity, not to legislate.

### Q3. Explain the General Clauses Act, 1897 as a codified aid.

> **Model answer — 15 marks**
>
> #### 1. Introduction and purpose
>
> The Act supplies standard definitions and general rules for Central Acts and regulations.
>
> #### 2. Principal rules
>
> Important rules concern commencement; gender and number; power to issue, amend, vary or rescind orders; continuation of subordinate instruments after re-enactment; and the effect of repeal. Repeal ordinarily preserves prior operation, accrued rights and liabilities, penalties and proceedings unless a contrary intention appears.
>
> #### 3. Authority and application
>
> *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84, explains the saving effect. The Act applies subject to a special or later enactment showing a different intention.
>
> #### 4. Detailed application
>
> The Act is a general interpretive statute, so its rules operate automatically unless the subject or context excludes them. The definitions provision avoids repeating common meanings. The commencement rule identifies when rights and duties begin. The power to issue an order ordinarily includes the ancillary power to amend, vary or rescind it, exercised in the same manner and subject to the same safeguards. On re-enactment, existing notifications and rules continue only so far as they are consistent with the new enactment. The repeal rule protects finality and prevents a repeal from retrospectively destroying proceedings already begun.
>
>
> **Definitions and commencement.** Section 3 standardises recurring expressions, subject to the subject or context. This lets Parliament use common terms without reproducing their definitions in every Central Act. Section 5 supplies a commencement rule where a Central Act does not express a particular commencement date. If a later Act instead provides for commencement by notification, the applicable notification must be checked. Different provisions may start on different dates if the enabling provision permits that arrangement. Identify the date applicable to the particular right or duty before considering whether a law operates retrospectively.
>
> **Time and language.** Section 9 explains how “from” and “to” can exclude the first and include the last day respectively when the prescribed period is expressed in that way. Section 10 deals with certain acts to be done in a court or office on a day when it is closed, subject to the statutory qualification. Section 13 supplies defaults for gender and for singular and plural, unless the subject or context indicates otherwise. These provisions resolve recurring drafting questions while leaving room for more specific legislative instructions.
>
> **Repeal and continuity.** Under section 6, repeal does not ordinarily undo the prior operation of the repealed enactment or destroy an accrued right, incurred liability or related proceeding. Suppose a liability arises under an Act and the Act is later repealed while recovery proceedings are pending. The relevant question is whether the liability had arisen before repeal and whether the new enactment displaces the saving. A person who merely hoped to qualify for a future benefit under the former Act must first establish an accrued right.
>
> **Delegated instruments.** Section 21 concerns the power to add to, amend, vary or rescind instruments issued under a statutory power, exercised in the like manner and subject to like conditions. Section 24 addresses continuity when legislation is repealed and re-enacted. An existing rule may continue if it is consistent with the re-enacted provisions and until it is superseded, subject to the new enactment. Thus a saved liability and a continuing rule present separate questions governed by different provisions.
>
> #### 5. Conclusion
>
> It promotes uniformity, continuity and certainty without overriding clear words of the later statute.

### Q4. Discuss constitutional interpretation and structural doctrines.

> **Model answer — 15 marks**
>
> #### 1. Interpretive approach and structural doctrines
>
> Constitutional interpretation uses text, structure, history, precedent, constitutional values and practical consequences because the Constitution is a supreme and enduring document. The **pith and substance** doctrine identifies the true nature of legislation; **colourable legislation** prevents doing indirectly what cannot be done directly; **occupied field/repugnancy** principles address conflicts between Union and State laws; residuary subjects belong to Parliament.
>
> #### 2. Presumption of validity and judicial review
>
> The court also presumes that legislation is constitutionally valid and prefers a construction that preserves validity where the language reasonably permits it. Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and institutional provisions are read harmoniously. Judicial review is controlled by precedent, the separation of powers and the requirement that the court decide legal questions rather than substitute its policy preference.
>
> #### 3. Basic structure, reading down and prospective overruling
>
> The basic-structure doctrine in *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225, permits amendment but protects constitutional supremacy, rule of law, judicial review and separation of powers. *Minerva Mills v Union of India*, (1980) 3 SCC 625, reaffirmed limited amending power. Courts may use **reading down** where a narrower lawful meaning is reasonably available. A larger bench may overrule precedent; **prospective overruling**, recognised in *I.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643, protects past transactions while changing the future rule.
>
>
> **Text and structure.** Begin with the words of the relevant provision and read them with neighbouring provisions and the design of the Constitution. The Preamble assists in identifying constitutional values. Part III guarantees rights, and Part IV sets out principles for governance. A harmonious reading seeks to give effect to both within their constitutional roles. For example, a social welfare objective may explain legislation restricting a right, but the restriction must still satisfy the applicable constitutional standard.
>
> **Legislative competence.** Articles 245 and 246 and the Seventh Schedule allocate legislative fields. In a competence challenge, identify the law’s object, operation and effect. A State statute whose true character falls within a State List entry can survive an incidental overlap with another field. Colourable legislation concerns the legislature’s ability to enact the substance of the measure. Changing its label cannot cure a lack of competence. Article 248 and Union List Entry 97 address residuary legislative power, subject to the constitutional allocation.
>
> **Repugnancy.** First determine whether each legislature had competence. A conflict under Article 254 typically concerns Parliamentary and State laws on a Concurrent List subject. Where the provisions are repugnant, the State law is ordinarily void to the extent of repugnancy. Under Article 254(2), a reserved State law that receives Presidential assent may prevail in that State, subject to Parliament’s power to override it. Ask whether both provisions can operate together before treating the State provision as displaced.
>
> **Review and remedies.** A court considers the ordinary meaning and any legally available narrower reading before striking down a provision. Reading down preserves the provision through a construction the text can bear. Severability asks whether an invalid portion can be removed while the remaining provisions function independently and consistently with the legislative scheme. Basic structure concerns limits on constitutional amendments under Article 368. In a problem question, identify whether the measure is an ordinary law or a constitutional amendment, then select the applicable test and explain the consequence.
>
> #### 4. Conclusion
>
> Constitutional interpretation preserves text, structure and values while respecting institutional limits.

### Q5. Explain interpretation of penal, taxing and remedial statutes.

> **Model answer — 15 marks**
>
> #### 1. Penal statutes
>
> A penal provision is strictly construed: punishment cannot be imposed unless the act and penalty clearly fall within the words. Where two reasonable meanings remain, the accused receives the benefit. *Tolaram Relumal v State of Bombay*, AIR 1954 SC 496, applies this rule. Mens rea is ordinarily presumed unless clear words or necessary implication create strict liability; vicarious liability needs statutory authority.
>
> The rule does not mean that courts ignore ordinary meaning, definitions, exceptions or the statutory purpose. The prosecution must establish every ingredient of the offence. A penal provision is not extended by analogy, and a doubtful penalty is not imposed merely because the conduct appears blameworthy.
>
> #### 2. Taxing statutes
>
> A tax requires clear authority. Courts do not impose tax by equity, analogy or implication; liability must fall within the charging provision. *A.V. Fernandez v State of Kerala*, AIR 1957 SC 657, applies the “four corners” principle. Machinery provisions may receive a practical construction.
>
> #### 3. Remedial statutes
>
> Remedial or beneficial statutes cure a defect or protect a class and are generally read liberally to advance the remedy, but not contrary to clear words.
>
>
> **Penal application.** Identify the prohibited conduct, the person subject to the prohibition, any mental element, and the prescribed consequence. Suppose a penal statute prohibits selling an unlicensed product. A court should first determine whether the accused’s conduct constitutes a sale within the Act. Mere possession does not become sale solely because both acts present a similar policy concern. If a statutory definition expressly includes an offer for sale, apply that definition. The benefit of genuine ambiguity arises after ordinary interpretive tools have been used.
>
> **Tax application.** Identify the taxable event, the person liable, the rate and the base on which it is imposed. For example, if an enactment charges a duty on the sale of a specified commodity, an administrative collection rule cannot by itself extend the duty to a transaction outside the charging provision. Once liability is established, assessment and collection provisions should operate effectively within the statute. The scope of a particular exemption must be examined separately against its own terms and conditions. Article 265 supplies the constitutional rule that taxation requires authority of law.
>
> **Remedial application.** First identify the condition that the legislation was enacted to remedy and the class it seeks to protect. Where a welfare provision can reasonably bear two readings, the court may adopt the reading that makes the remedy effective for its intended beneficiaries. For example, if a compensation scheme protects workers injured in the course of employment, an available reading may cover an injury during a task reasonably incidental to assigned work. The decision still depends on the definition of a protected worker, the required link to employment and any express exclusion.
>
> **Mixed statutes.** One enactment can contain remedial, administrative and penal provisions. A worker protection Act may confer a benefit, prescribe a claim procedure and punish obstructing an inspector. Read each clause according to its function within the scheme. Give the benefit provision practical effect, apply the prescribed procedure consistently with its purpose, and require the elements of the offence to be established before imposing a penalty. The remedy-oriented purpose of the Act cannot supply a missing element of an offence.
>
> #### 4. Conclusion
>
> The approach depends on the statute’s nature and purpose, always controlled by the enacted language.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q6. Explain *ejusdem generis*, *noscitur a sociis* and *reddendo singula singulis*.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Meaning.** *Ejusdem generis* means “of the same kind” and limits general words following a distinct class of specific words.
> 2. **Conditions.** The specific words must form a recognisable class; the list must not be exhaustive; and no contrary intention must appear.
> 3. **Related doctrines.** *Noscitur a sociis* means “a word is known by the company it keeps” and uses surrounding words to clarify an ambiguity. *Reddendo singula singulis* matches corresponding subjects and predicates.
> 4. **Case law.** In *Amar Chandra Chakraborty v Collector of Excise*, (1972) 2 SCC 442, the Supreme Court explained the conditions for *ejusdem generis*. *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills v Collector of Central Excise*, (1990) 3 SCC 447, illustrates contextual reading.
> 5. **Applying ejusdem generis.** First identify the specific items, then formulate the class they share without making it so broad that it captures everything. For example, “buses, lorries, taxis and other vehicles” in a motor-transport licensing provision may point to a class of motor vehicles. A bicycle would then fall outside the general words unless the definition or licensing scheme indicates a broader reach. A list containing unrelated items may supply no workable genus at all.
> 6. **Applying noscitur a sociis.** This wider principle uses associated words to select among possible meanings even when the provision contains no residual phrase such as “other articles”. In *Rohit Pulp*, the Court read the disputed category of coated paper in the context of the neighbouring categories in an excise notification. The surrounding description informed the scope of the disputed expression. An express definition or a clear indication of breadth can displace the inference from neighbouring words.
> 7. **Applying reddendo singula singulis.** This rule assigns each phrase to the subject with which it naturally belongs. In “a licence or permit may be renewed for five years or extended for thirty days respectively”, the stated pairing assigns renewal to the licence and extension to the permit. Even without “respectively”, grammar and subject matter can support a corresponding allocation when applying every predicate to every subject would distort the sentence.
> 8. **Conclusion.** These are aids to discover meaning, not automatic rules; the complete statutory scheme remains controlling.

### Q7. Distinguish mandatory and directory provisions.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Meaning.** A mandatory provision must be obeyed; non-compliance normally invalidates the act. A directory provision permits substantial compliance.
> 2. **Tests applied by courts.** Courts consider:
>
>    - the legislative purpose;
>    - the consequence of non-compliance;
>    - whether the provision protects public or private interest;
>    - the presence of a penalty;
>    - prejudice caused; and
>    - whether the requirement affects jurisdiction.
> 3. **Purpose and consequence.** The distinction concerns the legal effect of a failure to comply. The court reads the requirement together with its purpose and the rest of the scheme. An express provision invalidating the act for non-compliance strongly supports mandatory treatment. Where the legislature imposes a separate sanction for delay but permits the decision to stand, invalidity may defeat the intended scheme.
> 4. **Safeguards and substantial compliance.** A requirement protecting a person’s opportunity to be heard may be essential. Suppose a licensing authority cancels a licence without giving the statutory notice or any opportunity to respond. If notice is a condition of valid cancellation, the omission defeats the safeguard and the decision is liable to be set aside. An error in the format of a timely notice may have a different consequence if the recipient received the required information and could respond. Prejudice is relevant in assessing whether the purpose was met, subject to any express statutory consequence.
> 5. **Public duties and timing.** An administrative deadline may be directory where invalidating a late act would injure persons the statute was designed to protect. For example, if an officer is directed to decide a benefit application within thirty days, it would be inconsistent with the scheme to assume automatically that the applicant loses the benefit on day thirty-one. A deadline for filing a statutory appeal can operate differently if the Act makes time a condition and permits extensions only in specified circumstances.
> 6. **Decision in a problem.** Identify the precise requirement and the breach. Ask who is protected, whether the Act specifies a consequence, and whether compliance in substance achieved the purpose. Then distinguish the obligation to follow the rule from the question whether the resulting act remains valid.
> 7. **Conclusion.** “Shall” and “may” are relevant but not conclusive. Legislative intention, not one word alone, decides the classification.

### Q8. Explain *non-obstante* clauses, last antecedent and “and/or”.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Non-obstante clause.** It gives overriding effect only to the extent of an inconsistency.
> 2. **Last antecedent rule.** A qualifying phrase ordinarily relates to the nearest reasonable antecedent, subject to grammar and context.
> 3. **“And” and “or”.** “And” is normally conjunctive and “or” disjunctive. A court may interchange them only to avoid absurdity or defeat of purpose when the context supports it.
> 4. **Scope of the override.** A clause beginning “notwithstanding anything contained in section X” directs the reader to give the operative provision priority over section X in a conflict. Identify exactly which provisions are named and the subject on which they conflict. If an emergency procedure overrides the ordinary notice period, for example, that does not automatically remove a separate obligation to record reasons. Both obligations should operate unless the special provision displaces them.
> 5. **Scope of the qualifier.** Consider the wording “workers and contractors engaged for night work”. A nearby qualifying phrase might describe only contractors. The surrounding provisions could instead show that the benefit is for night work alone and therefore requires both groups to be engaged for it. Punctuation, parallel phrasing and the way the Act treats the groups are useful clues. The last antecedent rule supplies a starting inference, whose strength depends on those clues.
> 6. **Conjunction and alternatives.** “Submit proof of identity and proof of residence” ordinarily requires both. “Produce a passport or driving licence” ordinarily permits either. An alternative may be inclusive where producing both is permitted, or exclusive where the scheme deliberately makes the options mutually exclusive. A court should explain why the surrounding provisions require any departure from the ordinary reading. Mere preference for a more convenient outcome supplies no basis to change the connective.
> 7. **Method.** Locate the operative command, identify what the disputed language modifies or overrides, and test each reading against the rest of the provision. The result should preserve the specified conditions and leave compatible provisions effective. This approach prevents a grammatical inference from turning a limited exception into a general exemption.
> 8. **Conclusion.** These are grammatical aids and cannot override the statutory scheme.

### Q9. Explain commencement, prospective, retrospective and retroactive operation.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Meaning.** Commencement is when an Act becomes law. Prospective operation regulates future conduct; retrospective operation attaches new consequences to completed events; retroactive operation uses past facts for a present consequence.
> 2. **Presumption.** Courts presume that a law is prospective where it removes vested rights or imposes a new burden or penalty.
> 3. **Constitutional limit.** Article 20(1) forbids retrospective conviction or a heavier punishment. Procedural and genuinely clarificatory provisions are more readily applied to pending matters.
> 4. **Find the operative date.** The Act may specify a commencement date, authorise an appointed date by notification, or leave the matter to the applicable default rule. Under section 5 of the General Clauses Act, a Central Act with no expressed commencement date ordinarily comes into operation on the relevant date of assent. Where provisions commence in stages, identify the date of the specific provision in issue. Commencement and temporal reach answer different questions. An operative provision may itself direct that particular consequences apply from an earlier date.
> 5. **Distinguish past events from present consequences.** A rule increasing the levy on transactions completed before commencement changes the legal consequences of past events. A new licensing rule may instead apply to applications decided after commencement and use earlier experience to assess eligibility. The use of an antecedent fact alone does not establish that the new rule alters a completed transaction. Identify the event to which the liability or benefit attaches and the terms of any transitional provision.
> 6. **Authority and application.** In *CIT v Vatika Township (P) Ltd.*, (2015) 1 SCC 1, the Supreme Court treated an amendment imposing a surcharge as prospective. The reasoning rests on fairness where a new burden would attach to completed events. Express words or necessary implication may support retrospective civil operation, subject to constitutional limits. A procedural amendment may apply to pending proceedings, but the court should consider whether its actual effect takes away an accrued substantive right. Describing a change as an “explanation” does not settle that question.
> 7. **Conclusion.** Legislative intention, fairness, vested rights and constitutional limits determine temporal operation.

### Q10. Explain express and implied repeal and its consequences.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Express repeal.** The later Act expressly identifies the earlier law or provision.
> 2. **Implied repeal.** It is inferred only where the enactments are irreconcilable or the later law clearly occupies the whole field. Courts first attempt harmonious construction.
> 3. **Consequences.** The General Clauses Act preserves prior operation, accrued rights, liabilities, penalties and proceedings unless contrary intention appears.
> 4. **Authorities.** *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84, and *Municipal Council, Palai v T.J. Joseph*, AIR 1963 SC 1561.
> 5. **How to identify repeal.** An express provision may repeal an entire enactment, a named section or only specified words. Check the commencement provision and any transitional or saving clause to see when and to what extent the old law ceases to apply. Where a later statute says nothing about repeal, compare the scope and operation of the two laws. Mere overlap, repetition or the introduction of an additional remedy is insufficient. A specific earlier rule may continue to operate within a general later scheme if both can be followed.
> 6. **What section 6 saves.** Unless a different intention appears, repeal does not affect the previous operation of the enactment, acts duly done under it, accrued rights or incurred obligations and liabilities. It also preserves qualifying penalties and related investigations, proceedings and remedies. A proceeding relating to a saved liability may be instituted or continued as permitted by the section. An expectation of acquiring a new benefit after repeal does not by itself amount to an accrued right. In *Mohar Singh*, the inquiry was whether the new enactment disclosed an intention inconsistent with the saving.
> 7. **Illustration.** Suppose a licensing Act is replaced after a fee becomes due under the former law. First ask whether the fee became legally payable before repeal. Then check whether the replacement Act extinguishes existing debts or supplies a different scheme for recovery. If section 6 applies and the replacement shows no contrary intention, the accrued liability can remain enforceable. If an old rule is invoked for a new application under the replacement Act, its continued operation presents a separate question under section 24.
> 8. **Conclusion.** Repeal changes future law but does not ordinarily erase completed legal consequences.

### Q11. Explain interpretation and construction; classify statutes.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Meaning.** Interpretation finds the meaning of words; construction draws their legal effect when applied to facts.
> 2. **Classification.** Statutes may be substantive/procedural, penal/taxing/remedial, declaratory, enabling/disabling, mandatory/directory or temporary/perpetual.
> 3. **Meaning in application.** If an Act uses the expression “public place”, interpretation identifies the meaning the Act gives it. Construction determines how the definition and operative rule apply to the facts, such as a privately owned hall to which the public is admitted. Courts often use the two terms interchangeably. In an exam answer, the distinction shows why understanding a word and determining the legal consequence are related tasks.
> 4. **Classification by function.** Substantive provisions create or define rights, duties and liabilities. Procedural provisions regulate how those rights are asserted or enforced. A provision creating a right to compensation is substantive, while a provision prescribing the application form is ordinarily procedural. That distinction can matter when a new law affects pending proceedings. An enabling provision confers a power or capacity, such as the power to make rules. A disabling provision imposes a restriction, such as disqualification from holding an office. A declaratory provision explains or declares the legal position, although its actual effect must be assessed from its terms.
> 5. **Classification by object.** Penal provisions prescribe punishment for prohibited conduct, taxing provisions create fiscal liabilities, and remedial provisions address an identified defect or protect a class. Each asks a different first question. For a penal provision, identify the ingredients of the offence. For a taxing provision, identify the charge. For a remedial provision, identify the benefit and intended beneficiaries.
> 6. **Classification by duration and consequence.** A temporary enactment expires on a fixed date or event unless extended. A permanent enactment continues until repealed or otherwise displaced. Mandatory and directory usually describe particular requirements and the effect of failing to comply. Several classifications can apply within the same Act. A temporary welfare Act can confer substantive benefits, prescribe directory administrative steps and contain a penal provision. Identify the particular provision in issue before choosing the interpretive approach.
> 7. **Legal effect.** Classification guides the approach, but the true effect, statutory language and legislative purpose remain controlling.

### Q12. Explain Mimansa principles as an Indian interpretive method.

> **Model answer — 10 marks**
>
> 1. **Meaning.** Mimansa reads words in relation to the whole passage and its purpose.
> 2. **Principles.** It uses context, harmony, specific over general where necessary, meaningfulness of every word, repetition, beginning and conclusion, and practical object.
> 3. **Origin and relevance.** Mimansa developed as an Indian tradition for resolving questions about the meaning, purpose and relationship of texts, particularly apparently inconsistent commands. Its relevance to modern statutes is methodological. A reader should examine the words, their immediate connection, the unity of the passage and the object served by the command. These considerations can help explain why an isolated phrase does not always settle a dispute.
> 4. **Read the passage as a whole.** Start with the direct language and consider how it relates to the surrounding directions. The opening and conclusion of a passage may indicate the subject it addresses. Repetition can show emphasis. Placement can help determine whether a qualifying expression belongs to a particular provision or governs a wider group. The aim is to give the words an intelligible function within the complete scheme.
> 5. **Reconcile general and specific directions.** If a general provision requires all applicants to appear in person and a specific provision permits applicants with a qualifying disability to appear remotely, the specific provision can operate within its defined scope. The general requirement still governs applicants outside the exception. This illustrates how context and harmony allow both directions to retain legal effect.
> 6. **Purpose and limits.** A reading that leaves a direction useless should be examined against plausible readings that allow it to work. Purpose can help choose among readings the language will bear. In a modern statutory problem, the enacted words, the constitutional framework and binding precedent govern the result. Show the relationship between the disputed provisions, explain the practical effect of the competing readings, and justify the preferred interpretation by reference to the statute. The value of discussing Mimansa lies in demonstrating that reasoning on the facts rather than simply listing traditional principles.
> 7. **Limits.** These ideas resemble harmonious and contextual construction. They enrich legal reasoning but cannot override enacted text, the Constitution or binding precedent.

## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Q13. Legal fiction

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> A legal fiction directs the court to treat an assumed fact as true for a defined legal purpose. Once created, it must be carried to its logical conclusion, but not beyond the purpose for which it was created. It cannot be used to create an unrelated right or liability.
>
> A deeming provision requires the court to assume a fact or status for the purpose specified by the legislature. The court identifies what is deemed, why the fiction was enacted, and which consequences are necessary to give it effect. In *State of Bombay v Pandurang Vinayak Chaphalkar*, AIR 1953 SC 244, the Supreme Court explained that a statutory fiction must be carried to its logical conclusion within its purpose. For example, if an Act deems service complete on a specified date for calculating an appeal period, the calculation proceeds on that basis. The same fiction does not automatically establish actual receipt for an unrelated contractual dispute. The scope of the fiction is determined by the operative words and the scheme in which they appear.

### Q14. *Casus omissus*

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> It means a case omitted from the statute. Courts normally cannot supply it because that would be legislation. Only a narrow exception exists where the Act cannot work, the omission is clearly accidental and the intended meaning is certain from the scheme. *Padma Sundara Rao* is the leading authority.
>
> A *casus omissus* is a situation for which the legislature has made no provision. A court normally applies the enactment as written, even if the omission seems inconvenient. This follows from the separation between interpretation and lawmaking. In *Padma Sundara Rao v State of Tamil Nadu*, (2002) 3 SCC 533, the Supreme Court confined any departure to a case of clear necessity for which the reason is found within the four corners of the statute. For example, if the Act grants a benefit only to two expressly named categories, the court cannot add a third simply because its circumstances are similar. The court first reads the whole Act to determine whether there is a genuine gap or whether another provision already covers the situation.

### Q15. Prospective overruling

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> The court changes the rule for the future while protecting past transactions under the earlier rule. It balances correction of legal error with fairness and certainty. It was recognised in *I.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643.
>
> Prospective overruling allows a court to announce a new legal rule and control its temporal effect. The judgment specifying the new rule determines which completed transactions, pending matters or future cases it governs. In *I.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643, the Supreme Court used the doctrine to preserve the operation of earlier constitutional amendments while announcing its ruling on amendment of fundamental rights. The example illustrates the doctrine’s purpose of managing reliance and avoiding disruption after a change in law. It differs from legislative commencement because the relevant cutoff comes from the court’s directions in the overruling judgment. In an exam problem, identify the overruled rule, the new rule and the judgment’s instructions about which cases receive the new treatment.

### Q16. *Stare decisis*, ratio and obiter

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> *Stare decisis* means following settled precedent. **Ratio decidendi** is the principle necessary for the decision and is binding according to court hierarchy. **Obiter dicta** are observations not necessary for the result and are persuasive. Article 141 makes Supreme Court law binding on Indian courts.
>
> Article 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India. The *ratio decidendi* is the legal principle necessary to decide an issue on the material facts. An *obiter dictum* is an observation beyond what was necessary to decide that issue and may carry persuasive weight. For example, if a court sets aside an order because the statute required notice and none was served, that interpretation of the notice requirement forms part of the ratio. A remark about how a different statute might work may be obiter. To identify the binding proposition, ask what issue the court had to decide, which facts mattered, and which legal proposition supported the result. Cite that proposition with the case.

### Q17. Strict construction of a taxing statute

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> Tax liability must be supported by clear words. No tax is imposed by equity, analogy or implication. The charging provision is strictly read, while machinery provisions may receive a practical interpretation. *A.V. Fernandez* illustrates the four-corners principle.
>
> Article 265 requires legal authority for a tax. A charging provision must cover the taxable event, the person liable, and the applicable basis of the charge. Courts apply the language of the charge and cannot create a liability by analogy or perceived fairness. In *A.V. Fernandez v State of Kerala*, AIR 1957 SC 657, the Supreme Court explained that liability must fall within the terms of the charging law. For example, a charge on the sale of a named commodity does not extend to a different commodity solely because its use is similar. Provisions for assessment and collection may receive a practical construction once the charge is established. Where an exemption is claimed, read its conditions separately and determine whether the claimant satisfies them.

### Q18. Beneficial/remedial statute

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> It cures a defect or protects a disadvantaged class. It is generally interpreted liberally to advance the remedy and legislative purpose, but not contrary to clear words or to create an unchosen benefit.
>
> A beneficial or remedial statute addresses a social or legal problem and confers a remedy or protection on an identified class. Where the language reasonably supports more than one interpretation, the court favours the reading that makes the remedy effective. For example, a compensation provision covering an injury in the course of employment may extend to an injury during a task reasonably incidental to assigned work, if the wording and scheme support that connection. The claimant must still meet the statutory eligibility requirements, and an express exclusion must be applied. In a problem question, identify the defect, the protected class and the disputed condition. Explain how the preferred reading advances the remedy within the boundaries fixed by the Act.

### Q19. Effect of repeal

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> Repeal ordinarily does not erase the previous operation of the law, accrued rights or liabilities, penalties already incurred, or investigations and proceedings, unless the new law shows contrary intention. *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh* explains this saving rule.
>
> Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 ordinarily preserves the previous operation of a repealed Central enactment, anything duly done under it, accrued rights and incurred liabilities. It also protects qualifying penalties and connected investigations, proceedings and remedies. The savings apply unless a different legislative intention appears. In *State of Punjab v Mohar Singh*, AIR 1955 SC 84, the Court examined whether the replacement enactment disclosed an intention inconsistent with the saving. For example, where a liability had already arisen under the former Act and recovery was pending on repeal, the proceeding can ordinarily continue if the liability is saved. A person seeking to invoke the saving must first identify the right or liability that accrued before repeal.

### Q20. *Noscitur a sociis*

> **Model answer — 5 marks**
>
> An ambiguous word is understood by the surrounding words. The doctrine is contextual and cannot defeat a clear definition or statutory purpose. *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills* is a useful authority.
>
> The maxim means that an ambiguous expression takes colour from associated words. It helps select the meaning the expression bears in the statutory context. In *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills Ltd. v Collector of Central Excise*, (1990) 3 SCC 447, the Supreme Court construed “coated paper” in an excise notification in light of the adjoining categories, which indicated the industrial character of the papers excluded from the concession. For a simple illustration, “pens, pencils, markers and other instruments” in a rule about classroom supplies suggests instruments for writing or marking. The inference depends on the relationship among the words and yields to a clear definition or contrary indication in the statute. The maxim is broader than *ejusdem generis*, which specifically concerns general words associated with an identifiable class.
