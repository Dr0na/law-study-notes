# Module 2: General Principles and Rules of Interpretation

## 1. Starting method

Begin with enacted words in their grammatical and ordinary sense, read with the whole provision, Act, subject and purpose. If the language is genuinely ambiguous or literal application produces a result the law cannot reasonably have intended, consider established interpretive tools. No single rule mechanically decides every case.

## 2. Mimansa principles

Mimansa is a traditional Indian system of textual interpretation developed in the interpretation of Vedic injunctions. Its principles can inform modern legal reading as structured tools, not as a substitute for enacted text or binding constitutional and statutory rules. The syllabus specifically includes Mimansa as an Indian Knowledge System component.

Commonly discussed principles include:

- **Shruti:** express statement has primacy;
- **Linga:** indication or suggestive meaning;
- **Vakya:** meaning from the sentence as a whole;
- **Prakarana:** context or subject matter;
- **Sthana:** position or placement;
- **Samakhya:** title or designation;
- **Arthapatti:** necessary presumption/postulation to reconcile an apparent inconsistency;
- **Anupalabdhi:** knowledge from absence/non-perception, used in the classical system.

The exact order and application vary by school and commentary. For a legal problem, state the principle briefly, explain its relevance and then return to the statute's text and context.

## 3. Literal rule

The literal rule gives words their ordinary, grammatical meaning in context. Courts generally cannot add words merely because another policy would be preferable. If the language is clear, the text ordinarily controls.

**Limit:** ordinary meaning is contextual, not dictionary isolation. A literal reading that creates absurdity, defeats the enactment's structure or makes a provision unworkable may justify considering another established rule.

**Cases:** *Kanai Lal Sur v. Paramnidhi Sadhukhan*, AIR 1957 SC 907 (plain language and statutory construction); *Nelson Motis v. Union of India*, (1992) 4 SCC 711 (where wording is clear, courts ordinarily give effect to it).

## 4. Golden rule

The golden rule begins with ordinary meaning but permits a limited adjustment where the ordinary reading produces absurdity, inconsistency or repugnance. The court chooses a meaning that avoids the absurdity while changing the text as little as necessary.

It is not a general power to rewrite legislation. The alleged absurdity must be genuine and the adjusted construction must remain linguistically and contextually available.

## 5. Mischief rule (Heydon's Case)

The rule asks four questions:

1. What was the law before the Act?
2. What defect or mischief existed?
3. What remedy did the legislature provide?
4. What interpretation suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy?

**Case:** *Heydon's Case* (1584) 3 Co Rep 7a. In India, *Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar*, AIR 1955 SC 661 illustrates purposive attention to the problem addressed by a constitutional/statutory provision. Use the rule where text and context permit; purpose cannot override clear enacted language.

## 6. Harmonious construction

When provisions appear to conflict, courts should read them so each has effect if reasonably possible. The construction should preserve the scheme and avoid rendering any provision redundant. If irreconcilable conflict remains, specific rules such as special-over-general or later-over-earlier may become relevant, subject to legislative intent.

**Cases:** *Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore*, AIR 1958 SC 255; *CIT v. Hindustan Bulk Carriers*, (2003) 3 SCC 57.

## 7. Rule of last antecedent

A qualifying phrase is ordinarily read as modifying the immediately preceding words, rather than more remote words. Grammar and punctuation provide a starting point; context, statutory purpose and the whole provision can displace the rule.

**Case:** *M. K. Salpekar v. Sunil Kumar Shamsunder Chaudhari*, (1988) 4 SCC 21 is commonly cited in discussions of the rule. Confirm the precise proposition against the judgment before quoting.

## 8. Non-obstante clause

“Notwithstanding anything contained…” signals that the provision is intended to operate despite a specified conflicting provision. Determine:

1. what provision(s) are overridden;
2. the clause's scope and subject;
3. whether the conflict is real;
4. whether harmonious construction can reconcile the provisions.

The clause does not automatically override every other law or provision. Its effect depends on the text, legislative scheme and subject matter.

## 9. Legal fiction

A legal fiction directs the court to treat a state of affairs as true for a specified statutory purpose, even if it is not factually true. The court must give effect to the fiction, including necessary consequences, but must not extend it beyond the purpose for which it was created.

**Cases:** *East End Dwellings Co. Ltd. v. Finsbury Borough Council*, [1952] AC 109; *CIT v. Amarchand N. Shroff*, AIR 1963 SC 1448.

## 10. “And” / “or”

“And” is ordinarily conjunctive and “or” disjunctive, but context can require reading one for the other to fulfil legislative intent. Courts do so cautiously, where necessary to avoid defeating the enactment or creating an absurd result. It is not a general licence to substitute words.

**Case:** *Ishwar Singh Bindra v. State of Uttar Pradesh*, AIR 1968 SC 1450 discusses contextual construction of “and”/“or”.

## 11. Construction of general words

### Ejusdem generis

“Of the same kind.” Where specific words form a recognisable class and are followed by general words, the general words may be confined to that class. Conditions: specific words; a common genus; general words following; no indication that the genus was intended to be unrestricted.

**Case:** *Amar Chandra Chakraborty v. Collector of Excise*, (1972) 2 SCC 442.

### Noscitur a sociis

“A word is known by the company it keeps.” The surrounding words may colour the meaning of an ambiguous word. It is a contextual aid and should not defeat clear language or statutory purpose.

**Case:** *Rohit Pulp and Paper Mills Ltd. v. Collector of Central Excise*, (1990) 3 SCC 447.

### Reddendo singula singulis

“Referring each to each.” Where a sentence contains several subjects and objects, the words may be distributed so that each phrase attaches to its appropriate subject. It is used where grammar and context support separate application.

### Words of rank

General words following named persons or things of a lower rank may not ordinarily extend to a higher rank, where the list and context show a rank-based class. This is a narrow application related to ejusdem generis, not a universal rule.

## 12. Related subsidiary principles

- Every word should be given meaning where context permits; avoid treating words as surplusage.
- Read the statute as a whole, not isolated sentences.
- A statute should be construed, where possible, to be effective and workable (*ut res magis valeat quam pereat*).
- Same words in the same statute are presumed to have the same meaning, subject to a different context.
- Different words may suggest a different meaning, but the inference is contextual, not conclusive.

## Method for a problem answer

Identify ambiguity → state the ordinary meaning → read in context → select the relevant rule → apply it to facts → test against statutory scheme and purpose → state the most defensible construction.
