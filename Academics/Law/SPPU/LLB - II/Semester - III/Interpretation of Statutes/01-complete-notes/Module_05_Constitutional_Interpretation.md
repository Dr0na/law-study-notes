# Module 5: Modes of Interpretation of the Constitution

## 1. Why constitutional interpretation differs

The Constitution is supreme law, establishes government institutions, allocates powers and protects rights. Its provisions are often framed at a high level of generality and are intended to endure. Constitutional interpretation therefore uses text, structure, history, precedent, purpose and constitutional values, while respecting the written allocation of power and amendment procedures.

## 2. Textualism and originalism

- **Textualism:** starts with the ordinary public meaning of the constitutional words in their legal and structural context.
- **Originalism:** asks what the text meant when adopted, or how its original public meaning constrains application. Variants differ on the relevance of original intent, public meaning and historical practice.

Historical material informs interpretation but is not always conclusive. Indian constitutional adjudication uses multiple methods rather than a single exclusive theory.

## 3. Harmonious construction and structural interpretation

Constitutional provisions should be read together so that each has effect. The structure of the Constitution—federalism, separation of powers, rights and institutional design—can guide interpretation where text is open to competing readings.

## 4. Historical interpretation and Constituent Assembly Debates

The history of a provision and Constituent Assembly Debates may illuminate the problem considered, rejected alternatives and constitutional design. Debates are external aids; the adopted text controls. They should not be used to attribute one member's view to the whole Constituent Assembly.

## 5. Legislative competence doctrines

### Pith and substance

To assess legislative competence, identify the law's true nature, character and dominant subject. Incidental encroachment on another legislative field does not invalidate the law if, in pith and substance, it falls within the enacting legislature's competence.

### Colourable legislation

The doctrine asks whether a legislature has indirectly done what it lacks power to do directly. The inquiry concerns legislative competence and substance, not the legislature's motive.

### Occupied field / repugnancy

Where the Constitution gives concurrent legislative power, Article 254 addresses inconsistency between central and State laws, subject to its clauses and presidential assent provisions. “Occupied field” is a way of analysing legislative intent and conflict; it is not a free-standing rule that every central law excludes all State law on the subject.

### Residuary power

Legislative subjects not enumerated in the State or Concurrent Lists fall within Parliament's residuary competence under Article 248 and Entry 97 of List I, subject to the constitutional scheme.

## 6. Amending power and basic structure

Article 368 gives Parliament constituent power to amend the Constitution, subject to procedural requirements and the basic structure limitation. *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225 established that Parliament cannot alter the Constitution's basic structure. *Minerva Mills v. Union of India*, (1980) 3 SCC 625 reaffirmed limits on amending power and the importance of balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

## 7. Moral reasoning, national ethos and Directive Principles

Constitutional values—liberty, equality, dignity, fraternity, secularism, democracy and justice—can guide interpretation where text permits. Directive Principles inform constitutional meaning and legislative purpose; they are not enforceable as standalone claims but are fundamental in governance (Article 37). Courts seek harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles rather than treating either as invariably superior.

## 8. Pragmatism, precedent and overruling

Constitutional courts consider institutional consequences and workable remedies, but legal reasoning remains bounded by text and precedent. Article 141 makes law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts. A coordinate bench ordinarily follows an earlier coordinate decision or refers the issue to a larger bench. The Supreme Court may overrule its precedent for compelling legal reasons.

### Prospective overruling

Under prospective overruling, a court changes the law but limits the effect of the new rule to future cases or otherwise protects past transactions. The Supreme Court adopted the doctrine in *I. C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643, and later decisions have applied or discussed it in context. It is a remedial technique, not the default consequence of every overruling.

## 9. Advisory jurisdiction

Article 143 empowers the President to refer specified questions to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. The opinion is authoritative and highly persuasive but is not a judgment in an adversarial appeal with the same formal binding effect as a decree. Its significance depends on the question and constitutional setting.

## 10. Anti-majoritarian objection to judicial review

The objection is that unelected judges may invalidate laws enacted by elected representatives, constraining majority rule. Responses include constitutional supremacy, protection of rights and minorities, separation of powers, reasoned adjudication and the amendment process. The debate asks how to balance democratic legitimacy with constitutional limits; it does not negate judicial review under the Constitution.

## Conclusion

Constitutional interpretation combines fidelity to text with attention to structure, history, purpose and values. Doctrines such as pith and substance, repugnancy and basic structure each answer a distinct constitutional question and should not be treated as interchangeable.
