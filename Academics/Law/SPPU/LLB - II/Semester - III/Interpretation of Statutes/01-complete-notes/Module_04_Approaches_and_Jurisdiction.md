# Module 4: Approaches, Restraints and Jurisdictional Presumptions

## 1. Theoretical approaches

### Textual approach

The interpreter gives primary weight to the enacted words, grammar and structure. Textualism constrains judicial discretion by requiring reasons to be anchored in the law adopted by the legislature.

### Intentionalist approach

Seeks the legislature's intended meaning, principally as conveyed by text and context; legislative history may assist where legally appropriate.

### Purposive approach

Reads the text in light of the statutory purpose and the problem addressed, while remaining within the language and scheme the legislature enacted.

### Pragmatic / consequential approach

Considers practical operation and consequences among plausible readings. It cannot be used to displace a clear statutory command simply because its consequences seem inconvenient.

## 2. Judicial and juristic restraint / activism

- **Judicial restraint:** courts respect legislative choices, apply precedent and avoid deciding policy questions beyond the dispute.
- **Judicial activism:** courts interpret and enforce constitutional/statutory duties robustly, sometimes developing remedies to secure rights. Activism remains bounded by jurisdiction, text, precedent and separation of powers.
- **Juristic restraint/activism:** describes scholarly or interpretive tendencies. Jurists may counsel fidelity to enacted text and institutional limits, or advocate principled development of doctrine. These are analytical positions, not separate sources of binding law.

The sound judicial role is neither mechanical nor policy-making without authority: interpretation must be reasoned, legally grounded and attentive to institutional competence.

## 3. Presumptions about jurisdiction

### No exclusion of established jurisdiction lightly inferred

Courts presume that ordinary civil jurisdiction is not excluded unless the statute expressly says so or exclusion follows by necessary implication. Where a special tribunal is created, examine the statute's language, remedies and scheme.

### Ouster and adequate remedies

An exclusion clause is construed in context. Even where jurisdiction is excluded, constitutional judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 is not ordinarily eliminated. Errors of jurisdiction, failure of natural justice, bad faith or constitutional invalidity may remain reviewable, depending on the statute and case law.

**Cases:** *Dhulabhai v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1969 SC 78; *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261.

### Creation or enlargement of jurisdiction

Courts and tribunals are creatures of law. They possess jurisdiction conferred expressly or by necessary implication, not by convenience alone. A statute conferring jurisdiction is read in light of its subject, parties, procedure and remedies.

### Territorial jurisdiction

Territorial reach depends on the statute's express terms and constitutional limits. Legislation is presumed to operate within the enacting legislature's territory unless extraterritorial application is authorised or the necessary nexus exists. A statutory provision may have cross-border consequences without being impermissibly extraterritorial; examine the legal connection and governing doctrine.

### Presumption against breach of international law

Where statutory language is reasonably capable of two meanings, courts prefer a construction consistent with India's international obligations, unless Parliament clearly intended a contrary result. International law does not automatically override clear domestic legislation.

## 4. Problem method

For a jurisdiction question, identify the forum's source of power; define subject-matter, territorial and party limits; test statutory exclusions; consider alternative remedies and constitutional review; then apply the facts.
