# Module 7: Commencement, Operation, Expiry and Repeal

## 1. Commencement

Commencement is the date on which a statute or provision begins to operate as law. The Act may specify a date; provide that it comes into force on assent; or empower the government to appoint a date by Gazette notification. Different provisions may commence on different dates. Always check the commencement section and notifications, not only the date of enactment or assent.

Section 5 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies a default rule for Central Acts expressed to commence on a particular day, unless a contrary intention appears. State laws may be governed by the relevant State General Clauses Act.

## 2. Prospective, retrospective and retroactive operation

- **Prospective:** applies to events or conduct after commencement.
- **Retrospective:** changes the legal consequences of past events or transactions.
- **Retroactive:** sometimes used for a law that operates from a past date or attaches new consequences to a past event; terminology is not used uniformly.

There is a presumption against retrospective operation where a law changes substantive rights, imposes new liabilities or takes away vested rights, unless the legislature clearly provides otherwise. Procedural and declaratory laws may apply to pending matters, subject to statutory intent and fairness. Article 20(1) prohibits retrospective criminalisation and retrospective enhancement of punishment.

**Cases:** *CIT v. Vatika Township (P) Ltd.*, (2015) 1 SCC 1 (presumption against retrospective taxation that imposes a burden); *Hitendra Vishnu Thakur v. State of Maharashtra*, (1994) 4 SCC 602 (principles concerning procedural and substantive amendments).

## 3. Perpetual and temporary statutes

A perpetual statute continues until repealed or otherwise terminated. A temporary statute includes a sunset or expiry date and ordinarily ceases by effluxion of time unless extended or replaced. It may contain a saving clause for accrued rights, pending proceedings, penalties or actions taken while it was in force.

## 4. Effect of expiry of temporary statutes

Expiry is not always the same as repeal. The effect on accrued rights, liabilities, penalties and pending proceedings depends on the statute's saving clause, the nature of the provision and applicable general clauses law. The GCA's section 6 concerns repeal; it should not automatically be applied to expiry unless legislation or precedent supports the application. Examine the temporary statute itself first.

## 5. Express and implied repeal

- **Express repeal:** the later Act expressly identifies and repeals the earlier enactment or provisions.
- **Implied repeal:** inferred only where the provisions are so inconsistent that they cannot stand together, or the later law clearly covers the whole field with an intention to replace the earlier law. Courts prefer harmonious construction and avoid implied repeal where possible.

Where both laws can operate consistently, both should be given effect. For central-State inconsistency in a concurrent field, Article 254 supplies the constitutional rule, including its conditions and exceptions.

## 6. Consequences of repeal

Section 6 of the GCA provides, unless a different intention appears, that repeal does not ordinarily:

- revive something not in force or existing when repeal takes effect;
- affect the previous operation of the repealed enactment or anything duly done under it;
- affect accrued rights, privileges, obligations or liabilities;
- affect penalties incurred for offences under the repealed enactment; or
- affect investigation, legal proceeding or remedy concerning such rights or liabilities.

Such proceedings may be continued or instituted as if the repealing Act had not been passed. The section applies subject to contrary intention and the repeal/re-enactment context. Section 24 may preserve rules, orders, appointments and instruments on repeal and re-enactment where its conditions are satisfied.

## 7. Checklist for a problem question

1. Identify enactment, provision and commencement mechanism.
2. Verify the actual date by the Act or notification.
3. Identify when the relevant conduct/event occurred.
4. Decide whether the amendment is substantive, procedural, declaratory or penal.
5. Check express savings and transitional clauses.
6. Apply the relevant General Clauses Act and constitutional constraints.
7. State the effect on accrued rights, liabilities and pending proceedings.
