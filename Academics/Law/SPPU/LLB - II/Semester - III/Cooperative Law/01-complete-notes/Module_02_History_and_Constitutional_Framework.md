# Module 2: History, Growth and Constitutional Framework

## 1. Cooperative movement in India: historical outline

The modern Indian cooperative movement first developed as a response to rural indebtedness and dependence on moneylenders. The **Co-operative Credit Societies Act, 1904** enabled registration of cooperative credit societies. The **Co-operative Societies Act, 1912** widened the legal framework beyond credit and enabled central/federal organisations. Under the **Government of India Act, 1919**, cooperation became a provincial subject, laying the foundation for State-level legislation. The Bombay Presidency enacted its own cooperative legislation, including the Bombay Co-operative Societies Act, 1925. After the formation of Maharashtra, the Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Act, 1960 consolidated and amended the State framework; it came into operation by notification in 1962.

After Independence, cooperatives were treated as instruments for rural credit, agricultural inputs, marketing, processing, consumer supply and community development. Five-Year Plans supported cooperative institutions through credit, infrastructure, technical assistance and State partnership. Growth was uneven: cooperatives expanded significantly in some sectors and regions, while issues of politicisation, weak member control, overdue loans and administrative dependence also emerged.

## 2. Five-Year Plans and State role

The planning era treated cooperation as a means of organising small producers and extending institutional services, especially rural credit. State involvement took several forms: enabling legislation, registration and supervision; capital participation; loans, guarantees and subsidies; training and technical assistance; and support through apex and federal institutions. In Maharashtra, State partnership and support provisions are found in Chapter VI of the MCS Act (ss. 50 onwards), subject to the applicable text and conditions.

State aid can strengthen a society's capacity but may create dependence or dilute autonomy if public control displaces member control. Modern cooperative policy therefore seeks a balance between **support, accountability and democratic independence**.

## 3. National institutions

### National Co-operative Development Corporation (NCDC)

NCDC is a statutory corporation established under the National Co-operative Development Corporation Act, 1962. It plans, promotes and finances cooperative programmes in production, processing, marketing, storage, exports and related activities. It can support cooperative projects through loans, grants and other sanctioned assistance.

### National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD)

NABARD was established under the NABARD Act, 1981 and began operations in 1982. It is the apex development bank for agriculture and rural development. Its cooperative-sector role includes refinance and institutional development support for eligible rural credit institutions, including State Cooperative Banks and District Central Cooperative Banks, within the current regulatory framework.

## 4. Cooperative legislation

- **State cooperative laws:** govern societies whose objects and operations fall within the relevant State framework. In Maharashtra, the principal law is the MCS Act, 1960 and the Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Rules, 1961.
- **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002:** applies to societies with objects not confined to one State and serving members in more than one State. It is enacted under Parliament's legislative competence for multi-State cooperative societies. The Central Registrar administers the central statute.
- **Constitutional provisions:** Article 19(1)(c), Article 43B and Part IXB address freedom, policy and governance of cooperatives, subject to the Supreme Court's decision on the amendment's federal validity.

## 5. Constitutional framework

### Article 19(1)(c)

The 97th Constitutional Amendment added “co-operative societies” to the freedom to form associations or unions. The right is subject to the reasonable restrictions permitted by Article 19(4). Freedom to form an association does not, by itself, exempt a society from valid statutory regulation.

### Article 43B

As a Directive Principle, Article 43B directs the State to endeavour to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies. It informs policy and statutory interpretation but is not independently enforceable in the same manner as a fundamental right.

### Part IXB: Articles 243ZH–243ZT

Part IXB, inserted by the 97th Amendment, set constitutional standards for cooperative societies, including definitions, incorporation, board composition and term, elections, audit, general body meetings, returns and offences. Its application must be explained in light of *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021): the Supreme Court held that ratification by at least half the State legislatures was required for the amendment insofar as it affected State cooperative societies, because the amendment changed the federal distribution of legislative power. Part IXB was therefore held invalid in its application to State cooperative societies; it remains operative for multi-State cooperative societies and Union Territories, as explained by the Court.

**Exam caution:** Do not present Part IXB's board and election provisions as automatically binding constitutional rules for every Maharashtra State cooperative society after *Rajendra N. Shah*. Apply the MCS Act and other valid State law to State societies.

## 6. Evaluation

The constitutional design recognises cooperation as a voluntary, democratic form of association and promotes professional management. Yet regulation remains necessary because cooperatives handle members' funds and public-facing services. The constitutional balance is between the member's freedom to associate, the society's autonomy, public accountability and the States' legislative competence over State cooperatives.
