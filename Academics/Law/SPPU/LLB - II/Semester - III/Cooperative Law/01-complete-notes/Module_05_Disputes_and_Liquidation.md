# Module 5: Dispute Settlement, Recovery, Appeals and Liquidation

## 1. Disputes and jurisdiction — s.91

Section 91 refers specified disputes touching the constitution, elections of the committee or its officers, conduct of general meetings, management or business of a society to the Co-operative Court, provided the parties fall within the statutory classes. These include the society, committee, specified officers, members or past members and persons claiming through them, and other societies in the circumstances covered by the section. A dispute must satisfy both the subject-matter and party requirements.

The phrase “touching the business” is interpreted in context: a connection with a cooperative's business is necessary, but the dispute must also be between parties and of a kind covered by section 91. The Registrar may be the proper authority for particular statutory proceedings; not every grievance belongs before the Co-operative Court.

## 2. Co-operative Court and procedure

The State may constitute Co-operative Courts under section 91A. Under section 94, the Court has powers to summon witnesses, take evidence and require documents, broadly using civil-court procedures. Proceedings are generally conducted in the manner prescribed and may be summary. Section 96 provides for a decision/award after parties receive a reasonable opportunity to be heard. Section 97 provides an appeal to the Co-operative Appellate Court within the statutory period, generally two months for the specified orders.

The Maharashtra State Co-operative Appellate Court is constituted under the Act and hears appeals assigned by the statute. The Act provides appellate, review and revisional remedies in specified circumstances. Identify the exact order, forum and time limit before advising or answering a problem question.

## 3. Limitation — s.92

Section 92 contains special limitation rules, including:

- six years for specified disputes between the society and current/past officers, agents, servants or members relating to acts or omissions;
- two months from declaration of results for a dispute concerning an election;
- other disputes governed by the Limitation Act, 1963 as if the matter were a suit, subject to the Act's provisions;
- specific computation rules for recovery claims and societies under winding up/administrator arrangements.

The section allows the Court to admit certain delayed disputes on sufficient cause. It is inaccurate to state a single general limitation for all section 91 disputes.

## 4. Civil court bar — s.163

Section 163 bars civil or revenue court jurisdiction over matters the Act requires to be decided by the Registrar or Co-operative Court, subject to the section's text and recognised limits. In an answer, explain the statutory allocation of jurisdiction and then assess whether the particular dispute is within it; do not state that civil courts have no jurisdiction over every matter involving a cooperative society.

## 5. Recovery mechanisms

- **Section 98:** specified awards, orders and certificates may be recovered through the statutory modes, including as arrears of land revenue or through execution processes.
- **Section 101:** for categories of societies and dues specified by the section, the Registrar may issue a recovery certificate after the prescribed process. The certificate has strong statutory effect and is recoverable under section 156 as arrears of land revenue. Its scope and available challenge are governed by the current Act and case law.
- **Section 156:** provides modes of recovery, including attachment and sale, as prescribed.

Do not claim that section 101 applies to every society or every debt. Identify the society type, kind of due and statutory conditions.

## 6. Contempt and appellate court

The Act grants the Co-operative Court and Co-operative Appellate Court specified powers and provides a contempt-related statutory framework. Explain the source and limits of that authority from the applicable sections, rather than assuming the courts have all powers of a civil court or High Court.

## 7. Winding up and liquidation — ss.102–110

### Grounds and order (s.102)

The Registrar may initiate winding up after an inquiry or inspection, on an application supported by a resolution of three-fourths of members present at a special general meeting called for that purpose, or on the specified grounds on the Registrar's own motion. Grounds include failure to commence or cessation of work, very low share/member deposits as specified in the Act, or failure to comply with registration/management conditions. An interim order is communicated to the society; it may submit an explanation, and the Registrar must provide an opportunity of hearing before confirming or vacating it.

### Liquidator (ss.103–105)

The Registrar may appoint a liquidator. On the interim order, officers hand over custody and control of property, claims, books and records. On confirmation, the officers vacate and the general body ceases to exercise its powers while the winding-up order remains in force. Assets vest in the liquidator. Subject to the Act, Rules and Registrar's control, the liquidator may sue or defend proceedings, realise assets, investigate and settle claims, pay creditors according to priority, determine contributions and report on the winding up.

### Effect and completion (ss.106–110)

The order operates for creditors and contributories; proceedings against the society may be restricted. Liquidator accounts are audited. The Registrar terminates the process on the prescribed final report or statutory time framework, and surplus assets are disposed of according to section 110 and the Act. Cancellation of registration completes the legal dissolution process under the relevant provisions.

### Distinguish deregistration from winding up

Deregistration under section 21A cancels registration on statutory grounds. Winding up under Chapter X is the process for realising assets, settling liabilities and distributing any surplus. One should not assume that deregistration automatically follows every winding-up order or that the procedures are interchangeable.

## 8. Offences and penalties

The MCS Act creates offences for contraventions including false returns, failure to hand over records or property, obstruction, improper use of society property and other conduct identified in the Act. The relevant section defines the offence and penalty; prosecution/cognizance follows the statutory rules. For an exam answer, select the offence directly relevant to the question instead of giving an unsupported generic penalty.
