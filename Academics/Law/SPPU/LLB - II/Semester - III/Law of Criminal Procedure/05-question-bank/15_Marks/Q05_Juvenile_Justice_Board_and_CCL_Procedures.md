## 🧒 Q5. Detail the constitution, powers, and functions of the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB) under the JJ Act, 2015. Explain the procedure for dealing with a 'Child in Conflict with Law' (CCL).

### 1. Introduction
The juvenile justice system in India treats children who commit offences differently from adults. It is built on a reformative and rehabilitative foundation. The **Juvenile Justice Board (JJB)** is the primary statutory body constituted to conduct inquiries and determine reform plans for **Children in Conflict with Law (CCL)**, ensuring they are not subjected to the adversarial criminal courts.

### 2. Definition (with Section number)
- **Section 4 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015** governs the constitution and composition of the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB).
- **Section 2(13) of the JJ Act, 2015** defines a "Child in Conflict with Law" (CCL).
- **Section 15 of the JJ Act, 2015** defines the "Preliminary Assessment" procedure.

### 3. Detailed Analysis of JJB and CCL Procedures

#### I. Constitution and Composition of the JJB (Section 4)
The State Government must constitute one or more JJBs in every district. The Board consists of:
1. **Principal Magistrate**: A Judicial Magistrate First Class (JMFC) or Metropolitan Magistrate (MM) with at least 3 years of experience.
2. **Two Social Workers**: Vetted experts, including at least one woman, having at least 7 years of active experience in child welfare, health, or education.
- **Tenure**: Members are appointed for a term of **3 years**, and no member can be appointed for more than two consecutive terms.

#### II. Powers of the Board (Section 8)
- Exclusive authority to deal with all proceedings under the JJ Act relating to CCLs in the district.
- Powers of a JMFC/MM under the CrPC/BNSS when conducting inquiries.
- Power to grant bail to juveniles (Sec. 12).
- Power to direct the police or NGOs regarding the custody, welfare, and medical treatment of the child.

#### III. Step-by-Step Procedure for Dealing with a CCL

##### 1. Apprehension and Prohibition of Detention
When a child is apprehended by the police:
- The child must be placed under the charge of the **Child Welfare Police Officer (CWPO)** or the Special Juvenile Police Unit (SJPU).
- The child **must not** be placed in a police lock-up or jail. Under Section 10, they must be kept in an **Observation Home** until production before the JJB.
- The police cannot handcuff or use physical force on the child.

##### 2. Production Before the Board
The CWPO must produce the child before the JJB within **24 hours** of apprehension. The police must submit a social investigation report within 10 days, detailing the child's family and psychological background.

##### 3. Mandatory Bail (Section 12)
The child *must* be released on bail (with or without sureties) even if accused of a non-bailable offence, unless there are reasonable grounds to believe that:
- The release will bring the child into association with known criminals.
- It will expose the child to moral, physical, or psychological danger.
- The release would defeat the ends of justice.

##### 4. Conducting Inquiry
The JJB conducts a friendly, non-adversarial inquiry. No advocates wear uniforms, and the child is not placed in a trial dock. The inquiry must be completed within **4 months** (extendable by 2 months).

##### 5. Rehabilitation Orders (Section 18)
If the JJB finds that the child committed the offence, it may pass reformative orders:
- Allow the child to go home after counseling.
- Order the child to perform community service.
- Place the child on probation under the care of parents/guardians.
- Send the child to a **Special Home** for a maximum of **3 years**.

##### 6. Preliminary Assessment in Heinous Offences (Section 15)
For children in the **16 to 18 age group** accused of **heinous offences** (carrying a minimum punishment of 7 years, e.g., murder/rape):
- The JJB conducts a preliminary assessment regarding the child’s mental capacity to commit the offence, ability to understand consequences, and circumstances.
- If the JJB finds the child had the maturity of an adult, it can transfer the case to the **Children's Court** (Sessions Court) to try the child as an adult (Sec. 19).

### 4. Case Laws
- ***Subramanian Swamy v. Raju* (2014) SC**: Upheld the age limit of 18 as constitutional, but led to the 2015 Act permitting adult trials for heinous offences in the 16-18 category.
- ***Sampurna Behrua v. Union of India* (2018) SC**: Directed State Governments to ensure that JJBs are fully functional, meet regularly, and carry out periodic inspections of Observation and Special Homes.
- ***Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan* (2009) SC**: Determined that the date of commission of the offence is the sole criteria for determining juvenility, protecting children who turn adult during the trial.

### 5. Conclusion
The Juvenile Justice Board is structured to ensure that children are kept away from the punitive adult criminal system. While the JJ Act, 2015 permits adult trial for heinous offences in the 16-18 age group, the procedure under Section 15 ensures that this transfer occurs only after a careful psychological evaluation, preserving the reformative character of juvenile justice.
