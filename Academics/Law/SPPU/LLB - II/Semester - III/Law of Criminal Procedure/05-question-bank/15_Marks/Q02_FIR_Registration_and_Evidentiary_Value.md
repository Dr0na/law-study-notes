## 📝 Q2. Discuss the provisions relating to the registration of a First Information Report (FIR) under Section 173 of the BNSS, 2023. Analyze its evidentiary value and the mandatory registration guidelines.

### 1. Introduction
The First Information Report (FIR) is the point of entry for the criminal justice system in India. It is the information given to the police officer in charge of a station regarding the commission of a cognizable offence. The primary object of an FIR is to set the criminal law in motion, allowing the police to initiate an investigation. 

### 2. Definition (with Section number)
- **Section 173 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023** (formerly Section 154 of the CrPC, 1973) governs the registration of information in cognizable cases.
- Under Section 173, every information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence, if given orally to an officer in charge of a police station, must be reduced to writing, read over to the informant, and signed by them.

### 3. Detailed Analysis of FIR under BNSS, 2023

#### I. Essential Elements of FIR
1. **Oral or Written**: The information can be given orally or in writing.
2. **Cognizable Offence**: It must relate to a cognizable offence (where police can arrest without warrant).
3. **Signed and Recorded**: If oral, it must be reduced to writing and signed by the informant. A copy must be given to the informant free of cost.

#### II. New Digital Provisions: E-FIR (Section 173(1))
The BNSS, 2023 introduces specific provisions for electronic registration of FIRs:
- **E-FIR**: A person can send information about a cognizable offence via electronic communication (SMS, email, portal).
- **Signature Constraint**: To prevent fake or anonymous reports, the information sent electronically **must be signed** by the informant within **3 days** before it is formally registered as an FIR.

#### III. Preliminary Inquiry (Section 173(3) — New Feature)
The BNSS codifies a preliminary gate for specific cases:
- For offences carrying a punishment of **3 to 7 years**, the officer-in-charge can conduct a **Preliminary Inquiry** within **14 days** to determine if a prima facie case exists.
- The preliminary inquiry is conducted with the prior permission of an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police (DSP).
- If a prima facie case exists, a formal FIR is registered, and investigation begins.

#### IV. Mandatory Registration Guidelines
The law regarding mandatory registration of FIRs was settled by a Constitution Bench in ***Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of UP* (2014)**:
- **Mandatory Duty**: If the information clearly discloses a cognizable offence, the police officer **must** register the FIR. The officer cannot conduct a preliminary inquiry to test the credibility of the information.
- **Exceptions (Where Preliminary Inquiry is Allowed)**: The court listed limited categories where a preliminary inquiry is permitted before FIR registration:
  - Matrimonial/family disputes.
  - Commercial offences.
  - Medical negligence cases.
  - Corruption/bribery cases.
  - Cases involving abnormal delay in reporting (e.g., > 3 months) without reasonable explanation.

#### V. Evidentiary Value of FIR
An FIR is **not substantive evidence**. This means a court cannot convict an accused solely based on the contents of an FIR. Its evidentiary value includes:
- **Corroboration**: Under Section 157 of the BSA, 2023, the FIR can be used to support the testimony of the informant when they are examined in court.
- **Contradiction**: Under Section 145 of the BSA, the defence can use the FIR to point out inconsistencies or contradictions in the informant’s testimony during cross-examination.
- **Res Gestae**: If the FIR is registered immediately after the incident, it can be relevant as part of the transaction (res gestae) under Section 6 of the BSA.
- **Dying Declaration**: If the informant dies after registering the FIR due to injuries received in the incident, the FIR can be admitted as a dying declaration under Section 26 of the BSA.

---

### 4. Case Laws
- ***Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of U.P.* (2014) SC**: Laid down the absolute rule that registration of FIR is mandatory under Section 154 (Sec. 173 BNSS) if the disclosure reveals a cognizable offence.
- ***State of Andhra Pradesh v. Punati Ramulu* (1993) SC**: Held that if a police officer refuses to register an FIR and instead registers it only after visiting the crime scene, it constitutes a serious procedural lapse.
- ***Romesh Babaji Devreddy v. State of Maharashtra* (2007)**: Delayed FIR is not fatal to the prosecution if the delay is satisfactorily explained (e.g., trauma of victim, fear of accused).

### 5. Conclusion
Section 173 of the BNSS, 2023 updates the FIR process by permitting E-FIR with a 3-day signature rule and codifying the preliminary inquiry timeframe. Despite these changes, the fundamental rule established in *Lalita Kumari* remains: police must register an FIR for cognizable disclosures, protecting citizens from arbitrary police inaction.
