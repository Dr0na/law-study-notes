## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain registration of information, FIR and investigation under the BNSS.

Section 173 governs information relating to a cognizable offence. Information may be given orally or by electronic communication irrespective of the area where the offence occurred; electronic information must be signed within the statutory period. A free copy is given to the informant/victim. For specified offences against women, recording and safeguards apply, including recording by a woman police officer where prescribed. For offences punishable within the statutory range specified in section 173(3), a preliminary inquiry may be conducted with prior permission of an officer not below Deputy Superintendent of Police, within the prescribed period, to determine whether a prima facie case exists.

Under section 175, police may investigate cognizable cases subject to statutory conditions and magistrate oversight. Section 176 sets out investigation procedure, including prompt reporting to the Magistrate, proceeding to the scene, collection of evidence and forensic measures where applicable. Statements to police are governed by section 181 and are generally not substantive evidence, subject to statutory use for contradiction. Police confessions are restricted under the BSA; discovery-related information is treated under the applicable evidence rule. Section 193 governs the police report on completion of investigation.

An FIR is not substantive evidence of the truth of its contents. It may corroborate its maker under BSA section 160 or contradict the maker under the applicable contradiction provisions. *Lalita Kumari v Government of Uttar Pradesh*, (2014) 2 SCC 1, interpreted the CrPC rule on mandatory registration; apply it with care alongside the BNSS’s express preliminary-inquiry provision. The BNSS also recognises electronic means and forensic investigation as part of procedural modernization.

**Conclusion:** FIR sets criminal law in motion and preserves the earliest account; investigation must remain lawful, impartial and subject to judicial safeguards.

### Q2. Discuss bail, including anticipatory bail, under the BNSS.

Bail balances personal liberty and attendance at trial. The BNSS distinguishes bailable and non-bailable offences and provides ordinary, default and anticipatory bail.

In a bailable offence, release is governed by section 478. For a person accused of a non-bailable offence, section 480 gives the court discretion subject to statutory restrictions, seriousness, risk of absconding, witness interference and other relevant factors. Section 479 addresses release of undertrial prisoners after detention for the prescribed portion of the maximum term, with statutory exclusions and special treatment for first-time offenders; calculate from the current text and check exceptions. Section 187 governs remand and default bail when the investigation is not completed within the statutory period, subject to the offence category and procedure.

Section 482 empowers the High Court or Court of Session to grant anticipatory bail to a person who apprehends arrest for a non-bailable offence. The court may impose conditions such as availability for interrogation, non-interference with witnesses and restrictions on travel. The power is discretionary, based on facts, not a blanket immunity from investigation. *Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v State of Punjab*, (1980) 2 SCC 565, rejects rigid formulas; *Sushila Aggarwal v State (NCT of Delhi)*, (2020) 5 SCC 1, holds anticipatory bail need not invariably be time-limited, while allowing conditions appropriate to the case.

**Conclusion:** Bail decisions require individualized reasons, statutory compliance and protection of both liberty and the investigation/trial.

### Q3. Explain procedure for trial of warrant cases instituted on a police report under the BNSS.

A warrant case concerns an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment or imprisonment exceeding two years. Chapter XIX, sections 261–266 BNSS, governs a warrant case instituted on a police report.

After supply of documents under section 230, the Magistrate considers the police report and documents. Under section 262, if the charge is groundless, the accused is discharged with reasons. If there is ground for presuming commission of an offence triable as a warrant case, the Magistrate frames a written charge under section 263 and explains it to the accused. If the accused pleads guilty, section 264 applies; otherwise, the prosecution evidence is taken under section 265, with cross-examination and the statutory opportunity to recall witnesses. If no evidence supports conviction, the accused is acquitted under section 266. If a case is made out, the accused is called upon to enter defence and produce evidence. After arguments and applicable examination of the accused, judgment follows under the BNSS.

The procedure protects fair notice, disclosure, cross-examination and defence rights while avoiding a full trial where the accusation is groundless. The right to speedy trial under Article 21, recognised in *Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar*, AIR 1979 SC 1360, informs procedural fairness.

**Conclusion:** The Magistrate’s sequence is disclosure, discharge-or-charge, prosecution evidence, defence, arguments and judgment; each stage must comply with the BNSS and fair-trial guarantees.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. Explain the principles of autrefois acquit and autrefois convict.

Article 20(2) protects against being prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. Section 337 BNSS embodies the statutory rule against a second trial after a competent court has convicted or acquitted a person, subject to its exceptions. The identity of the offence and prior competent adjudication matter; merely similar facts or a separate offence with distinct ingredients may not bar proceedings. Article 20(2) applies to prosecution and punishment, while the statutory rule may extend to the trial bar described in the section. *Maqbool Hussain v State of Bombay*, AIR 1953 SC 325, explains that departmental/customs confiscation was not a prior prosecution and punishment by a court. *S.A. Venkataraman v Union of India*, AIR 1954 SC 375, distinguishes departmental inquiry from prosecution. The rule protects finality and prevents oppressive repeated trials.

### Q5. Explain summary proceedings for maintenance under section 144 BNSS.

Section 144 empowers a Magistrate of the first class to order a person with sufficient means to provide monthly maintenance to a wife unable to maintain herself, a legitimate or illegitimate minor child unable to maintain itself, a major child unable to maintain itself due to physical or mental abnormality/injury, and father or mother unable to maintain themselves, where neglect or refusal is shown. The Magistrate may grant interim maintenance and proceeding expenses. Section 145 governs procedure, section 146 alteration on changed circumstances, and section 147 enforcement. The remedy is preventive and summary; it does not replace civil/family remedies. *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum*, (1985) 2 SCC 556, recognises the secular social-justice purpose of the former CrPC maintenance provision; the BNSS successor should be applied by its text.

### Q6. Explain appeals under the BNSS.

Appeal is statutory; no appeal lies unless provided by law. Sections 413–435 BNSS govern appeals. A person convicted may appeal as provided by the relevant provision and sentence; the State may appeal against acquittal or inadequate sentence in prescribed circumstances, and a victim has a statutory appeal right as specified. The appellate court may dismiss summarily where permitted, suspend sentence and grant bail, take additional evidence in the statutory circumstances, and alter, reverse or affirm the decision within its powers. Notice and hearing are required before an order prejudicial to the accused. Reference and revision are distinct supervisory remedies, not substitutes for an available appeal. Quote the specific appeal provision for the type of order in the problem.

## Part C — short notes

### E-FIR / electronic information

BNSS section 173 permits information about a cognizable offence to be supplied electronically irrespective of territorial area; the informant must sign it within the statutory period. A copy is supplied free. Electronic reporting does not dispense with the duty to investigate lawfully or comply with safeguards.

### Anticipatory bail

Section 482 BNSS allows the High Court or Court of Session to direct release on bail in anticipation of arrest for a non-bailable offence, subject to conditions. It is discretionary and fact-specific: *Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia*; *Sushila Aggarwal*.

### Summons case versus warrant case

Under BNSS section 2, a summons case is one relating to an offence that is not a warrant case. A warrant case concerns death, life imprisonment or imprisonment exceeding two years. Warrant trials involve formal charge procedures; summons trials use the simpler procedure in sections 274–282.

### Child in conflict with law

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 treats a person below 18 alleged to have committed an offence as a child in conflict with law. The Juvenile Justice Board conducts inquiry. For a child aged 16–18 alleged to have committed a heinous offence, section 15 requires preliminary assessment; it is not a trial or automatic transfer to adult court. Best interests, rehabilitation and statutory safeguards govern.
