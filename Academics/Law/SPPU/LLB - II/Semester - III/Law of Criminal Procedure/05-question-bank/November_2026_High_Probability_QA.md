# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Questions and Model Answers — Law of Criminal Procedure (LCC 0701)

> **Use and limits.** This is a reasoned revision guide, not an official prediction or answer key. It follows the 70-mark Semester III pattern seen in recent papers: Part A, answer any 2 of 4 (15 marks each); Part B, answer any 3 of 5 (10 marks each); Part C, answer any 2 short notes (5 marks each). The questions below are selected from the SPPU syllabus and recent-paper themes. Prepare the whole syllabus because the University may change the selection.
>
> **Answer-length guide:** 15 marks: a developed answer with statutory framework, steps/safeguards, relevant cases and a reasoned conclusion; 10 marks: focused explanation with principal provisions, key steps and 2–3 relevant authorities where appropriate; 5 marks: concise definition, provision, 3–4 points and a short concluding sentence. Page counts depend on handwriting; completeness and relevance matter more than padding.

## Part A — 15-mark predicted questions

### Q1. Explain the procedure for trial of summons cases by a Magistrate under the BNSS, 2023.

**Answer (15 marks)**

#### 1. Introduction and meaning

A summons case is a criminal case relating to an offence that is **not a warrant case**. A warrant case concerns an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or imprisonment exceeding two years. The summons procedure is intended to provide a fair and comparatively simple trial for less serious offences, while preserving notice, hearing and defence rights.

#### 2. Statutory framework

The definition is in **section 2(1)(x) BNSS** (summons case) read with **section 2(1)(z)** (warrant case). The ordinary summons-case procedure is in **sections 274–282 BNSS**. A summons case can arise on a complaint or on a police report; some provisions differ according to its source.

#### 3. Main stages of trial

1. **Substance of accusation — section 274:** The Magistrate states the particulars of the offence to the accused. A formal charge is not required. The Magistrate must ensure the accused understands the accusation.
2. **Plea and plea of guilty — section 275:** If the accused admits the accusation, the Magistrate records the plea as required and may convict. In petty cases, the statutory procedure permits a written plea and payment of the specified fine, subject to the section’s conditions.
3. **Prosecution evidence — section 276:** If the accused does not plead guilty, or is not convicted on the plea, the Magistrate hears the prosecution and takes the evidence. The accused has the opportunity to cross-examine prosecution witnesses.
4. **Defence evidence — section 277:** The accused is given an opportunity to produce defence evidence. The court may issue process for attendance of a witness or production of a document, subject to the statutory safeguards against using the process for vexation, delay or defeating justice.
5. **Acquittal or conviction — section 278:** After considering the evidence and hearing the parties, the Magistrate records an acquittal or conviction and passes the appropriate order.
6. **Non-appearance of complainant — section 279:** In a complaint case, the Magistrate may acquit the accused when the complainant does not appear, subject to the section and the court’s power to adjourn or dispense with attendance where justified.
7. **Withdrawal of complaint — section 280:** Before final order, the complainant may seek permission to withdraw; if permission is granted, the accused is acquitted.
8. **Stopping proceedings — section 281:** In a summons case instituted otherwise than upon complaint, a Magistrate may stop proceedings at the stage permitted by the section and record the prescribed result, having regard to the stage of evidence.
9. **Conversion — section 282:** If the nature of the case makes a warrant-case procedure necessary in the interests of justice, the Magistrate may convert it and rehear witnesses as required by law.

#### 4. Safeguards and principles

The absence of a formal charge does not mean absence of fair notice. The particulars must make the accusation intelligible, and the accused must be able to answer it. The opportunity to cross-examine, call defence evidence and receive a reasoned decision are central safeguards. Article 21 protects a fair and speedy criminal process. In *Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar*, (1980) 1 SCC 81 (and the connected proceedings), the Supreme Court treated speedy trial as part of Article 21. The case interpreted the former CrPC framework; its constitutional principle remains relevant when applying BNSS procedure.

#### 5. Distinction from summary trial

A summons trial is the ordinary procedure for a summons case. A **summary trial** under sections 283–288 is a specially shortened procedure for specified eligible offences and is governed by its own conditions and record requirements. The two expressions should not be treated as interchangeable.

#### 6. Conclusion

The sequence is: state the particulars, record the plea, hear prosecution evidence, allow the defence, and decide by acquittal or conviction. Sections 274–282 simplify trial without removing the accused’s right to understand and contest the case. A good answer should distinguish summons procedure from summary procedure and identify the statutory stage at each step.

### Q2. Explain appeals under the BNSS, 2023.

**Answer (15 marks)**

#### 1. Introduction

An appeal is a statutory remedy by which a superior court examines a decision of a lower criminal court. It may address errors of fact, law, sentence or procedure within the powers conferred by statute. Since appeal is not inherent, the right and its scope must be found in the BNSS or another applicable law.

#### 2. Statutory framework

The general rule is in **section 413 BNSS**: no appeal lies unless the BNSS or another law provides for it. The principal provisions are **sections 413–435**. An answer should identify the provision relevant to the particular appellant and order rather than state that every order is appealable.

#### 3. Principal appeal routes

1. **Appeal against conviction:** Sections **415–417** provide routes according to the court which convicted and the nature or extent of sentence. The forum may be the Court of Session or High Court; statutory exceptions apply, including specified guilty-plea cases and petty cases.
2. **State appeal against sentence:** Under **section 418**, the State may appeal on the ground of inadequacy of sentence, subject to the statutory forum and notice requirements. The accused must receive a fair opportunity to show cause against enhancement.
3. **Appeal against acquittal:** **Section 419** provides for an appeal against acquittal in the circumstances and by the authority specified there. The provision distinguishes an appeal by the State from a victim’s appeal and requires attention to the forum and leave requirements where applicable.
4. **Victim’s appeal:** The proviso to **section 413** recognises a victim’s right to appeal against acquittal, conviction for a lesser offence, or inadequate compensation, to the court to which an appeal ordinarily lies against that court’s conviction. Identify the applicable limb in the facts.
5. **Appeals from certain other orders:** The BNSS specifies appeals for particular orders, including security and related proceedings. These are available only where expressly provided.

#### 4. Powers and procedure of appellate court

The appellate court may dismiss an appeal summarily where the statutory conditions are met, or call for the record and hear the parties. Under **section 430**, it may suspend execution of sentence pending appeal and release the appellant on bail or bond, for recorded reasons and subject to law. **Section 432** deals with the powers of the appellate court and permits, depending on the case, reversal or alteration of findings and sentence, acquittal, retrial or other lawful disposition. **Section 431** provides for taking additional evidence in the circumstances stated by the section, with reasons recorded. The court must comply with hearing and notice requirements before making an order prejudicial to the accused.

#### 5. Important principles and cases

- ***Bani Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1996) 4 SCC 720:** An appeal should be decided on merits; dismissal merely for non-appearance of the appellant is not the proper course. This interpreted the former CrPC appeal provisions.
- ***Chandrappa v. State of Karnataka*, (2007) 4 SCC 415:** An appellate court reviewing acquittal has full power to reappreciate evidence, but must give due weight to the presumption of innocence and the trial court’s view; where two reasonable views are possible, the acquittal should not be disturbed lightly.
- ***Mallikarjun Kodagali v. State of Karnataka*, (2019) 2 SCC 752:** The victim’s statutory appeal right is an important participatory safeguard in criminal justice. The decision considered the CrPC amendment that introduced that right; apply the present BNSS text for current proceedings.

#### 6. Appeal distinguished from revision

An appeal is available only by statute and ordinarily gives the appellate court the review powers specified for that appeal. Revision under sections 436–445 is supervisory and is not a substitute for an appeal that the party could have filed. The BNSS also restricts a party who has an appeal remedy from using revision to bypass it, subject to statutory provisions.

#### 7. Conclusion

The BNSS creates specific appeal routes and defines the appellate court’s powers. In an examination answer, state the no-appeal-without-statute rule, identify the appellant and order, explain the forum and remedies, and mention the safeguards against enhancement or other prejudicial orders. Avoid treating appeal and revision as identical.

### Q3. Explain bail under the BNSS, including bail in bailable and non-bailable cases and anticipatory bail.

**Answer (15 marks)**

#### 1. Introduction and meaning

Bail is release from custody on a bond or bail bond, subject to conditions ensuring attendance and protecting the process. The law balances individual liberty and the presumption of innocence with the need to secure investigation, protect witnesses and ensure trial attendance. Article 21 requires that restrictions on liberty follow fair, just and reasonable procedure.

#### 2. Statutory framework

The definition of bail is in **section 2(1)(b) BNSS**. The main provisions are **sections 478–496**. Important provisions include section 478 (bailable offences), section 480 (non-bailable offences before courts other than the High Court or Court of Session), section 479 (maximum period of detention of an undertrial), section 482 (anticipatory bail), section 483 (special powers of High Court and Court of Session), and section 187 (remand and default bail).

#### 3. Bail in bailable offences — section 478

Where a person accused of or suspected of a bailable offence is arrested or detained, and is prepared to give bail, release is ordinarily a **right**, not a matter of unrestricted discretion. If the person is indigent and unable to furnish surety, the statute permits release on a personal bond in the circumstances stated in section 478. The officer or court should not use inability to pay as a reason for unnecessary detention where the section applies.

#### 4. Bail in non-bailable offences — section 480

Bail is discretionary and must be judicially exercised. The Magistrate considers the accusation, supporting material, seriousness of the offence, punishment, antecedents, likelihood of absconding, risk of evidence tampering or witness intimidation, and the need for further custody. Section 480 places restrictions in specified serious cases, including cases where reasonable grounds indicate commission of an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment, subject to statutory exceptions and provisos. Special consideration is provided in the circumstances set out in the provision, including for a child, woman, sick or infirm person. Reasons must be recorded where required.

#### 5. Undertrial detention — section 479

Section 479 provides for release of an undertrial who has undergone detention up to the prescribed fraction of the maximum imprisonment, with a shorter threshold for a first-time offender, subject to the section’s exclusions. The court must consider the precise statutory conditions, including the offence category, multiple cases and any applicable statutory restriction. The provision does not apply mechanically without checking the text and facts.

#### 6. Default bail — section 187

Where the investigation is not completed within the applicable statutory period, the accused may claim release on bail upon satisfying the conditions in section 187. The period depends on the offence and prescribed punishment. The right is statutory and must be exercised in accordance with the provision, including readiness to furnish bail when the right accrues.

#### 7. Anticipatory bail — section 482

A person who has reason to believe they may be arrested for a non-bailable offence may apply to the High Court or Court of Session for a direction that, upon arrest, they be released on bail. The court may impose conditions, including availability for interrogation, non-interference with witnesses, and restrictions on leaving India. The power is discretionary and fact-specific; it does not create immunity from investigation.

#### 8. Leading cases

- ***State of Rajasthan v. Balchand*, (1977) 4 SCC 308:** Emphasised liberty and the principle commonly expressed as “bail, not jail,” subject to the facts and statutory restrictions.
- ***Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab*, (1980) 2 SCC 565:** Anticipatory bail should not be controlled by rigid formulas; discretion must be exercised judicially on the facts.
- ***Sanjay Chandra v. CBI*, (2012) 1 SCC 40:** Pre-trial detention should not become punishment; the court considers attendance, evidence risks and the nature of the accusation.
- ***Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi)*, (2020) 5 SCC 1:** Anticipatory bail need not invariably be limited to a fixed period, though appropriate conditions may be imposed.

These decisions were decided under the CrPC framework; their constitutional and interpretive principles remain relevant, while the current application must follow BNSS wording.

#### 9. Conclusion

Bail under the BNSS ranges from a statutory entitlement in bailable cases to a reasoned discretion in non-bailable cases, with separate rules for undertrials, default bail and anticipatory bail. A strong answer distinguishes each route, cites the relevant section and explains the liberty–justice balance.

## Part B — 10-mark predicted questions

### Q4. Discuss autrefois acquit and autrefois convict and their relationship with Article 20(2).

**Answer (10 marks)**

#### 1. Meaning and constitutional basis

*Autrefois acquit* means previously acquitted; *autrefois convict* means previously convicted. Both express protection against a second prosecution for the same offence after a competent adjudication. **Article 20(2)** provides that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. The constitutional text requires both prosecution and punishment; the statutory protection may be wider in its operation.

#### 2. BNSS provision — section 337

**Section 337 BNSS** bars a second trial where a person has already been tried by a competent court for an offence and has been convicted or acquitted, and the statutory conditions are satisfied. The section contains qualifications, including circumstances in which a later trial for a distinct offence or for an offence constituted by different consequences may be permitted. The court must examine the identity of the offence, the prior proceeding, competence of the first court and the statutory exceptions.

#### 3. Article 20(2) and section 337 compared

1. Article 20(2) is a fundamental right and applies where there was a prior prosecution and punishment for the same offence.
2. Section 337 is the procedural statutory rule against retrial after a qualifying conviction or acquittal and sets out its own conditions and exceptions.
3. A departmental inquiry or administrative penalty does not automatically amount to prosecution and punishment by a court.
4. A later charge with distinct ingredients is not necessarily the “same offence,” even if it arises from related facts.
5. The rule protects finality, prevents harassment and safeguards fairness, while preserving prosecution of genuinely distinct offences under statutory exceptions.

#### 4. Cases

- ***Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1953 SC 325:** Customs confiscation was not a prior prosecution and punishment by a court for Article 20(2); the constitutional bar did not apply.
- ***S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India*, AIR 1954 SC 375:** Departmental proceedings were distinguished from prosecution before a court for the constitutional protection.
- ***State of Bombay v. S.L. Apte*, AIR 1961 SC 578:** The test is whether the offences are the same in law, with the same ingredients, not merely whether they arise from the same facts.

#### 5. Conclusion

Article 20(2) and section 337 protect against repetitive criminal process, but their conditions must be separately applied. The decisive questions are whether the first adjudication was by a competent court, whether the statutory bar is engaged, and whether the later prosecution concerns the same offence.

### Q5. Explain the procedure for trial of warrant cases instituted on a police report.

**Answer (10 marks)**

A warrant case concerns an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or imprisonment exceeding two years (**section 2(1)(z) BNSS**). A warrant case instituted on a police report is governed by **sections 261–266 BNSS**, following supply of documents under section 230.

1. **Consideration and discharge — section 262:** The Magistrate considers the police report and documents and hears the prosecution and accused as required. If the charge is groundless, the accused is discharged by a reasoned order.
2. **Framing of charge — section 263:** If there is ground for presuming commission of an offence triable as a warrant case, the Magistrate frames a written charge. The charge is read and explained to the accused.
3. **Plea — section 264:** If the accused pleads guilty, the Magistrate records the plea and may convict in accordance with the section. If not, the trial proceeds.
4. **Prosecution evidence — section 265:** The prosecution produces evidence. Witnesses may be cross-examined; the accused receives the statutory opportunity to challenge the evidence and seek recall where permitted.
5. **Acquittal where no case — section 266:** If, after prosecution evidence and any examination required by law, no evidence exists that the accused committed the offence, the Magistrate records acquittal.
6. **Defence and judgment:** Where a case remains, the accused is called upon to enter defence and may produce evidence. After arguments and compliance with relevant examination requirements, the Magistrate gives judgment and passes a lawful sentence or other order.

The process ensures disclosure, a clear written accusation, prosecution proof, cross-examination, defence and a reasoned decision. The accused is presumed innocent and retains the right to a fair and reasonably prompt trial under Article 21. *Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar*, (1980) 1 SCC 81, established the constitutional importance of speedy trial under the former CrPC regime; the principle informs current procedure.

**Conclusion:** The warrant case on police report follows the sequence: documents → discharge or charge → plea → prosecution evidence → defence → arguments and judgment. Cite the stage-specific BNSS sections and explain the fair-trial safeguard at each stage.

### Q6. Explain the law relating to maintenance of wives, children and parents under the BNSS.

**Answer (10 marks)**

**Section 144 BNSS** provides a speedy, summary remedy where a person with sufficient means neglects or refuses to maintain a person whom the section protects. It is a social-justice measure intended to prevent destitution; it does not determine all civil or matrimonial rights.

#### Persons who may claim

A Magistrate of the first class may order maintenance for:

1. A wife unable to maintain herself, within the statutory meaning and subject to the section’s conditions;
2. A legitimate or illegitimate minor child, married or unmarried, unable to maintain itself;
3. A major child, other than a married daughter, unable to maintain itself because of physical or mental abnormality or injury; and
4. A father or mother unable to maintain themselves.

The provision also addresses a divorced woman who has not remarried, subject to the statutory explanation and applicable law.

#### Procedure and enforcement

The claimant must establish the relationship, inability to maintain, the respondent’s sufficient means, and neglect or refusal. The Magistrate may grant **interim maintenance and expenses of proceedings** during the case. **Section 145** governs procedure; **section 146** permits alteration on a change in circumstances; and **section 147** provides for enforcement. The amount and effective date are decided under the statutory framework and facts. The remedy is summary and is designed to operate promptly.

#### Cases

- ***Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum*, (1985) 2 SCC 556:** The Supreme Court recognised the secular and remedial purpose of the former CrPC maintenance provision and held that a divorced Muslim woman unable to maintain herself could seek relief under it, subject to the law then considered.
- ***Chaturbhuj v. Sita Bai*, (2008) 2 SCC 316:** “Unable to maintain herself” does not require absolute destitution; the inquiry is whether the claimant can maintain herself in the manner contemplated by law.
- ***Rajnesh v. Neha*, (2021) 2 SCC 324:** The Supreme Court issued guidelines on disclosure of assets and liabilities and harmonisation of maintenance orders to reduce inconsistent proceedings. The case concerns the previous statutory regime; apply the BNSS provision and current applicable personal law.

**Conclusion:** Section 144 offers an accessible and prompt maintenance remedy where statutory conditions are met. Explain the eligible claimants, neglect/refusal, interim relief, alteration and enforcement, and use older CrPC decisions as interpretive guidance rather than substitutes for BNSS text.

### Q7. Explain the jurisdiction of criminal courts in inquiry and trial.

**Answer (10 marks)**

The general rule is that an offence is ordinarily inquired into and tried by a court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed. **Sections 197–209 BNSS** set out the ordinary rule and exceptions concerning place of inquiry and trial. The object is to connect the proceeding with the offence, witnesses and evidence, while preventing technical jurisdictional rules from defeating justice.

1. **Ordinary place — section 197:** The offence is ordinarily tried where it was committed.
2. **Uncertain or multiple local areas — section 198:** Where the place is uncertain, the offence is committed partly in different areas, or it is continuing, it may be tried in any competent area specified by the section.
3. **Act and consequence in different areas — section 199:** Where an act is an offence by reason of a consequence that ensues, trial may lie where the act occurred or where the consequence ensued, as provided by law.
4. **Related offences and special situations — sections 200–205:** The BNSS provides rules for offences connected with other offences, offences committed during a journey, and other specified circumstances. Apply the section matching the facts; do not assume venue from convenience alone.
5. **Place of trial and jurisdictional allocation — sections 206–209:** These provisions deal with uncertainty, transfer/reference to the competent court and special rules for particular offences, as applicable.
6. **Cognizance and initiation:** Jurisdiction over place must be distinguished from the power to take cognizance. The latter is principally addressed in sections 210 onward; a complaint is governed by sections 223–226, and inquiry before a Magistrate by sections 227–232.

The court must have legal competence over the offence and territorial connection under the BNSS. Objections should be raised promptly; procedural irregularities are assessed under the BNSS provisions on irregular proceedings and prejudice. The purpose is fair access to evidence and parties, not a technical escape from a valid prosecution.

**Conclusion:** Begin with section 197’s ordinary rule, then identify the applicable exception based on uncertainty, continuing conduct, act/consequence, connected offences or journey. Keep territorial venue, court competence and cognizance analytically distinct.

## Part C — 5-mark predicted short notes

### Q8. Short note: Police diary / case diary under section 192 BNSS.

**Provision:** **Section 192 BNSS** requires the investigating police officer to maintain a day-to-day diary of investigation, recording matters such as the time information reached the officer, commencement and closure of investigation, places visited and circumstances ascertained.

The case diary is an aid to the court in understanding the course of investigation; it is **not substantive evidence** and the accused has no automatic right to inspect it merely because it exists. The court may call for and use it only in the manner permitted by the BNSS and the law of evidence. If the police officer uses the diary to refresh memory, or the court uses it to contradict that officer, the applicable evidence-law safeguards govern disclosure and use.

**Case:** *Shamshul Kanwar v. State of U.P.*, (1995) 4 SCC 430, explains that a case diary is not evidence and its use is controlled by statute (decided under the former CrPC).

**Conclusion:** The case diary supports judicial supervision of investigation but cannot itself prove guilt or replace admissible evidence.

### Q9. Short note: Public nuisance under the BNSS.

**Provision:** **Sections 152–163 BNSS** provide preventive and summary powers concerning public nuisances. Under **section 152**, an empowered Executive Magistrate may make a conditional order for removal of a nuisance or obstruction in the situations specified by the section, such as obstruction of a public way or conduct injurious to public health.

The person directed must obey or appear and show cause. The procedure includes service, inquiry where the order is contested, and the Magistrate’s determination under the following sections. Urgent measures may be taken in circumstances authorised by the BNSS. These powers are preventive and address public injury; they do not finally decide private title disputes.

**Conclusion:** The BNSS nuisance procedure enables a Magistrate to secure prompt public protection through a conditional order and a fair opportunity to object.

### Q10. Short note: Anticipatory bail.

**Provision:** **Section 482 BNSS** authorises the High Court or Court of Session to direct that a person apprehending arrest for a non-bailable offence be released on bail if arrested. The court considers the facts and may impose conditions, including availability for interrogation, non-interference with witnesses and travel restrictions.

The remedy is discretionary, preventive and fact-specific. It does not stop investigation or amount to a finding of innocence. ***Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab*, (1980) 2 SCC 565**, rejects rigid restrictions on the discretion; ***Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi)*, (2020) 5 SCC 1**, holds that anticipatory bail need not automatically end after a fixed period. Both interpret the former CrPC provision; section 482 BNSS governs current applications.

**Conclusion:** Anticipatory bail protects liberty against unjustified arrest while allowing proportionate conditions to protect investigation and trial.

### Q11. Short note: Release on probation of good conduct.

The **Probation of Offenders Act, 1958**, especially **section 4**, permits a court, in eligible cases and after considering the circumstances and character of the offender, to release the person on probation of good conduct instead of sentencing them immediately to imprisonment. The court may require a bond, with or without sureties, for the prescribed period and impose conditions. A probation officer’s report may assist the court. If the offender breaches the bond, the court may proceed according to the Act.

Probation is a rehabilitative alternative to imprisonment; it is not acquittal. The court must consider statutory exclusions and the nature of the offence. ***Rattan Lal v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1965 SC 444**, explains the reformative purpose of probation legislation.

**Conclusion:** Probation allows eligible offenders to reform in the community under court-supervised conditions, subject to the 1958 Act.

### Q12. Short note: Dispute concerning land or water likely to cause breach of peace.

**Provision:** **Sections 164–167 BNSS** empower an Executive Magistrate to address specified disputes concerning possession of land, water or boundaries when the statutory likelihood of breach of peace exists. The proceeding is preventive: the Magistrate determines who was in actual possession on the relevant date, not title or ownership. The order is subject to civil-court determination and statutory directions concerning attachment or emergency situations.

The parties receive an opportunity to state their claims and produce material. Where the dispute concerns a right of use, the related statutory provision may apply. The purpose is to preserve peace and prevent forcible self-help while leaving civil rights for the competent civil court.

**Conclusion:** The Magistrate’s order is a temporary preventive measure based on possession, not a final declaration of title.
