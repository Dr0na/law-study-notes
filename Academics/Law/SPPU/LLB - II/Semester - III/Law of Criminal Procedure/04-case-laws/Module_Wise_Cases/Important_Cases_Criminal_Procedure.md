# ⚖️ Detailed Module-Wise Case Law Digest

This digest provides facts, issues, holdings, and exam relevance for the most important cases under each module of the Savitribai Phule Pune University (SPPU) Law of Criminal Procedure (LCC 0701) syllabus.

---

## 🏛️ Module 1: Introduction to BNSS, 2023

### Case 1: *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) SC
- **Facts**: The passport of the petitioner was impounded by the government under Section 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967 in "public interest" without providing any reasons.
- **Issue**: Did the impounding procedure violate Article 21 of the Constitution by depriving the petitioner of personal liberty without a fair procedure?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that the phrase "procedure established by law" under Article 21 cannot mean *any* procedure. It must be **just, fair, and reasonable**. A procedure that does not follow the rules of natural justice (*audi alteram partem*) is not a valid procedure.
- **Exam Relevance**: Cite this to explain why criminal procedure rules under the BNSS (such as arrest rights and fair trial) must be constitutionally balanced and just.

### Case 2: *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979) SC
- **Facts**: A petition for *habeas corpus* was filed for thousands of undertrial prisoners who had been kept in Bihar jails for periods longer than the maximum sentence they would have received if convicted.
- **Issue**: Does the prolonged detention of undertrials violate the right to life and liberty under Article 21?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court ruled that the **right to a speedy trial** is a fundamental right implicit in Article 21. Keeping undertrial prisoners in jail without starting their trials is a violation of this right. It also ordered the provision of free legal aid to poor prisoners.
- **Exam Relevance**: Essential citation for questions on the "Rights of Accused" or the concept of a "Fair Trial."

## ⚖️ Module 2: Jurisdiction of Criminal Courts (Inquiry & Trials)

### Case 1: *State of U.P. v. Mohammad Naim* (1964) SC
- **Facts**: During a trial, the High Court made sweeping adverse remarks against the entire police force of Uttar Pradesh, questioning their integrity.
- **Issue**: Does the High Court have the power to make generalized remarks against individuals/departments who are not parties to the case?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that while courts have wide powers, they must observe judicial restraint. Adverse remarks should not be made against persons who are not before the court, unless absolutely necessary for the decision.
- **Exam Relevance**: Use to discuss judicial discipline, court powers, and the limits of revisional/inherent jurisdiction.

### Case 2: *Kiran Devi v. State of Rajasthan* (2020) SC
- **Facts**: A dispute arose regarding where a criminal complaint of matrimonial cruelty and dowry harassment could be tried, as the incidents occurred in multiple states.
- **Issue**: How is territorial jurisdiction determined when incidents of a continuous offence occur in different jurisdictions?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that under Section 178 of CrPC (now Section 198 of BNSS), where an offence is a **continuing one** and is committed in more local areas than one, it can be tried by a court having jurisdiction over any of those areas.
- **Exam Relevance**: Cite in Module 02 when explaining "Territorial Jurisdiction in Inquiries and Trials."

## 🔍 Module 3: Information to Police and Investigation

### Case 1: *Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of U.P.* (2014) SC
- **Facts**: A petition was filed alleging that the police refused to register an FIR regarding the kidnapping of a minor girl despite receiving clear information.
- **Issue**: Is a police officer bound to register an FIR immediately upon receiving information of a cognizable offence, or do they have the discretion to conduct a preliminary inquiry first?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court ruled that the registration of an FIR is **mandatory** under Section 154 CrPC (Sec. 173 BNSS) if the information discloses a cognizable offence. Police cannot conduct a preliminary inquiry to determine if the information is credible; they can only do so in specific cases (e.g., medical negligence, matrimonial disputes, commercial crimes) within a strict time limit.
- **Exam Relevance**: The single most important case for any question on "FIR," "Cognizable Offence," and "Investigation."

### Case 2: *D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997) SC
- **Facts**: A PIL was filed in response to increasing custodial deaths and police violence in West Bengal and other states.
- **Issue**: What guidelines should be established to prevent custodial torture and regulate police powers during arrest?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court laid down **11 mandatory guidelines** that police must follow during arrest and detention. These include: wearing clear identification tags, preparing an arrest memo witnessed by a local resident/family member, performing a medical checkup every 48 hours, and informing a relative/friend of the arrest within 8-12 hours.
- **Exam Relevance**: Essential citation for "Rights of Arrested Persons" and "Arrest Procedure."

## 🔒 Module 4: Bail, Bond, Maintenance & Public Order

### Case 1: *Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab* (1980) SC
- **Facts**: The Minister of Agriculture of Punjab filed for anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC, fearing arrest on corruption charges.
- **Issue**: What are the principles governing the grant of anticipatory bail, and can courts lay down rigid conditions?
- **Held**: A Constitution Bench held that Section 438 is an entry point for protecting **personal liberty** (Art. 21). The power to grant anticipatory bail is discretionary and cannot be bound by rigid rules. The court must balance the liberty of the individual with the interest of the state’s investigation.
- **Exam Relevance**: Mandatory citation for questions on "Anticipatory Bail" (Sec. 482 BNSS).

### Case 2: *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985) SC
- **Facts**: A 62-year-old Muslim woman was divorced by her husband via triple talaq. She filed a petition for maintenance under Section 125 of the CrPC. The husband argued that under Muslim Personal Law, he was only bound to maintain her during the *Iddat* period.
- **Issue**: Does Section 125 of the CrPC apply to divorced Muslim women, overriding their personal law?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that Section 125 is a **secular, welfare-oriented provision** designed to prevent vagrancy. It applies to all citizens regardless of their religion. The husband’s liability to maintain his wife continues even after *Iddat* if she is unable to maintain herself.
- **Exam Relevance**: Mandatory citation for "Maintenance of Wives, Children, and Parents" (Sec. 144 BNSS).

## 📝 Module 5: Charge, Trials and Investigation Procedures

### Case 1: *State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal* (1992) SC
- **Facts**: An FIR was registered against a former Chief Minister alleging corruption. The accused sought to quash the FIR in the High Court under Section 482 CrPC (Sec. 528 BNSS).
- **Issue**: Under what circumstances can the High Court exercise its inherent power to quash an FIR or criminal proceedings?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court laid down **7 categories/guidelines** where the High Court can quash an FIR. These include: (i) where the allegations do not disclose a prima facie offence, (ii) where the allegations are absurd or inherently improbable, and (iii) where the proceeding is maliciously instituted with an ulterior motive.
- **Exam Relevance**: Essential case for questions on "Inherent Powers of the High Court" (Sec. 528 BNSS).

### Case 2: *K. Anbazhagan v. Superintendent of Police* (2004) SC
- **Facts**: A petition was filed to transfer a corruption trial against a Chief Minister from Tamil Nadu to another state, alleging that a fair trial was impossible in the home state due to political influence.
- **Issue**: Under what circumstances can a criminal trial be transferred to another state?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that a **fair trial** is a fundamental right. If there is a reasonable apprehension that justice will be compromised or witnesses threatened, the court must exercise its power to transfer the case (Sec. 406 CrPC / Sec. 446 BNSS) to ensure the integrity of the trial.
- **Exam Relevance**: Cite in Module 05 for "Concept of Fair Trial" or "Transfer of Criminal Cases."

## ⚖️ Module 6: Judgment, Appeals, Revision & Execution

### Case 1: *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) SC
- **Facts**: The constitutional validity of the death penalty under Section 354(3) CrPC (Sec. 393 BNSS) was challenged as violating Articles 14, 19, and 21.
- **Issue**: Is the death sentence constitutional, and how should courts exercise their discretion in sentencing?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the death penalty. However, it ruled that the death penalty must be restricted to the **"rarest of rare cases"** where the alternative option of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed.
- **Exam Relevance**: Essential citation for "Judgment" and "Sentencing Powers" questions.

### Case 2: *Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Admn.)* (1988) SC
- **Facts**: The petitioner, convicted of conspiracy in the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, petitioned the President for mercy/pardon. The petition was rejected.
- **Issue**: Is the executive power of pardon, suspension, or commutation subject to judicial review?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court held that the President’s power of pardon under Article 72 (and Governor under Art. 161) is an executive power. The court cannot inspect the merits of the President's decision but can review if the decision was arbitrary, discriminatory, or based on irrelevant considerations.
- **Exam Relevance**: Use for questions on "Execution, Suspension, Remission, and Commutation of Sentences" (Sec. 453-477 BNSS).

## 🧒 Module 7: Juvenile Justice Act, 2015

### Case 1: *Subramanian Swamy v. Raju* (2014) SC
- **Facts**: In the aftermath of the 2012 Delhi gang rape, a petition challenged the JJ Act, 2000’s age limit of 18, arguing that minors committing heinous crimes should be tried in adult criminal courts.
- **Issue**: Can the court lower the age bar of 18 for heinous offences?
- **Held**: The Supreme Court upheld the age of 18 as constitutional, stating that the legislature is competent to draw a bright-line rule. However, this sparked the legislative change leading to the **JJ Act, 2015**, which introduced Section 15 permitting adult trial for heinous offences committed by 16-18 year olds.
- **Exam Relevance**: Mandatory citation for "Preliminary Assessment" and "Juvenile Jurisdiction."

### Case 2: *Sampurna Behrua v. Union of India* (2018) SC
- **Facts**: A PIL highlighted the widespread failure of state governments to constitute functioning Juvenile Justice Boards (JJBs) and Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) and the poor state of Child Care Institutions.
- **Issue**: Failure of the state to implement the statutory machinery of the JJ Act.
- **Held**: The Supreme Court ordered the mandatory setup of functional JJBs and CWCs in every district, the recruitment of trained personnel, and regular social and infrastructural audits of all child-care homes.
- **Exam Relevance**: Cite when discussing "Institutional Mechanisms" or "JJB/CWC Administration."
