# Module 2: Jurisdiction of Criminal Courts in Inquiry and Trials

## Introduction

Jurisdiction is the authority of a court to hear and decide a case. In criminal proceedings, jurisdiction determines which court has the power to inquire into or try a particular offence. The BNSS prescribes detailed rules regarding the jurisdiction of criminal courts, initiation of inquiry proceedings, complaints to magistrates, and inquiry proceedings before magistrates. The proper exercise of jurisdiction is fundamental to the validity of any criminal proceeding.

> **Key Principle**: A court must have jurisdiction over (a) the subject matter, (b) the person, and (c) the territory to validly try a case. A trial conducted without jurisdiction is a nullity.

### A. Place of Inquiry or Trial (Secs. 197-202)

#### Sec. 197 — Ordinary Place of Inquiry and Trial
- Every offence shall ordinarily be inquired into and tried by a court within whose **local jurisdiction** it was committed

#### Sec. 198 — Offence Committed on Journey or Voyage
When an offence is committed while a person is on a journey or voyage, it may be inquired into or tried by a court through whose jurisdiction:
- The person passed during the journey
- The journey or voyage terminates

#### Sec. 199 — Offence Including Several Particulars
When an offence includes **several particulars** committed in different local jurisdictions:
- It may be inquired into or tried by a court having jurisdiction over any of such local areas

#### Sec. 200 — Continuing Offence
Where the offence is a **continuing offence** committed in more than one local jurisdiction:
- It may be inquired into or tried by a court having jurisdiction over any of such local areas

#### Sec. 201 — Place of Trial Where Act is an Offence by Reason of Relation to Other Offence
When an act is an offence by reason of its relation to another offence:
- The charge may be inquired into or tried by a court competent to try either offence

#### Sec. 202 — Offence Committed by Letter
When an offence is committed by means of a **letter or electronic communication**:
- It may be inquired into or tried at the place where such letter or communication was sent, or where it was received

**New in BNSS**: The inclusion of "electronic communication" in Sec. 202 addresses cyber crimes and digital offences — absent in corresponding CrPC provision (Sec. 182 CrPC).

---

### B. Conditions for Initiation of Proceedings (Secs. 203-209)

#### Sec. 203 — Power to Order Cases to be Tried Together
When several persons are associated in a single design, the Magistrate may try them jointly, even if they are accused of different offences.

#### Sec. 204 — Where Doubt Exists as to Jurisdiction
When it is uncertain in which of several local areas an offence was committed:
- It may be inquired into or tried by a court having jurisdiction over any of these areas

#### Sec. 205 — Power to Transfer Cases
- The Sessions Judge may transfer any case from one Criminal Court to another subordinate to him
- The Magistrate may transfer complaint cases

#### Sec. 206 — Withdrawal of Cases by Sessions Judge
The Sessions Judge may withdraw any case or class of cases from any Magistrate within the sessions division and try or commit for trial.

#### Sec. 207 — Power of Magistrate to Direct Investigation
A Magistrate competent to take cognizance may direct an investigation by police, even if the case would normally be triable by another court.

#### Sec. 208 — Accused Outside India
When a person accused of an offence committed in India is outside India:
- They may be inquired into or tried as if the offence was committed at any place in India at which they may be found

#### Sec. 209 — Offences Committed Outside India
Any offence committed outside India which may, by law, be inquired into or tried in India:
- Shall be inquired into or tried according to the provisions of this Sanhita

## 2. Initiation of Inquiry Proceedings (Secs. 210-214)

### Sec. 210 — Cognizance of Offences by Magistrates

This is a **critical provision** — it specifies the modes by which a Magistrate takes cognizance of offences.

A Magistrate of the first class, or any Magistrate specially empowered, may take cognizance of any offence:

**(a) Upon receiving a complaint** of facts constituting such offence

**(b) Upon a police report** of such facts (charge sheet under Sec. 193)

**(c) Upon information received from any person other than a police officer**, or upon his own knowledge, that such offence has been committed

> **Cognizance** means the court taking judicial notice of an offence. It is a purely judicial function. Taking cognizance does not mean issuance of process — it means application of judicial mind.

**Landmark Case**: *R.R. Chari v. State of UP* (1951) — Taking cognizance does not involve any formal action but occurs when a Magistrate applies his mind to the suspected commission of an offence.

---

### Sec. 211 — Transfer by Magistrate to Another Magistrate
A Magistrate taking cognizance may, if satisfied that the case should be tried by another Magistrate, transfer the case to such Magistrate.

### Sec. 212 — Magistrate May Dispense with Attendance of Accused
In certain minor cases, the Magistrate may dispense with the personal attendance of the accused and permit appearance by pleader.

### Sec. 213 — Special Provision for Cases Under Certain Acts
Special provisions for cases under specific Acts where cognizance is taken by specific courts.

### Sec. 214 — Prosecution for Contempt of Lawful Authority
Courts may take cognizance of offences relating to contempt of lawful authority of public servants.

## 3. Exceptions to the General Principle of Moving Criminal Courts (Secs. 215-222)

These provisions require **prior sanction or approval** before courts can take cognizance of certain offences. They act as safeguards against frivolous prosecutions.

### Sec. 215 — Prosecution of Judges and Public Servants
No Court shall take cognizance of any offence alleged to have been committed by a Judge, Magistrate, or public servant acting in discharge of official duty, **except with the previous sanction** of:
- Central Government (for Union employees)
- State Government (for State employees)

> **Purpose**: To protect public servants from harassment through frivolous prosecutions for acts done in good faith during official duty.

**Landmark Case**: *Matajog Dobey v. H.C. Bhari* (1956) — The protection is intended for acts done in official capacity, not private acts.

### Sec. 216 — Previous Sanction for Prosecution
Prosecution for certain offences requires prior sanction:
- Offences under specific BNS sections
- The sanctioning authority must apply its mind to the facts

### Sec. 217 — Prosecution for Offences Against Marriage
No court shall take cognizance of offences under Secs. 82-86 of BNS (marriage offences) except upon complaint by:
- The aggrieved person, or
- Parent or guardian (where the person is minor or of unsound mind)

### Sec. 218 — Prosecution Sanction — 120-Day Deemed Sanction
The Central or State Government shall decide on a request for sanction within **120 days**. If the Government fails to decide within this period, the sanction shall be **deemed to have been accorded**.

> **New in BNSS**: This deemed sanction provision is a significant reform to prevent governments from blocking prosecutions by inaction.

### Sec. 219 — Limitation for Taking Cognizance
No court shall take cognizance of certain offences after the expiry of the prescribed limitation period.

### Sec. 220-222 — Other Restrictions
Various restrictions on taking cognizance for specific categories of offences (defamation, matrimonial offences, etc.).

## 4. Complaint to Magistrates (Secs. 223-226)

### Sec. 223 — Examination of Complainant
Upon receiving a complaint, the Magistrate shall:
- **Examine the complainant and witnesses on oath** (mandatory)
- The substance of the examination shall be reduced to writing
- Signed by the complainant and witnesses, and also by the Magistrate

**Exception**: This examination is not required when the complaint is made by a public servant acting in discharge of duty or by a court.

### Sec. 224 — Postponement of Issue of Process
The Magistrate may, if he thinks fit, postpone the issue of process and direct an **investigation by police** or an inquiry by another Magistrate.

### Sec. 225 — Dismissal of Complaint
If, after examining the complainant and witnesses, the Magistrate finds that there are **no sufficient grounds for proceeding**, he shall:
- **Dismiss the complaint**
- Record reasons briefly in writing
- The complainant shall be informed

> **Important**: The Magistrate must apply his judicial mind before dismissing — he cannot dismiss mechanically.

### Sec. 226 — Issue of Process
If the Magistrate is satisfied that there are sufficient grounds for proceeding:
- He shall issue **summons** to the accused (for summons case)
- He shall issue **warrant** if the case so requires (for warrant case)
- He may direct that the case be tried by a competent Magistrate

## 5. Inquiry Proceedings Before Magistrates (Secs. 227-232)

### Sec. 227 — Commencement of Proceedings Before Magistrate
When an accused appears or is brought before a Magistrate:
- The Magistrate shall proceed to hear the prosecution and take evidence

### Sec. 228 — Procedure When Magistrate Is Not Competent
When the Magistrate finds that the offence is exclusively triable by Court of Session or by another Magistrate:
- He shall **commit the case** to the Court of Session, or
- Transfer the case to the competent Magistrate

### Sec. 229 — Commitment of Case to Court of Session
When the offence is triable exclusively by the Court of Session:
1. The Magistrate shall commit the case to the Court of Session
2. Supply copies of documents to the accused (Sec. 230)
3. Send the case to the Court of Session

### Sec. 230 — Supply of Copies to Accused
In every case committed to the Court of Session, the Magistrate shall supply the accused with:
- Copy of the police report
- Copy of the FIR
- Statements of witnesses recorded under Sec. 183
- Confessions and statements recorded under Sec. 183
- Any other document relied upon by the prosecution

> This provision ensures the accused has **full access to prosecution material** — a cornerstone of fair trial.

### Sec. 231 — Trial Before Magistrate
When the Magistrate is competent to try the case, he shall proceed with the trial according to the provisions for:
- Warrant cases (Secs. 261-273)
- Summons cases (Secs. 274-282)

### Sec. 232 — Sessions Judge to Dispose of Committed Cases
The Court of Session shall proceed to try the case committed to it in accordance with Secs. 248-260.

## Landmark Judgments

### 1. R.R. Chari v. State of UP (1951)
- **Issue**: What constitutes "taking cognizance"?
- **Held**: Taking cognizance is a mental act; it occurs when the Magistrate applies his mind to the suspected commission of an offence

### 2. Matajog Dobey v. H.C. Bhari (1956)
- **Issue**: Scope of sanction for prosecution of public servants
- **Held**: Protection is for acts done in official capacity; court must determine whether the act was connected with official duty

### 3. Subramanian Swamy v. Manmohan Singh (2012)
- **Issue**: Whether government can indefinitely delay sanction for prosecution
- **Held**: Government must decide sanction within a reasonable time; indefinite delay amounts to denial of justice

### 4. H.S. Bains v. State (1980)
- **Issue**: Powers of Magistrate at the stage of taking cognizance
- **Held**: Magistrate must apply judicial mind; cannot take cognizance mechanically

### 5. Priyanka Srivastava v. State of UP (2015)
- **Issue**: Safeguards before summoning accused in complaint cases
- **Held**: Magistrate should carefully scrutinize the complaint and must examine the nature of allegations before issuing process

## Model 5 Marks Answer

**Q: What is cognizance? How can a Magistrate take cognizance under BNSS?**

**Cognizance** means the judicial notice taken by a court of an offence. It is a mental act of the Magistrate applying his mind to the suspected commission of an offence (*R.R. Chari v. State of UP*, 1951).

Under **Sec. 210 BNSS**, a Magistrate of the first class may take cognizance in three ways:
1. **Upon receiving a complaint** of facts constituting the offence
2. **Upon a police report** (charge sheet under Sec. 193)
3. **Upon information from any person or upon his own knowledge** that the offence has been committed

Taking cognizance does not mean issuance of process; it simply means the Magistrate has applied his judicial mind to the facts presented. Only after cognizance does the Magistrate proceed to examine the complainant (Sec. 223) or issue process (Sec. 226).

## Model 10 Marks Answer

**Q: Discuss the exceptions to the general principle of moving criminal courts, with special reference to prosecution of public servants under BNSS.**

**Introduction**: While any person can set the criminal law in motion through a complaint or FIR, certain safeguards exist to prevent abuse of the criminal process. These are "exceptions" or "restrictions" on taking cognizance.

**General Principle**: Under Sec. 210 BNSS, a Magistrate may take cognizance of any offence upon complaint, police report, or upon information received. However, for certain categories of offences, prior sanction or approval is required.

**Prosecution of Public Servants (Sec. 215)**: No court shall take cognizance of an offence alleged to have been committed by a Judge, Magistrate, or public servant while acting in discharge of official duty, except with the **previous sanction** of the Central or State Government. The purpose is to protect honest public servants from frivolous and vexatious prosecution (*Matajog Dobey v. H.C. Bhari*, 1956). The sanction requirement applies only to acts done in official capacity, not private acts.

**120-Day Deemed Sanction (Sec. 218)**: A significant reform in BNSS. If the government fails to decide on a sanction request within **120 days**, the sanction is **deemed to have been accorded**. This prevents governments from indefinitely blocking prosecutions by inaction, addressing the concern raised in *Subramanian Swamy v. Manmohan Singh* (2012).

**Marriage Offences (Sec. 217)**: Cognizance of offences against marriage (Secs. 82-86 BNS) can only be taken on complaint by the aggrieved person, parent, or guardian.

**Limitation (Sec. 219)**: Certain offences have limitation periods beyond which cognizance cannot be taken.

**Conclusion**: These exceptions balance the need for accessible criminal justice with protection against its abuse. The deemed sanction provision under Sec. 218 is a significant improvement over CrPC, ensuring accountability of the executive.
