# Module 1: Introduction to Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023

## Introduction

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) is the primary procedural law governing criminal proceedings in India. It replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) and came into force on **1st July 2024**. The BNSS consolidates and amends the law relating to criminal procedure, aiming to ensure speedy justice, transparency in investigation, and a victim-centric criminal justice system.

Criminal procedure is the mechanism through which substantive criminal law (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023) is enforced. While substantive law defines offences and punishments, procedural law prescribes the method by which offenders are investigated, tried, and punished. A fair and just procedure is the cornerstone of the rule of law.

> **Key Point**: The BNSS is Act No. 46 of 2023, received Presidential assent on 25th December 2023, and consists of 531 sections spread across 44 chapters, replacing the CrPC which had 484 sections.

---

## 1. Criminal Justice System: Inquisitorial and Adversarial/Accusatorial

### A. Inquisitorial System

The Inquisitorial System is followed in countries such as France, Germany, and other civil law jurisdictions.

**Key Features:**
- The **judge plays an active role** in investigating the case and gathering evidence
- The judge directs the proceedings, questions witnesses, and determines the truth
- There is **no separate investigation agency** — the judge acts as both investigator and adjudicator
- The accused has a limited role; the entire proceeding is judge-driven
- Emphasis is on **discovering the truth** rather than establishing guilt beyond reasonable doubt
- The accused is expected to cooperate and may be questioned by the court

**Advantages:**
- Efficient in fact-finding
- Less adversarial, reducing litigation tactics
- Comprehensive investigation by a trained judicial officer

**Disadvantages:**
- Risk of **bias** as the same authority investigates and decides
- Potential violation of the right against self-incrimination
- Less protection for the accused

---

### B. Adversarial/Accusatorial System

The Adversarial System is followed in India, England, the USA, and other common law jurisdictions.

**Key Features:**
- The **judge acts as a neutral umpire** between the prosecution and the defence
- Two opposing parties (prosecution and defence) present their cases independently
- The **burden of proof** lies on the prosecution to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt
- The accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty
- Cross-examination is a fundamental feature
- The judge does not independently investigate; the court relies on evidence presented by the parties

**Advantages:**
- Ensures **fairness** through neutral adjudication
- Protects the rights of the accused
- Promotes adversarial testing of evidence through cross-examination
- Upholds the presumption of innocence

**Disadvantages:**
- Can be time-consuming and expensive
- Outcome may depend on the skill of lawyers rather than merits
- May not always lead to truth discovery

---

### C. Indian Position

India follows the **Adversarial System** with certain modifications:

- The judge can question witnesses under Sec. 165 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)
- The court can summon material witnesses under Sec. 313 BNSS
- The court can order further investigation
- **Malimath Committee (2003)** recommended incorporating some inquisitorial features, but India largely retains the adversarial model

| Feature | Inquisitorial | Adversarial (Indian) |
|---------|---------------|----------------------|
| Role of Judge | Active investigator | Neutral umpire |
| Burden of Proof | Shared | On prosecution |
| Presumption | No presumption of innocence | Presumed innocent |
| Cross-examination | Limited | Fundamental right |
| Countries | France, Germany | India, UK, USA |

## 2. International Norms and Constitutional Perspectives

### A. International Norms on Criminal Justice

Key international instruments that shape criminal justice principles:

1. **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948**
   - Article 9: No arbitrary arrest or detention
   - Article 10: Right to fair and public hearing
   - Article 11: Presumption of innocence

2. **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), 1966**
   - Article 9: Right to liberty and security of person
   - Article 14: Right to a fair trial
   - Article 15: Protection against retroactive criminal law

3. **UN Standard Minimum Rules for Treatment of Prisoners (Mandela Rules)**

4. **UN Body of Principles for Protection of Persons under Detention**

---

### B. Constitutional Perspectives

The Constitution of India provides fundamental safeguards for persons accused of criminal offences:

#### Article 14 — Equality Before Law
- Ensures equal protection of law in criminal proceedings
- No discrimination in the application of criminal procedure
- Applies to investigation, trial, and punishment
- **Landmark Case**: *Budhan Choudhary v. State of Bihar* (1955) — Classification must be reasonable and have a rational nexus with the object

#### Article 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences
Three key protections:
1. **No ex post facto laws** (Art. 20(1)): No person shall be convicted of an offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of commission
2. **No double jeopardy** (Art. 20(2)): No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once
3. **No self-incrimination** (Art. 20(3)): No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself
- **Landmark Case**: *Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani* (1978) — Right against self-incrimination extends to police interrogation

#### Article 21 — Protection of Life and Personal Liberty
- No person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law
- The procedure must be **just, fair, and reasonable** — *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)
- Includes: Right to speedy trial, right to legal aid, right to bail, right against custodial torture
- **Landmark Cases**:
  - *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979) — Right to speedy trial
  - *D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997) — Guidelines on arrest

#### Article 22 — Protection Against Arrest and Detention
- Art. 22(1): Right to be informed of grounds of arrest
- Art. 22(2): Right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner
- Art. 22(2): Right to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours of arrest
- Art. 22(4)-(7): Provisions regarding preventive detention

## 3. Rationale and Salient Features of BNSS, 2023

### Rationale of Criminal Procedure

Criminal procedure serves several critical purposes:
1. **Protection of individual rights** — Ensures the accused is not arbitrarily deprived of liberty
2. **Balance of interests** — Balances the rights of the accused with the interest of society
3. **Due process** — Ensures fair, just, and reasonable procedure
4. **Efficiency** — Provides mechanisms for speedy disposal of cases
5. **Accountability** — Holds police, prosecution, and judiciary accountable

---

### Salient Features of BNSS, 2023

The BNSS introduces several significant changes:

#### (i) Specific Timelines for Investigation and Trial
- **Charge framing**: Within 60 days from first hearing (Sec. 263)
- **Judgment**: Within 45 days of completion of trial (Sec. 392)
- **Sessions Court verdict**: Within 30 days (extendable to 45) after arguments (Sec. 258)

#### (ii) Use of Technology
- **E-FIR**: Registration of FIR through electronic means (Sec. 173)
- **Audio-video recording**: Mandatory recording of search and seizure (Sec. 105, 185)
- **Electronic service**: Summons through electronic communication (Sec. 64)
- **Video conferencing**: Examination of witnesses by audio-video means (Sec. 265)

#### (iii) Victim-Centric Provisions
- Victim defined to include guardian or legal heir (Sec. 2(y))
- Victim to be heard before withdrawal from prosecution (Sec. 360)
- IO to inform victim of investigation progress within 90 days (Sec. 193)
- Proceeds of crime to be distributed among victims (Sec. 107)

#### (iv) Speedy Justice
- Trial in-absentia of Proclaimed Offenders (Sec. 356)
- Prosecution sanction deemed granted if not decided within 120 days (Sec. 218)
- Specific timelines at every stage of trial

#### (v) Forensic Investigation
- Mandatory forensic evidence collection for offences punishable with 7 years or more (Sec. 176)

#### (vi) Handcuff Provisions
- Use of handcuffs permitted in specific cases: habitual offenders, organized crime, terrorism, murder, rape (Sec. 43)

---

### Organisation of Police, Prosecution & Defence

#### Police Organisation
- **Police** are the primary agency for investigation under the BNSS
- Officer in charge of police station registers FIR, investigates cognizable offences
- Hierarchy: Constable → Sub-Inspector → Inspector → DySP → SP → DIG → IG → DGP
- **Duties**: Maintenance of law and order, prevention and detection of crime, arrest, investigation

#### Prosecution Organisation
- **Public Prosecutor (Sec. 18)**: Appointed for High Court and district courts; must have 7 years of practice
- **Special Public Prosecutor (Sec. 18(8))**: For specific cases; must have 10 years of practice
- **Assistant Public Prosecutor (Sec. 19)**: For cases in Magistrate's courts
- **Directorate of Prosecution (Sec. 20)**: State-level body headed by Director of Prosecution

#### Defence
- Every accused has the right to be defended by a counsel of their choice
- Right to free legal aid under Article 39A and Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987
- Defence counsel has the right to cross-examine prosecution witnesses

#### Prison Authorities
- Governed by the Prisons Act, 1894 and relevant state prison manuals
- Responsible for custody and rehabilitation of convicted persons

## 4. Object, Scope, and Significance of BNSS, 2023

### Object
- To consolidate and amend the law relating to criminal procedure
- To ensure speedy justice, transparency, and accountability
- To make the criminal justice system victim-centric and technology-driven

### Scope
- The BNSS applies to the **whole of India** except:
  - Nagaland (except Chapters IX, XI, XII)
  - Tribal areas as defined in the Sixth Schedule
- It governs all stages of criminal proceedings: investigation, inquiry, trial, appeal, revision, and execution
- It does not affect any special or local law in force (Sec. 5 — Saving clause)

### Significance
- Replaces the 50-year-old CrPC with a modernized procedural code
- Introduces technology at every stage of the criminal process
- Prescribes specific timelines to prevent delayed justice
- Strengthens victim rights and participation

## 5. Definitions under Section 2 of BNSS, 2023

Section 2(1) provides key definitions:

| Term | Definition (Sec. 2) | Significance |
|------|---------------------|--------------|
| **Audio-video electronic means** (a) | Use of communication device for video conferencing, recording of search/seizure/evidence | New definition — reflects technology integration |
| **Bail** (b) | Release from custody upon conditions, on execution of bond/bail bond | Comprehensive definition (not defined in CrPC) |
| **Bailable offence** (c) | Offence shown as bailable in First Schedule | Bail as of right |
| **Bail bond** (d) | Undertaking for release with surety | Distinguished from personal bond |
| **Bond** (e) | Personal undertaking for release without surety | Distinguished from bail bond |
| **Charge** (f) | Includes any head of charge | Broad definition |
| **Cognizable offence** (g) | Offence where police may arrest without warrant | FIR mandatory |
| **Complaint** (h) | Allegation made to Magistrate; excludes police report | Initiates magisterial proceedings |
| **Electronic communication** (i) | Written/verbal/pictorial/video content by electronic device | New definition — wide scope |
| **High Court** (j) | Highest court in the state | Appellate and revisional jurisdiction |
| **Inquiry** (k) | Every inquiry other than a trial, by Magistrate or Court | Distinguished from investigation |
| **Investigation** (l) | Collection of evidence by police officer or authorized person | Core police function |
| **Non-cognizable offence** (o) | Offence where police cannot arrest without warrant | Requires Magistrate's order |
| **Offence** (q) | Any act/omission punishable by any law in force | Broad — includes special laws |
| **Police report** (t) | Report by police officer to Magistrate under Sec. 193(3) | Charge sheet / final report |
| **Summons-case** (x) | Case relating to offence which is not a warrant-case | Simplified trial procedure |
| **Victim** (y) | Person who suffered loss/injury; includes guardian/legal heir | Expanded definition |
| **Warrant-case** (z) | Offence punishable with death, life, or imprisonment > 2 years | More elaborate trial procedure |

### Key New Definitions in BNSS (Not in CrPC)
- **Bail** and **Bail bond** — Now statutorily defined
- **Audio-video electronic means** — Reflects digitization
- **Electronic communication** — Wide definition covering all digital modes
- **Victim** — Expanded to include guardian and legal heir

## 6. Hierarchy and Powers of Criminal Courts (Secs. 6-17, 21-29)

### A. Classes of Criminal Courts (Sec. 6)

Besides the High Courts, every State shall have:

```
High Court
    │
    ├── Court of Session (Sec. 8)
    │       ├── Sessions Judge
    │       └── Additional Sessions Judge
    │
    ├── Judicial Magistrates (Sec. 9)
    │       ├── Chief Judicial Magistrate (Sec. 10)
    │       ├── Additional CJM (Sec. 10)
    │       ├── Sub-divisional Judicial Magistrate (Sec. 10)
    │       ├── Judicial Magistrate First Class (Sec. 9)
    │       ├── Judicial Magistrate Second Class (Sec. 9)
    │       └── Special Judicial Magistrate (Sec. 11)
    │
    └── Executive Magistrates (Sec. 14)
            ├── District Magistrate (Sec. 14)
            ├── Additional District Magistrate (Sec. 14)
            ├── Sub-divisional Magistrate (Sec. 14)
            ├── Executive Magistrate (Sec. 14)
            └── Special Executive Magistrate (Sec. 15)
```

---

### B. Sentencing Powers (Secs. 22-23)

| Court | Maximum Sentence |
|-------|-----------------|
| **High Court** (Sec. 22(1)) | Any sentence authorized by law |
| **Sessions Judge / Addl. Sessions Judge** (Sec. 22(2)) | Any sentence (death requires HC confirmation) |
| **Chief Judicial Magistrate** (Sec. 23(1)) | Up to 7 years imprisonment |
| **Judicial Magistrate First Class** (Sec. 23(2)) | Up to 3 years or fine up to ₹50,000 or both, or community service |
| **Judicial Magistrate Second Class** (Sec. 23(3)) | Up to 1 year or fine up to ₹10,000 or both, or community service |

### C. Key Provisions

- **Territorial divisions** (Sec. 7): State → Sessions Division → District → Sub-division
- **Court of Session** (Sec. 8): Established for every sessions division; presided over by Sessions Judge appointed by HC
- **Subordination** (Sec. 13): CJM subordinate to Sessions Judge; other JMs subordinate to CJM
- **Executive Magistrates** (Sec. 14): Appointed by State Government; DM is the head
- **Special Executive Magistrates** (Sec. 15): Minimum rank of SP or equivalent (**change from CrPC** where DySP rank was sufficient)
- **Mode of conferring powers** (Sec. 26): By name or by official designation
- **Withdrawal of powers** (Sec. 28): HC or State Government may withdraw powers

## 7. Powers of Police Officers and Aid to Magistrates (Secs. 30-34)

### Sec. 30 — Powers of Superior Police Officers
- Police officers superior in rank to an officer in charge of a police station may exercise the same powers throughout their jurisdiction

### Sec. 31 — Public to Assist Magistrates and Police
Every person is bound to assist when reasonably demanded:
- In taking or preventing escape of any person
- In prevention or suppression of breach of peace
- In prevention of injury to public property

### Sec. 32 — Aid to Person Executing Warrant
Any person may aid in executing a warrant if the person to whom the warrant is directed is near and acting in execution

### Sec. 33 — Public to Give Information of Certain Offences
Awareness of commission or intention to commit specified offences under BNS must be reported (Secs. 103-105, 111-113, 140-144, 147-154, 178-182, etc.)

### Sec. 34 — Information to be Given to Magistrate or Police Officer
Information about specified offences shall be given to nearest Magistrate or police officer

## 8. Public Prosecutors and Their Functions (Secs. 18-20)

### Public Prosecutors (Sec. 18)

| Category | Appointment | Qualification |
|----------|-------------|---------------|
| **Public Prosecutor (HC)** | Central/State Govt. after consulting HC | 7 years practice |
| **Additional Public Prosecutor (HC)** | Central/State Govt. | 7 years practice |
| **Public Prosecutor (District)** | State Govt. | 7 years practice; name in DM's panel |
| **Special Public Prosecutor** | Central/State Govt. for specific cases | 10 years practice |

**Key Provision**: The Court may permit the **victim to engage an advocate** to assist the prosecution (Sec. 18(8) proviso).

### Assistant Public Prosecutors (Sec. 19)
- Appointed by State Government for Magistrate's courts
- No police officer who investigated the case can be appointed as APP

### Directorate of Prosecution (Sec. 20)
- **Director of Prosecution**: Monitors cases with 10+ years imprisonment, life, or death; under Home Department
- **Deputy Director**: Monitors cases with 7-10 years imprisonment
- **Assistant Director**: Monitors cases with less than 7 years imprisonment

## Landmark Judgments

### 1. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)
- **Principle**: Procedure established by law under Article 21 must be just, fair, and reasonable
- **Significance**: Expanded the scope of Article 21 to include procedural fairness

### 2. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)
- **Principle**: Right to speedy trial is a fundamental right under Article 21
- **Significance**: Led to release of thousands of undertrial prisoners

### 3. D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997)
- **Principle**: Laid down 11 guidelines to be followed during arrest
- **Significance**: Strengthened the rights of arrested persons; many guidelines now codified in BNSS

### 4. Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978)
- **Principle**: Right against self-incrimination under Art. 20(3) extends to police interrogation
- **Significance**: Accused cannot be compelled to answer questions that may incriminate

### 5. Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of UP (2014)
- **Principle**: Registration of FIR is mandatory under Sec. 154 CrPC (now Sec. 173 BNSS) when information discloses a cognizable offence
- **Significance**: Police cannot refuse to register FIR

## Model 5 Marks Answer

**Q: Explain the salient features of BNSS, 2023.**

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (Act No. 46 of 2023) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 and came into force on 1st July 2024. Its salient features include:

1. **Specific Timelines**: Charge framing within 60 days (Sec. 263), judgment within 45 days (Sec. 392)
2. **Technology Integration**: E-FIR (Sec. 173), audio-video recording of search (Sec. 105), electronic service of summons (Sec. 64)
3. **Victim-Centric Approach**: Victim defined inclusively (Sec. 2(y)), victim hearing before withdrawal (Sec. 360)
4. **Speedy Justice**: Trial in-absentia for proclaimed offenders (Sec. 356), deemed prosecution sanction (Sec. 218)
5. **Forensic Mandate**: Mandatory forensic evidence for offences with 7+ years punishment (Sec. 176)

Thus, the BNSS modernizes India's criminal procedure with technology, timelines, and victim protection.

## Model 10 Marks Answer

**Q: Discuss the hierarchy of criminal courts under BNSS, 2023 and their sentencing powers.**

**Introduction**: Section 6 of the BNSS establishes the hierarchy of criminal courts in every State. This hierarchy is crucial for determining jurisdiction, sentencing authority, and appellate structure.

**Classes of Criminal Courts (Sec. 6)**:
Besides the High Courts, every State shall have: (i) Courts of Session, (ii) Judicial Magistrates of the first class, (iii) Judicial Magistrates of the second class, and (iv) Executive Magistrates.

**Court of Session (Sec. 8)**: Established for every sessions division, presided over by a Sessions Judge appointed by the High Court. Additional Sessions Judges may also be appointed. The Court of Session can pass any sentence authorized by law, but death sentences require confirmation by the High Court (Sec. 22(2)).

**Judicial Magistrates (Sec. 9-13)**: The Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM), appointed by the High Court, heads the Judicial Magistrates in every district. CJM can sentence up to 7 years (Sec. 23(1)). Magistrate First Class can sentence up to 3 years or fine up to ₹50,000 (Sec. 23(2)). Magistrate Second Class can sentence up to 1 year or fine up to ₹10,000 (Sec. 23(3)). A new feature is "community service" as a sentencing option.

**Executive Magistrates (Sec. 14-17)**: Appointed by the State Government. The District Magistrate heads them. They perform administrative and executive functions (maintenance of public order, security proceedings) but do not try criminal cases.

**Sentencing Powers**:

| Court | Maximum Sentence |
|-------|-----------------|
| High Court | Any sentence |
| Sessions Judge | Any sentence (death needs HC confirmation) |
| CJM | 7 years |
| JMFC | 3 years or ₹50,000 fine |
| JMSC | 1 year or ₹10,000 fine |

### Visual Guide: Criminal Court Hierarchy & Sentencing under BNSS
![The Judicial Ladder Infographic](../03-visual-aids/images/court_hierarchy.jpg)

**Subordination (Sec. 13)**: CJM is subordinate to Sessions Judge; all other JMs are subordinate to CJM. Among Executive Magistrates, all are subordinate to the DM (Sec. 17).

**Conclusion**: The BNSS retains the hierarchical structure from CrPC with important additions like community service as a sentencing option and the requirement of SP-rank officers for Special Executive Magistrates (Sec. 15).
