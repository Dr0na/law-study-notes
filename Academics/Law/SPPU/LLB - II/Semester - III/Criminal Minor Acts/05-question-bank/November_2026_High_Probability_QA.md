# November 2026 SPPU Predicted Q&A — Criminal Minor Acts (LDSE 0706)

Reasoned predictions based on the syllabus and April/October 2025 papers; not guaranteed. Current 70-mark format: Part A 2×15, Part B 3×10, Part C 2×5. Verify current commencement and amendments. In particular, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 has phased commencement; do not describe every provision as already operative.

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Define organised crime and explain interception under MCOCA, 1999.

MCOCA was enacted to control organised crime and its economic support. Section 2(1)(e) defines organised crime as continuing unlawful activity by an individual, singly or jointly, as a member of or on behalf of an organised crime syndicate, by use of violence, threat, intimidation, coercion or other unlawful means to gain pecuniary benefits or undue economic/other advantage, with the statutory charge-sheet and cognizance history. Section 2(1)(d) defines an organised crime syndicate as a group of two or more persons acting singly or collectively as a syndicate/gang indulging in organised crime.

Sections 13–16 regulate interception of wire, electronic or oral communication. An application must be made by an authorised officer and supported by the prescribed particulars. The Competent Authority (a senior police officer specified by the Act) may authorise interception only on statutory grounds, such as necessity to obtain evidence of a MCOCA offence, and after considering whether other investigative procedures have been tried and failed or are unlikely to succeed or too dangerous. The authorisation must identify the target, facilities, type/duration and relevant particulars, and remain within the statutory time limit; extensions require fresh justification. Emergency authorisation is confined to the Act’s urgent conditions and must be promptly reviewed. Records, minimisation, sealing and handling of intercepted material are regulated. Unlawfully obtained interception may be excluded, and misuse attracts statutory consequences.

Interception is a serious intrusion into privacy and must be necessary, proportionate and procedurally compliant. *People’s Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India*, (1997) 1 SCC 301, interpreted safeguards for telephone tapping under the Telegraph Act; its constitutional privacy principles inform strict compliance but do not replace MCOCA’s own procedure.

**Conclusion.** Organised-crime liability depends on the statutory definition and prior activity conditions. Interception is an exceptional investigative tool requiring prior authority, necessity, narrow scope and safeguards.

### Q2. Explain powers, functions and procedure of the Data Protection Board under the DPDP Act, 2023.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 creates the Data Protection Board of India under section 18 as a digital-first statutory body. Its members must have ability, integrity and standing with relevant expertise, and at least one must be an expert in law. The Board is intended to inquire into contraventions, issue directions and impose penalties under the Act, following a fair procedure.

Section 27 enumerates the Board’s powers/functions on specified triggers, including personal-data-breach intimations, Data Principal complaints, government references and court directions. Section 28 provides inquiry procedure; the Board must give the affected person a reasonable opportunity to be heard and has limited civil-court powers for specified procedural matters under section 28(7). Section 29 provides appeal to the Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) within the statutory period; section 30 makes Appellate Tribunal orders executable as decrees. Section 31 permits mediation where the Board considers a complaint suitable; section 32 concerns voluntary undertakings; section 33 provides penalties. Section 37 enables the Central Government, on the Board’s advice and after repeated penalties, to direct blocking access under statutory conditions. The Board is not a civil court deciding all private disputes; its jurisdiction is confined by the Act.

**Commencement qualification (important for November 2026):** Notification G.S.R. 843(E), dated 13 November 2025, brought sections 18–26 and specified institutional provisions into force immediately; section 27(1)(d) commenced one year later; most substantive provisions, including the rest of section 27, are scheduled for commencement 18 months after publication (13 May 2027). Therefore describe section 27 as the enacted framework and distinguish provisions operative by the date of the examination from those scheduled later. The Board itself was established by notification G.S.R. 844(E), 13 November 2025.

**Conclusion.** The Board provides an independent statutory inquiry and penalty mechanism, but its powers are bounded by notice, hearing, appeal and phased commencement.

### Q3. Explain prohibition, control and regulation under the NDPS Act, 1985.

The NDPS Act consolidates and amends the law relating to narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances, controlling illicit traffic while permitting medical and scientific use under regulation. Section 8 prohibits cultivation, production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport, warehousing, use, consumption, import/export and transhipment except for medical/scientific purposes and in the manner authorised by the Act, Rules or orders.

Sections 9 and 10 empower the Central and State Governments respectively to permit, control and regulate specified activities. The Act establishes central and state authorities/officers and permits licences, permits and conditions for lawful activity. Offences and punishments are in sections 15–40, varying by substance and quantity; “small” and “commercial” quantities are notified for each substance, with intermediate quantities treated under the statutory scheme. Section 37 imposes stringent bail conditions for specified serious offences, requiring an opportunity for the Public Prosecutor and reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty and unlikely to commit an offence while on bail. Search, seizure and arrest powers are controlled by provisions including sections 41–43; section 50 safeguards personal search, and section 52A concerns disposal of seized narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances. Procedural compliance is critical.

**Conclusion.** The Act combines strict prohibition of unauthorised conduct with regulated medical/scientific channels and procedural protections. Quantity, substance, authorisation and search compliance are decisive.

## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. Explain offences and penalties under UAPA.

UAPA, 1967 addresses unlawful associations, terrorist organisations and terrorist acts. Sections 2 and 3 define relevant terms and empower the Central Government to declare an association unlawful, subject to confirmation by the Tribunal under sections 4–5. Section 10 criminalises membership/activities in a declared unlawful association, subject to statutory elements and exceptions. Section 15 defines terrorist act; sections 16–23 provide offences and penalties, with severity depending on death, injury, weapons, financing and other conduct. Section 38 criminalises association with a terrorist organisation with intention to further its activities; section 39 addresses support; section 40 addresses raising funds. Section 43D(5) imposes special bail restrictions for offences under Chapters IV and VI. In *NIA v Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali*, (2019) 5 SCC 1, the Court addressed the bail threshold under section 43D(5); apply later binding developments and current law when analysing a live case. Constitutional safeguards and proof of statutory ingredients remain essential.

### Q5. Explain electronic signatures and electronic governance under the Information Technology Act, 2000.

The IT Act gives legal recognition to electronic records and electronic signatures, subject to exclusions and statutory conditions. Sections 3 and 3A provide for digital and electronic signatures. Section 4 recognises electronic records where law requires writing; section 5 recognises electronic signatures where signature is required. Sections 6–8 facilitate electronic filing, issue/grant of licences and publication by government agencies. Sections 10A recognises contracts formed through electronic means. Certifying Authorities are licensed and regulated under Chapter VI; subscribers must comply with duties such as safeguarding private keys under section 40A and related provisions. Electronic records and signatures support secure transactions but authentication, integrity, attribution and admissibility remain important.

### Q6. Explain data fiduciary obligations under the DPDP Act.

Under the DPDP Act, a Data Fiduciary determines the purpose and means of processing personal data. When operative, sections 4–10 require lawful processing, notice and consent or another permitted use; consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, with clear affirmative action. Data Fiduciaries must provide accessible notices, enable withdrawal, maintain reasonable security safeguards, notify breaches, erase data when the purpose ends subject to legal retention, and provide grievance redressal. Significant Data Fiduciaries have additional obligations, including a Data Protection Officer in India, independent data auditor and periodic impact assessments as prescribed. Children’s personal data has special safeguards, including verifiable parental consent and restrictions on tracking/targeted advertising subject to statutory exceptions. For November 2026, note phased commencement: most substantive obligations are scheduled for May 2027, while institutional provisions are already in force.

## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### MCOCA interception

Sections 13–16 provide for authorised interception of wire, electronic or oral communications for investigation of MCOCA offences, on necessity and statutory grounds. Prior authorisation, defined scope, duration, record-keeping and review safeguards are required. It is not a routine or unrestricted police power.

### NDPS section 50

Section 50 safeguards a person who is to be searched personally by requiring, when the provision applies, that the person be informed of the right to be taken before the nearest Gazetted Officer or Magistrate. The requirement is distinct from searches of premises or vehicles. *State of Punjab v Baldev Singh*, (1999) 6 SCC 172.

### Digital Personal Data Protection Board

The Board is established under DPDP Act section 18. Its inquiry/penalty powers are set out in section 27 and appeal is under section 29. Commencement is phased; state the date-specific status rather than implying all substantive data-fiduciary duties were operative in November 2026.

### Cyber fraud

Cyber fraud includes phishing, impersonation, unauthorised account access, payment fraud and identity misuse. Depending on facts, offences may arise under IT Act provisions such as sections 66C and 66D and other criminal statutes. Preserve electronic evidence, report promptly and use current cybercrime reporting channels.
