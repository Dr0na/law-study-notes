Use these answer patterns when writing the predicted questions. The marks below refer to the marks printed on the question, not a guarantee that a topic will appear. Keep answers relevant and proportionate; do not add padding.

- **15 marks (Part A):** Start with a short introduction and definition; identify the Act and controlling provisions; explain the legal test or procedure under numbered subheadings; discuss exceptions, safeguards or application; support the main propositions with relevant cases and their ratios; conclude with the legal position. Aim for a developed answer of about **450–650 words** (roughly 5–7 handwritten pages, depending on handwriting).
- **10 marks (Part B):** Give a brief introduction; state the key provisions; explain the principal elements/steps in numbered points; include **2–3 directly relevant authorities** where the topic has settled case law; finish with a concise conclusion. Aim for about **300–450 words** (roughly 3–4 handwritten pages).
- **5 marks (Part C):** Define the topic or identify the case; cite the controlling provision; give 3–4 core points and one authority or illustration where appropriate; close in one sentence. Aim for about **120–200 words** (roughly 1–2 handwritten pages).

## Part A — 15-mark answers

### Q1. Explain IPO, FPO, private placement, bonus issue and QIP under the SEBI ICDR Regulations, 2018.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

**Primary market.** A company raises capital by issuing securities to investors. The Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018 (ICDR) govern public issues and specified capital raising by listed entities. Disclosure enables informed investment and allocates responsibility for offer documents.

**IPO.** An initial public offer is the first public offer of securities by an unlisted issuer. The issuer must satisfy eligibility conditions or use the alternative route prescribed by ICDR, appoint registered intermediaries, prepare and file a draft offer document, obtain SEBI observations where required, make prescribed disclosures, and follow book-building/fixed-price, allotment, refund and listing procedures. A prospectus must comply with Companies Act requirements.

**FPO.** A further public offer is a public issue by an already listed issuer. It is subject to ICDR eligibility, disclosure, due diligence, filing, pricing, allotment and post-issue requirements.

**Private placement.** Under Companies Act section 42, securities are offered to a select group of identified persons through a private placement offer-cum-application; the statutory ceiling, payment, separate bank account, allotment and filing rules apply. It cannot be advertised as a public offer. Listed issuers must also consider applicable SEBI requirements.

**Bonus issue.** A company may capitalise eligible reserves and issue fully paid bonus shares to existing shareholders in proportion to holdings, subject to Companies Act section 63, articles, board/shareholder approvals and ICDR conditions. Bonus shares are not a substitute for dividend and do not raise fresh cash.

**QIP.** A qualified institutions placement is a listed issuer’s private placement of eligible securities to qualified institutional buyers under the ICDR framework. It uses a placement document and prescribed pricing, allocation, shareholder approval and post-issue conditions; it is designed to enable efficient capital raising while protecting market integrity.

**Conclusion.** These routes differ in audience, disclosure, eligibility and process. The issuer must select a legally available route and comply with the current ICDR text, Companies Act and exchange requirements.

### Q2. Discuss SEBI’s role, powers and functions in the securities market.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

The Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992 establishes SEBI to protect investors, promote development of the securities market and regulate it. Section 11 states its functions and duties. These include regulating stock exchanges and securities intermediaries; registering and supervising brokers, merchant bankers, investment advisers, portfolio managers, depositories, custodians and other market participants; preventing fraudulent and unfair trade practices and insider trading; regulating substantial acquisition and takeovers; investor education; and market research.

Section 11(4) authorises specified protective and regulatory directions, including suspension of trading, restraint on persons, and impounding of proceeds or securities in accordance with law. Section 11B permits directions and disgorgement in the statutory circumstances. Section 11C provides investigation powers; section 11D permits cease-and-desist orders. SEBI may inspect records, call for information, conduct inquiries and impose monetary penalties through adjudication under the Act. Powers must be exercised for statutory purposes, with notice and opportunity where required.

Appeals from specified SEBI orders lie to the Securities Appellate Tribunal under section 15T, subject to time limits and statutory conditions; a further appeal on a question of law lies to the Supreme Court under section 15Z. SEBI also makes regulations under section 30, including ICDR, LODR, insider trading, takeover and intermediary regulations.

**Conclusion.** SEBI combines market development, investor protection and enforcement. Accountability, procedural fairness and appellate review constrain its regulatory powers.

### Q3. Explain dematerialisation and rematerialisation under the Depositories Act, 1996.

**Model answer — 15 marks**

Dematerialisation converts physical securities into electronic book-entry form; rematerialisation converts electronic holdings back into certificates. The Depositories Act, 1996 creates the statutory framework for depositories, participants, issuers and beneficial owners.

For dematerialisation, an investor opens a demat account with a registered depository participant, submits a dematerialisation request form and certificates, and the participant forwards the request to the depository and issuer/registrar. After verification, the physical certificates are cancelled and the corresponding securities credited electronically. The beneficial owner is recorded in depository records; the depository is treated as registered owner for transfer purposes while the beneficial owner enjoys rights and benefits under section 10.

For rematerialisation, the beneficial owner submits a rematerialisation request through the participant. The depository debits the electronic balance and the issuer/registrar issues physical certificates subject to statutory and procedural requirements. Both processes require matching identity, security details and valid records.

**Advantages** include faster settlement, reduced risk of loss, theft, forgery and bad delivery, easier transfer, consolidation and pledge. **Limitations** include dependence on digital systems, intermediary charges, cyber/security risks, and the need for accurate account and identity records. The depository, participant and issuer have duties under the Act and regulations; beneficial-owner rights are not extinguished by electronic form.

**Conclusion.** Dematerialisation improves market efficiency and settlement security, while rematerialisation remains a statutory option. Both operate through regulated intermediaries and electronic records.
## Part B — 10-mark answers

### Q4. Explain insider trading under the SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

Insider trading involves trading in listed securities while in possession of unpublished price sensitive information (UPSI), contrary to the SEBI Act and PIT Regulations. An insider includes a connected person or a person in possession of or having access to UPSI. UPSI is information not generally available that, if generally available, is likely materially to affect the price of securities. The Regulations prohibit communication/procurement of UPSI except for legitimate purposes, performance of duties or legal obligations, subject to safeguards. Listed entities maintain codes of conduct, designated-person controls, trading windows, pre-clearance and structured digital databases. Trading plans and permitted defences are subject to regulatory conditions. SEBI may investigate and impose penalties under section 15G of the SEBI Act, along with other remedies. The policy protects market fairness and investor confidence.

### Q5. Explain listing obligations and disclosure principles under SEBI LODR.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 impose continuing duties on listed entities. Core principles include timely and accurate disclosure, equal treatment of shareholders, transparency, accountability and protection of investor rights. Requirements include board and committee governance, independent directors and women directors where prescribed; audit and risk management committees; related-party transaction approval/disclosure; annual reports; website disclosures; investor grievance redressal; and prompt disclosure of material events under Regulation 30 and Schedule III. A listed entity appoints a compliance officer and establishes systems for compliance and record retention. Exact obligations differ by entity category and are subject to amendments and SEBI circulars.

### Q6. Explain inbound and outbound investment under FEMA.

**Model answer — 10 marks**

The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 regulates foreign exchange and cross-border investment. Inbound foreign direct investment is governed by FEMA, Non-Debt Instruments Rules, sectoral caps, entry routes and conditions; some sectors require the government route, while others permit automatic investment subject to conditions. Reporting and pricing requirements apply. Foreign portfolio investment is regulated under SEBI and FEMA rules and differs from strategic FDI. Outbound investment by Indian residents/entities is governed by the Overseas Investment Rules, Regulations and Directions, including permitted financial commitment, overseas direct investment (ODI), portfolio investment, guarantees, reporting and disinvestment rules. Contraventions may attract adjudication and penalties; enforcement is allocated under FEMA. Always verify current sectoral policy, caps and RBI directions because these change.
## Part C — 5-mark short notes

### Minimum public shareholding

**Model answer — 5 marks**

Rule 19A of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Rules, 1957 and SEBI requirements prescribe minimum public shareholding for listed companies, subject to current thresholds, timelines and exemptions. Public float supports liquidity, price discovery and shareholder participation. Quote the current rule rather than relying on an outdated percentage.

### Prospectus

**Model answer — 5 marks**

Under the Companies Act, 2013, a prospectus is a document inviting the public to subscribe for or purchase securities. It must contain prescribed material disclosures. Misstatements can attract civil and criminal liability under sections 34–35 and related provisions, subject to statutory defences.

### SEBI and investor complaints

**Model answer — 5 marks**

SEBI protects investors through market regulation, intermediary supervision, disclosure rules, enforcement and investor education. Investors may use the issuer’s grievance process and SEBI’s online complaint redressal mechanism, while statutory claims and appeals remain governed by the relevant law.

### QIP

A qualified institutions placement is a listed issuer’s private placement to qualified institutional buyers under ICDR. It has eligibility, pricing, allocation and disclosure conditions and facilitates capital raising without a public offer.
