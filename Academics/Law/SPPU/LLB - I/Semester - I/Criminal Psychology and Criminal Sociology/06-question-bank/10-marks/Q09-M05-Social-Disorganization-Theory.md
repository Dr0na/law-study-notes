## 🎯 Introduction

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Social Disorganization Theory explains how breakdown of social institutions and community relationships in certain neighborhoods leads to high crime rates. Developed by Chicago School sociologists in 1920s-1930s, it focuses on characteristics of places (neighborhoods), not individuals.

</div>

## 📖 Concept of Social Disorganization

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Social Disorganization** = Breakdown of effective social controls and institutions in a community.

**Characteristics:**
- Weak family structures
- Low economic status
- High residential mobility
- Ethnic heterogeneity
- Weak community organizations
- Conflicting values

**Result:** Cannot control behavior of residents → High crime rates

</div>

---

## 🏙️ Chicago School Studies (1920s-1930s)

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Key Researchers:** Robert Park, Ernest Burgess, Clifford Shaw, Henry McKay

### Key Findings:

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**1. Crime Concentrated in Specific Areas**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Crime rates highest in certain Chicago neighborhoods - remained high even as populations changed
</div>

<br>

**2. Characteristics of High-Crime Areas**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Poverty, poor housing, high turnover, ethnic diversity, near industrial areas
</div>

<br>

**3. Ecological Approach**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Studied crime like ecologists study plants/animals - mapped crime distribution - found patterns
</div>

</div>

### Concentric Zone Theory (Park & Burgess):

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Cities grow in concentric circles from center:

**Zone I - Central Business District:** Commercial, factories

**Zone II - Transition Zone (HIGH CRIME):**
- Area in transition from residential to commercial
- Deteriorating housing
- High poverty
- High mobility (people move in/out constantly)
- Ethnic diversity
- **Highest crime and delinquency rates**

**Zones III-V:** Crime decreases as move outward - more stable, organized communities

**Key Finding:** Zone II (transition zone) consistently had highest crime rates regardless of who lived there - **place matters more than people**

</div>

</div>

## 🔑 Factors Contributing to Social Disorganization

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**Factor 1: Poverty**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Economic deprivation weakens institutions, limits resources for social control
</div>

<br>

**Factor 2: Residential Instability**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
High turnover - people don't form lasting relationships - weak community ties
</div>

<br>

**Factor 3: Ethnic Heterogeneity**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Diverse groups with different values - difficult to achieve consensus on norms - communication barriers
</div>

<br>

**Factor 4: Family Disruption**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Single-parent households, weak parental supervision - cannot control children effectively
</div>

<br>

**Factor 5: Weak Institutions**

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">
Schools, churches, community organizations weak or absent - no collective efficacy
</div>

</div>

## 💡 Policy Implications

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

**1. Community Organization:** Build/strengthen community organizations

**2. Economic Investment:** Improve economic conditions in disorganized areas

**3. Housing Stability:** Programs to reduce residential mobility

**4. Community Policing:** Build police-community partnerships

**5. Collective Efficacy:** Foster informal social control

</div>

## 🎯 Conclusion

<div style="margin-left: 20px;">

Cohen's theory focuses on **status frustration** leading lower-class boys to form delinquent subcultures with inverted middle-class values. Miller argues delinquency results from conforming to **distinct lower-class culture** with its own focal concerns (TTSEFA), not reaction to middle class.

Both theories explain gang formation and delinquency but differ in explanation - Cohen sees reaction, Miller sees separate culture. Both highlight importance of class culture in shaping behavior and suggest that addressing delinquency requires understanding cultural context, not just punishing individuals.

Social Disorganization Theory shows crime is concentrated in specific areas with weak social controls. Chicago School demonstrated that neighborhood characteristics matter more than individual characteristics - stable organized communities have low crime regardless of poverty. Policy should focus on building community organization and collective efficacy.

</div>
