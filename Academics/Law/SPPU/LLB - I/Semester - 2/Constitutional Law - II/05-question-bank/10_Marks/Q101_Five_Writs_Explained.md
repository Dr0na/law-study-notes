### Introduction (1 mark)

**Article 32 - Right to Constitutional Remedies:**
Article 32 provides the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the **"Heart and Soul"** of the Constitution. Under this Article, Supreme Court can issue **five types of writs** - Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto. These writs are borrowed from English common law and are powerful remedies for protection of rights.

---

### ⚖️ The Five Writs (8 marks)

### 5 Types of Writs (COUNT = 5)

#### 1️⃣ Habeas Corpus 🔓

**Meaning:** Latin phrase meaning **"You may have the body"** or "Produce the body"

**Purpose:**
To release a person from **illegal or wrongful detention**. It is the most important writ for protecting personal liberty.

**When Issued:**
- Person detained illegally without authority
- Detention violates legal procedure
- Preventive detention misused
- Custody without following law

**Against Whom:**
- Public authorities (police, jail, government)
- Private persons (if detention is unlawful)

**Effect:**
- Court directs detaining authority to produce the detained person
- Court examines legality of detention
- If illegal, person released immediately

**Limitations:**
- Cannot be issued if detention is legal and proper procedure followed
- Cannot question merits of detention during emergency (Article 359 suspends remedy)

**Examples:**
- **Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)**: Challenged preventive detention
- **Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983)**: Released person detained after acquittal
- **Kanu Sanyal Case**: Naxalite leader's detention challenged

**Real-Life Example:** Police arrests someone without FIR or proper grounds. Person files habeas corpus; court examines and orders release if detention illegal.

---

#### 2️⃣ Mandamus 📋

**Meaning:** Latin phrase meaning **"We command"**

**Purpose:**
To command a **public official or government** to perform a public duty which they have failed or refused to perform.

**When Issued:**
- Public authority fails to perform legal duty
- Discretionary power exercised illegally
- Administrative action delayed without reason
- Statutory duty not discharged

**Against Whom:**
- Government and government officials
- Public authorities and bodies
- Tribunals performing public duties
- **Cannot** be issued to private individuals

**Essential Conditions:**
1. There must be a **legal duty** to be performed
2. Duty must be **public** in nature
3. Applicant must have **legal right** to performance of that duty
4. No other adequate remedy available

**Limitations:**
- Not issued against President or Governor (Article 361)
- Not for enforcing private contracts
- Not when alternative remedy exists
- Not for policy decisions

**Examples:**
- **University of Mysore v. Govind Rao (1965)**: To compel university to admit student
- **Sukhdev v. Bhagatram (1975)**: Against statutory corporations

**Real-Life Example:** Govt official refuses to issue certificate despite all documents provided. Person files mandamus; court commands official to issue certificate.

---

#### 3️⃣ Prohibition 🚫

**Meaning:** Latin phrase meaning **"To forbid"** or "To prohibit"

**Purpose:**
To **prevent lower courts or tribunals** from exceeding their jurisdiction or acting contrary to law. It is a preventive writ.

**When Issued:**
- Lower court/tribunal exceeding jurisdiction
- Acting without jurisdiction
- Violating principles of natural justice
- Going to decide matter beyond its power

**Against Whom:**
- Lower courts (below High Court)
- Tribunals and quasi-judicial bodies
- **Cannot** be issued against legislative bodies
- **Cannot** be issued against private persons

**When Issued - Timing:**
- Issued **before** the final order/judgment
- Preventive in nature (stops before damage)
- Stops proceedings at early stage

**Difference from Certiorari:**
- **Prohibition**: Before judgment (preventive)
- **Certiorari**: After judgment (curative)

**Examples:**
- To stop trial court from proceeding in case where it lacks jurisdiction
- To prevent tribunal from deciding matter not under its power

**Real-Life Example:** A labor court starts hearing a civil service matter (which only CAT can hear). Party files prohibition; High Court stops labor court from proceeding.

---

#### 4️⃣ Certiorari ✔️

**Meaning:** Latin phrase meaning **"To be certified"** or "To be informed"

**Purpose:**
To **quash the order** of a lower court or tribunal that has:
- Exceeded jurisdiction
- Acted without jurisdiction
- Acted in violation of principles of natural justice
- Committed error of law apparent on face of record

**When Issued:**
- **After** the order/judgment has been passed
- Curative in nature (corrects after damage)
- For jurisdictional errors

**Against Whom:**
- Lower courts and tribunals
- Quasi-judicial bodies
- Administrative authorities exercising judicial functions

**Grounds for Issue:**
1. **Excess of jurisdiction**: Court/tribunal went beyond its powers
2. **Absence of jurisdiction**: Had no power to decide
3. **Error apparent on face of record**: Clear mistake in order
4. **Violation of natural justice**: Principles of fairness violated

**Limitations:**
- Only for jurisdictional errors (not errors of fact/law within jurisdiction)
- Not issued if alternative appeal remedy available
- Discretionary remedy

**Examples:**
- **Province of Bombay v. Khushaldas (1950)**: Sales Tax Tribunal's order quashed
- **Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Syed Ahmad Ishaque (1955)**: Question of disqualification

**Real-Life Example:** Income Tax Tribunal passes order on a matter it has no jurisdiction to decide. Party files certiorari; High Court quashes the order as without jurisdiction.

---

#### 5️⃣ Quo Warranto ❓

**Meaning:** Latin phrase meaning **"By what authority"** or "By what warrant"

**Purpose:**
To **check the authority** of a person holding a **public office** and prevent usurpation of public office by unqualified persons.

**When Issued:**
- Person holding public office not qualified
- Appointment violates law
- Office obtained illegally
- Prevents illegal holding of office

**Against Whom:**
- Person holding substantive public office
- **Must be public office** (not private employment)
- Office must be of public nature

**Essential Conditions:**
1. Office must be **public** and **substantive** (not private or ministerial)
2. Office must be created by **statute or Constitution**
3. Holder must be **legally incompetent** to hold the office
4. Any person can file (not just aggrieved party)

**Not Issued For:**
- Private employment
- Ministerial/administrative posts
- When person has already vacated office

**Examples:**
- **University of Mysore v. Govind Rao (1965)**: University Vice-Chancellor's appointment challenged
- Challenging appointment of public officials who don't meet eligibility criteria

**Real-Life Example:** Person appointed as Vice-Chancellor without required qualifications. Anyone can file quo warranto; court examines qualifications and removes if unqualified.

---

### Conclusion (1 mark)

These five writs are powerful constitutional remedies for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and other legal rights. While Article 32 empowers only the Supreme Court to issue writs for Fundamental Rights, **Article 226** gives High Courts wider power to issue writs for Fundamental Rights as well as other legal rights, making High Courts more accessible forums for justice.

## 🎯 Quick Distinctions (Examiner's Favorite)

### Prohibition vs Certiorari (Most Asked)

| Feature | Prohibition | Certiorari |
|---------|------------|------------|
| **Timing** | **Before** judgment | **After** judgment |
| **Nature** | Preventive | Curative |
| **Purpose** | Stops proceedings | Quashes order |
| **Stage** | During trial | After decision |
| **Effect** | Prohibits further action | Nullifies order |

**Remember**: **PRE**-vents = **PRO**-hibition (comes before)
