### Introduction (1 mark)

**Judicial Appointments Controversy:**
The method of appointing judges in India has evolved through judicial interpretation, culminating in the **collegium system** where judges select judges. The attempt to replace this with **NJAC (National Judicial Appointments Commission)** through the 99th Amendment was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015, reaffirming that **independence of judiciary is part of basic structure** and cannot be compromised.

---

### ⚖️ Evolution of Collegium System (4-5 marks)

**Context:** The appointment system evolved through three "Judges Cases" spanning 1981-1998.

### 3 Judges Cases - Evolution (COUNT = 3)

#### 1️⃣ First Judges Case - S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) 👔
**What it held:** Government has primacy in judicial appointments.

**Interpretation of "Consultation":**
- Article 124 requires President to "consult" CJI
- Court held: "Consultation" does NOT mean "concurrence"
- Government's view can prevail over CJI's opinion
- CJI's opinion merely advisory

**Effect:**
- Government dominated appointments
- Led to concerns about independence
- Political considerations crept in

#### 2️⃣ Second Judges Case - SC Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. UOI (1993) ⚖️
**What it changed:** **Revolutionary shift** - CJI's opinion made binding, collegium born.

**Key Holdings:**

**A. Overruled First Judges Case:**
- First Judges Case incorrectly interpreted
- "Consultation" actually means "concurrence"
- CJI's opinion has primacy

**B. Collegium System Emerged:**
- **CJI + 2 senior-most judges** form collegium
- Collegium recommends names
- President appoints based on recommendation
- Plurality of judges needed (not CJI alone)

**C. Reason for Plurality:**
- Prevents individual bias
- Collective wisdom
- Checks and balances within judiciary

**Effect:**
- Power shifted from Executive to Judiciary
- Independence enhanced
- But transparency concerns arose

#### 3️⃣ Third Judges Case - Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (1998) 👨‍⚖️👨‍⚖️👨‍⚖️
**What it refined:** Expanded collegium size for better consultation.

**Key Changes:**

**Collegium Expanded:**
- For Supreme Court: **CJI + 4 senior-most judges** (5-judge collegium)
- For High Court: **CJI + 2 SC judges + Chief Justice of that HC**
- Plurality strengthened

**Consultation Process:**
- CJI must consult 4 senior judges
- Their views must be considered
- Consensus preferred
- If 2 disagree, name generally not recommended

**Current System:** Based on this case (since 1998)

---

### 🏛️ NJAC - Creation and Striking Down (4-5 marks)

**Context:** Due to criticism of collegium (opacity, nepotism allegations), government attempted reform through constitutional amendment.

### 99th Amendment - Creating NJAC (2014)

**Constitutional Changes:**
- Added Article 124A (NJAC establishment)
- Added Article 124B (Functions)
- Modified Article 124(2) (Appointment procedure)

**NJAC Composition (6 members):**

1️⃣ **Chief Justice of India** - Chairperson  
2️⃣ **2 senior-most SC judges** - Members  
3️⃣ **Union Law Minister** - Member  
4️⃣ **2 eminent persons** - Nominated by PM + CJI + Leader of Opposition  
   - For 3 years, cannot be renominated  
   - At least one from SC/ST/OBC/minority/women  

**Decision-Making:**
- Any **2 members can veto** a recommendation
- Meant to have checks and balances

**Functions:**
- Recommend appointments to SC and HCs
- Recommend transfers of HC judges
- Ensure broad-based consultative process

### Supreme Court Struck Down NJAC (2015)

**Case:** Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015) - **Fourth Judges Case**

**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Decision**: 4:1 (Justice Chelameswar dissented)  

**Held - NJAC Unconstitutional:**

**4 Main Reasons:**

#### Reason 1: Violates Independence of Judiciary ⚖️
**Primary ground:** Judicial independence is part of basic structure; NJAC undermines it.

- Executive presence (Law Minister) affects independence
- Even with minority (1/6), can influence
- Independence cannot be compromised

#### Reason 2: Veto Power Problematic ⚠️
**Practical issue:** Any 2 members can veto, including Law Minister + one other.

- Law Minister + one eminent person can block
- Gives excessive power
- Can paralyze appointments

#### Reason 3: Eminent Persons Selection Flawed 👥
**Concern:** Process of selecting "eminent persons" not transparent.

- Nominated by PM (Executive influence)
- No clear criteria
- Can be manipulated

#### Reason 4: Constitutional Amendment Cannot Destroy Basic Structure 📜
**Legal principle:** 99th Amendment, though Constitutional, violates basic structure.

- Even Constitutional amendments have limits
- Cannot destroy independence
- Kesavananda doctrine applied

**Result:** NJAC struck down, Collegium system restored

**Dissent:**
- Justice Chelameswar favored NJAC
- Argued collegium has problems too
- NJAC provided some accountability

### 📊 Comparison: Collegium vs NJAC

| Feature | Collegium | NJAC (Struck Down) |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Composition** | CJI + 4 SC judges | CJI + 2 judges + LM + 2 eminent |
| **Executive Role** | ❌ None | ✅ Law Minister |
| **Transparency** | ❌ Low | ✅ Better (intended) |
| **Independence** | ✅ Complete | ❌ Compromised (SC held) |
| **Accountability** | ❌ Low | ✅ Some (intended) |
| **Status** | ✅ Current system | ❌ Struck down (2015) |

---

### Conclusion (1 mark)

While the collegium system has flaws (opacity, lack of transparency, allegations of nepotism), the Supreme Court held that **independence of judiciary is non-negotiable** and more important than other considerations. The striking down of NJAC in 2015 reaffirmed that judicial independence is part of basic structure. However, debates continue on how to reform the collegium to make it more transparent and accountable while preserving independence.
