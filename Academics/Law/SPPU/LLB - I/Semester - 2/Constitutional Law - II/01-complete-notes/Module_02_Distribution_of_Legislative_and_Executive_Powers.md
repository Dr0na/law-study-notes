# Module 2: Distribution of Legislative and Executive Powers

## Introduction

One of the most important features of a federal Constitution is the distribution of powers between the Union (Centre) and the States. The Indian Constitution divides both legislative (law-making) and executive (administrative) powers clearly to avoid conflicts and ensure smooth governance.

---

## 1. The Scheme of Distribution of Legislative Powers

The Constitution distributes legislative powers through the **Seventh Schedule**, which contains three lists:

### The Three Lists

#### **List I - Union List** (Article 246)
- Contains **97 subjects** (originally 97, now varies with amendments)
- Only Parliament can make laws on these subjects
- **Examples**: Defense, Foreign Affairs, Banking, Currency, Railways, Airways, Highways, Atomic Energy, Citizenship, Elections

**Why Union List?**
- Subjects of national importance
- Require uniform policy across the country
- Need centralized control for efficiency

#### **List II - State List** (Article 246)
- Contains **66 subjects** (originally 66, now varies)
- Only State Legislature can make laws on these subjects
- **Examples**: Police, Public Order, Public Health, Agriculture, Local Government, Land, Water, State Taxes

**Why State List?**
- Subjects of local/regional importance
- Better handled by states who understand local needs
- Diversity in implementation based on local conditions

#### **List III - Concurrent List** (Article 246)
- Contains **47 subjects** (originally 47, now varies)
- **Both Parliament and State Legislature** can make laws
- **Examples**: Criminal Law, Criminal Procedure, Marriage and Divorce, Contracts, Civil Procedure, Education, Forests, Economic and Social Planning

**Why Concurrent List?**
- Subjects of common interest to both Union and States
- Allows flexibility and coordination
- Union can provide uniform framework, States can adapt

### Conflict Resolution Rule (Article 254)
**If both Parliament and State make laws on Concurrent subjects:**
- Union law prevails over State law
- State law becomes void to the extent of inconsistency
- **UNLESS**: President has given assent to the State law (then State law prevails in that State)

## 2. Territorial Extent of Union and State Legislature

### Parliament's Territorial Jurisdiction (Article 245)

**General Rule:**
- Parliament can make laws for the **whole of India** or any part of it
- Parliament can make laws for Indian citizens **anywhere in the world** (extra-territorial legislation)

**Examples of Extra-Territorial Laws:**
- Indian Penal Code applies to Indian citizens even outside India for certain offenses
- Citizenship laws apply to Indians abroad

### State Legislature's Territorial Jurisdiction (Article 245)

**General Rule:**
- State Legislature can make laws only for the **whole or any part of that State**
- State laws generally cannot have extra-territorial operation

**Exception:**
- State laws can have extra-territorial effects in some cases if there is sufficient territorial nexus
- Example: A law affecting property owned by state residents but located outside the state

---

## 3. Limitations to the Territorial Jurisdiction of Parliament

Even though Parliament has wide powers, there are some limitations:

### A. Constitutional Limitations
- Parliament cannot violate the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution
- Cannot take away Fundamental Rights beyond reasonable restrictions
- Must follow the amendment procedure for constitutional changes

### B. Federal Limitations
- Generally should not encroach upon State subjects (State List)
- Should respect the federal structure

### C. International Law Limitations
- Must respect international treaties and conventions India has signed
- Cannot make laws violating India's international obligations

### D. Limitations Under Article 368
- Certain amendments require ratification by States
- This includes changes to federal structure, High Courts, representation of States in Parliament

## 5. Residuary Powers (Article 248)

**What are Residuary Powers?**
- Power to make laws on matters **not mentioned** in any of the three lists

**Who Has Residuary Powers in India?**
- **Parliament** (Union) has exclusive power to make laws on residuary subjects
- This is unlike USA where residuary powers are with States

**Examples of Residuary Subjects:**
- When computers and internet emerged, they were not in any list
- Parliament made laws on them using residuary powers
- Goods and Services Tax (GST) was added later through amendment

**Why Union Has Residuary Powers:**
- Ensures national issues can be addressed
- Prevents legislative vacuum
- Reflects India's unitary bias

---

## 6. Expansion of Legislative Powers of Union Under Different Circumstances

Parliament's power can expand beyond the Union List in certain situations:

### A. During National Emergency (Article 250)
- Parliament can make laws on **State List subjects**
- This power lasts for 6 months after the emergency ends
- Such laws become inoperative after that period

### B. When Rajya Sabha Passes Resolution (Article 249)
- If Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by **2/3rd majority** that a State subject has become of national importance
- Parliament can then make laws on that State subject
- Resolution remains in force for **one year** (can be renewed)

### C. To Implement International Agreements (Article 253)
- Parliament can make laws on any subject (even State List) to implement:
  - International treaties
  - Agreements at international conferences
  - Decisions of international bodies
- This overrides the normal division of powers

### D. During President's Rule (Article 356)
- When President's Rule is imposed in a State
- Parliament can make laws for that State on State List subjects
- State Legislature is suspended during this period

### E. By Consent of States (Article 252)
- If two or more State Legislatures pass resolutions requesting Parliament
- Parliament can make laws on State List subjects for those States
- Other States can adopt such laws later by passing resolutions

## 7. Interpretation of Legislative Lists

When disputes arise about which list a subject falls under, courts use certain principles:

### Principles of Interpretation:

#### A. **Liberal and Broad Interpretation**
- Each entry should be given wide meaning
- Shouldn't be interpreted in narrow or technical sense

#### B. **Subject Matter Approach**
- Look at the substance, not just the form
- Determine the true character and pith & substance of the law

#### C. **Pith and Substance Doctrine**
- If a law substantially falls under one list, it's valid
- Even if it incidentally touches another list
- **Example**: A State law primarily about agriculture is valid even if it incidentally affects trade (Union subject)

#### D. **Doctrine of Occupied Field**
- If Parliament has already made a comprehensive law on a Concurrent subject
- State cannot make law on the same matter (field is "occupied")
- State can only supplement, not contradict

#### E. **Harmonious Construction**
- Try to interpret entries so that both Union and State can function
- Avoid interpretation that makes one list redundant
- Balance federal spirit

#### F. **Ancillary/Incidental Powers**
- Power to make laws includes power to make laws on incidental/ancillary matters
- **Example**: Power over railways includes power over railway safety

## 8. Distribution of Executive Powers

### Basic Rule (Article 73 & 162)

**Union's Executive Power (Article 73):**
- Extends to matters where Parliament has power to make laws
- Covers: Union List + Concurrent List + Residuary Subjects

**State's Executive Power (Article 162):**
- Extends to matters where State Legislature has power to make laws  
- Covers: State List + Concurrent List (within the state)

### Important Principle:
"Executive power follows legislative power"
- Whoever can make law on a subject, has executive power over it

---

## 9. Centre-State Co-ordination

The Constitution provides for cooperation between Union and States:

### A. Mutual Duties and Obligations

**Union's Duties to States (Article 256):**
- Ensure State governments function according to Constitution
- Give directions to States for implementing Union laws

**State's Duties to Union (Article 355):**
- Protect States from external aggression and internal disturbance
- Ensure State government functions according to Constitution

### B. Coordination Mechanisms

**1. Inter-State Council (Article 263)**
- Can be established by President
- Investigates and discusses subjects of common interest
- Makes recommendations for better coordination
- **Sarkaria Commission** recommended permanent Inter-State Council

**2. Zonal Councils**
- Created by States Reorganization Act, 1956
- Five zones: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern, North-Eastern
- Forum for discussing inter-state and Centre-State issues

**3. Finance Commission (Article 280)**
- Recommends distribution of financial resources
- Every 5 years
- Ensures financial coordination

**4. Planning Commission / NITI Aayog**
- Plans for economic development
- Coordinates Centre-State planning efforts

**5. National Development Council**
- Consists of Prime Minister, Chief Ministers, Union Ministers
- Reviews development plans
- Ensures cooperation in development

## 10. Inter-Governmental Delegation of Powers

### Can Union and States delegate powers to each other?

### A. Delegation by Parliament to State Governments (Article 258)

**Parliament CAN:**
- Authorize State Governments to exercise Union's executive functions
- This is called "delegation" or "entrustment"
- Consent of State Government **not required**

**Examples:**
- Collection of central taxes by State machinery
- State police enforcing Union laws

### B. Delegation by State to Union Government (Article 258A)

**State Legislature CAN:**
- Authorize Union Government to exercise State's executive functions
- Consent of Union Government **required** (unlike Article 258)

**Example:**
- Centre managing certain state subjects with state's consent

### Important Points:
- Only **executive functions** can be delegated (not legislative powers)
- Legislature cannot delegate essential legislative functions
- The delegating authority remains responsible

---

## 11. Delegation by the Centre

**What Centre Can Delegate:**

1. **To State Governments (Article 258)**
   - Any Union executive function
   - With or without State's consent
   - State must comply (constitutional duty)

2. **To Officers and Authorities (Article 53 & 73)**
   - President exercises executive power through officers
   - Can delegate to Ministers, Secretaries, other officials
   - Normal administrative practice

**What Centre CANNOT Delegate:**
- Cannot transfer legislative power to States
- Cannot delegate essential functions of high constitutional offices
- Cannot delegate judicial powers

---

## 12. Entrustment of State's Power to the Centre

This is covered under Article 258A:

**Process:**
- State Legislature passes a law
- Authorizes Union Government to exercise State functions
- **Requires Union's consent** (unlike Article 258)

**Example:**
- State may request Centre to manage a complex subject requiring expertise
- State may seek Centre's help during crisis

**Difference from Article 258:**
- Article 258: Union → State (Union's choice, State must accept)
- Article 258A: State → Union (State's choice, Union can refuse)

---

## 13. Centre's Direction to the State (Article 256-257)

### Union Can Give Directions to States:

**Article 256 - General Directions:**
- Union can give directions to States for exercising their executive power
- Ensures compliance with Union laws
- States must comply with such directions

**Article 257 - Specific Directions:**

**Regarding Communications:**
- Ensure means of communication (railways, national highways) are not obstructed

**Regarding Infrastructure:**
- Protect railways, postal, telegraph, telephone infrastructure
- Ensure smooth operation of these services

**If State Doesn't Comply:**
- Union can use Article 356 (President's Rule)
- Governor can report to President
- Considered failure of constitutional machinery

---

## 14. All India Services (Article 312)

### What are All India Services?

Services whose members serve both Union and States but are recruited centrally.

### Three All India Services:

1. **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** - Created by Constitution
2. **Indian Police Service (IPS)** - Created by Constitution
3. **Indian Forest Service (IFS)** - Created by Parliament

### Features:

**Recruitment:**
- Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) conducts exams
- Centralized recruitment ensures uniform standards

**Service:**
- Officers serve in States
- Under State Government's control for day-to-day work
- But cadre controlled by Centre

**Discipline:**
- Union Government has say in disciplinary matters
- State cannot arbitrarily dismiss or punish
- Protects officers from state-level pressure

**Why All India Services?**
- Ensures efficient administration across India
- Provides uniform standards
- Officers can be transferred between states
- Maintains national perspective in state administration
- Acts as a unifying force

### Article 312 - Creation of New Services:
- Rajya Sabha can resolve to create new All India Services
- Requires **2/3rd majority** of members present and voting
- Parliament then creates the service by law

---

## 15. Inter-State Council (Article 263)

### What is Inter-State Council?

A constitutional body to promote Centre-State and Inter-State cooperation.

### Constitutional Provision (Article 263):

**President MAY establish Inter-State Council if it appears:**
1. Public interests would be served, OR
2. For inquiring into and advising on disputes between States, OR
3. For discussing subjects of common interest

### Establishment and Composition:

**Sarkaria Commission (1988) Recommended:**
- Permanent Inter-State Council should be established
- Based on this recommendation, Council was established in **1990**

**Composition:**
- **Chairman**: Prime Minister
- **Members**:
  - All Chief Ministers
  - Administrators of Union Territories
  - Union Cabinet Ministers (6) nominated by PM
  - Governors of States under President's Rule

### Functions:

1. **Investigate and discuss** subjects of common interest between Union and States
2. **Make recommendations** for better coordination
3. **Advise** on disputes between States
4. **Discuss** implementation of policies and programs

### Importance:

✅ Forum for resolving conflicts peacefully  
✅ Strengthens federal cooperation  
✅ Gives voice to States in national matters  
✅ Promotes cooperative federalism  
✅ Prevents Centre-State confrontation  

### Limitations:

❌ Only advisory body (recommendations not binding)  
❌ Meets irregularly  
❌ Cannot enforce decisions  
❌ Political will needed for effectiveness

## Important Cases to Remember

### 1. **State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)**
- **Issue**: Whether Union Cess on coal encroaches on State's power over land
- **Held**: Parliament's power over regulation and development of mines includes power to levy cess
- **Principle**: Ancillary powers doctrine

### 2. **State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959)**
- **Issue**: Conflict between Union and State law on Concurrent subject
- **Held**: Union law prevails over State law in case of repugnancy
- **Principle**: Article 254 explained

### 3. **Deep Chand v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1959)**
- **Issue**: What happens when there's a conflict on Concurrent List subject?
- **Held**: If both Parliament and State Legislature have legislated on same matter, Parliamentary law prevails
- **Principle**: Supremacy of Parliamentary law on Concurrent subjects

### 4. **Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967)**
- **Issue**: Interpretation of entries in Lists
- **Held**: Entries should be interpreted broadly
- **Principle**: Liberal interpretation of Legislative Lists
