# Q07: Censorship of Films vs Print Media

## 📝 Question
Why is pre-censorship considered valid for cinematograph films but generally invalid for print media? Discuss with reference to the Cinematograph Act and relevant case laws. (15 Marks)

---

## 🧾 MODEL ANSWER

### 1. 🧾 Answer Synopsis
- **Introduction**: Concept of prior restraint.
- **Print Media**: Pre-censorship is generally unconstitutional (*Brij Bhushan*).
- **Films**: Pre-censorship is valid due to mass impact (*K.A. Abbas*).
- **Cinematograph Act**: Role of CBFC.
- **Conclusion**: Differential treatment based on the medium's impact.

### 2. 🧠 Introduction
Pre-censorship (or prior restraint) means stopping the publication or exhibition of material before it reaches the public. Under Article 19(1)(a), freedom of speech generally implies freedom from pre-censorship. However, Indian law treats print media and cinematograph films differently regarding this rule.

### 3. 📖 Body
#### A. Print Media: Freedom from Pre-Censorship
For newspapers and books, pre-censorship is viewed as a direct assault on the freedom of the press.
- **Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950)**: The Chief Commissioner of Delhi issued an order requiring an English weekly to submit all communal matter for scrutiny before publication. The Supreme Court struck down the order, holding that pre-censorship is a restriction on the liberty of the press and is generally unconstitutional unless it strictly falls under the emergency grounds of Art 19(2).

#### B. Cinematograph Films: Validity of Pre-Censorship
Unlike print, films are subjected to mandatory pre-censorship under the **Cinematograph Act, 1952**. No film can be exhibited publicly unless it is certified by the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC).
- **Why the difference?**: The Supreme Court explained the rationale in the landmark case of **K.A. Abbas v. Union of India (1970)**.
  1.  **Mass Impact**: Motion pictures are able to stir up emotions more deeply than any other product of art. A film combines visual and audio elements, making it highly immersive.
  2.  **Audience**: Films are viewed by a mixed audience of all ages, literacy levels, and social backgrounds in a darkened hall, which lowers the viewer's critical defenses.
  3.  **Potential for Harm**: Because of this profound impact, a bad film can incite violence, communal riots, or moral depravity much faster than a book.

#### C. The Cinematograph Act, 1952
The Act establishes the CBFC, which examines films against the guidelines framed under Section 5B (which mirror the restrictions of Article 19(2)). The Board issues certificates:
- **U**: Unrestricted public exhibition.
- **UA**: Unrestricted, but parental guidance for children under 12.
- **A**: Restricted to adults.
- **S**: Restricted to specialized audiences (e.g., doctors).

### 4. ⚖️ Case Laws
- **S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (1989)**: While upholding film censorship, the SC warned that the standard of censorship must be that of a reasonable, strong-minded human being, not that of a weak or vacillating mind. The state cannot ban a film simply to appease a mob threatening violence.

### 5. 🧾 Conclusion
The differential treatment of print and film is based on the "medium is the message" philosophy. While the written word appeals to the intellect, the moving image appeals to the emotions. Therefore, pre-censorship of films is a valid "reasonable restriction" under Article 19(2) to maintain public order and decency.
