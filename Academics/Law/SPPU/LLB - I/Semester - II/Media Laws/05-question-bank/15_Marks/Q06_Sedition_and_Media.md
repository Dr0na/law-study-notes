# Q06: Sedition and Media

## 📝 Question
What is Sedition? Discuss the conflict between the law of sedition and freedom of the press in India, referring to landmark judicial pronouncements. (15 Marks)

---

## 🧾 MODEL ANSWER

### 1. 🧾 Answer Synopsis
- **Introduction**: Definition of Sedition (Sec 124A IPC).
- **The Conflict**: Free speech vs. State security.
- **Judicial Interpretation**: *Kedar Nath Singh* (Incitement to violence is mandatory).
- **Recent Trends**: Misuse against journalists and the Supreme Court's stay.
- **Conclusion**: Need for reform to protect democratic dissent.

### 2. 🧠 Introduction
Sedition, defined under Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code (now Section 152 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita), criminalizes any act (by words, signs, or visible representation) that brings or attempts to bring hatred, contempt, or excites disaffection towards the Government established by law. It is a colonial-era law originally used to suppress the Indian freedom struggle.

### 3. 📖 Body
#### A. The Conflict: Free Press vs. Sedition
In a democracy, the media's primary job is to criticize the government, expose its flaws, and hold it accountable. However, Section 124A is worded so broadly that any strong criticism could technically be interpreted as causing "disaffection" (which means disloyalty or feelings of enmity). This creates a "chilling effect" on investigative journalism and free speech.

#### B. The Judicial Interpretation: Saving Free Speech
To save the law from being unconstitutional under Article 19(1)(a), the Supreme Court had to read it down.
- **Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962)**: This is the locus classicus on sedition. The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of Section 124A but severely restricted its scope.
  - **The Ruling**: The court held that mere strong words, vigorous criticism, or expressions of disapprobation of government measures do NOT constitute sedition.
  - **The Test**: Sedition only applies if the words have the **"intention or tendency to create public disorder, or disturbance of law and order, or incitement to violence."**

#### C. Misuse Against the Media
Despite the clear guidelines in *Kedar Nath*, the law is frequently misused by police to arrest journalists, cartoonists, and activists who criticize the government. The process itself becomes the punishment, violating the freedom of the press.

#### D. Recent Judicial Trends
- **S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India (2022)**: Recognizing the rampant misuse of the law against free speech, the Supreme Court ordered that the 152-year-old sedition law be kept in abeyance (paused) until the Union Government re-examines it. No new FIRs were to be registered under Sec 124A.

### 4. ⚖️ Case Laws
- **Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab (1995)**: The SC held that merely shouting slogans like "Khalistan Zindabad" does not amount to sedition if it does not incite violence or create public disorder.
- **Vinod Dua v. Union of India (2021)**: The SC quashed a sedition FIR against a journalist for criticizing the government's handling of the COVID-19 pandemic, reiterating the *Kedar Nath* principles.

### 5. 🧾 Conclusion
Sedition and freedom of the press are inherently at odds. While the state has a right to protect itself from violent overthrow, criticism of the government is the lifeblood of democracy. The law of sedition must be strictly confined to acts of violence, or ideally, repealed to prevent its misuse against the media.
