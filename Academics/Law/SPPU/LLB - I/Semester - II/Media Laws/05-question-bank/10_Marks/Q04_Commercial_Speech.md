# Q04: Commercial Speech

## 📝 Question
Is 'Commercial Speech' protected under Article 19(1)(a)? Discuss with the help of case laws. (10 Marks)

---

## 🧾 MODEL ANSWER

### 1. 🧾 Answer Synopsis
- **Introduction**: Meaning of commercial speech (advertisements).
- **Initial View**: Not protected (*Hamdard Dawakhana*).
- **Modern View**: Protected as part of free speech and right to know (*Tata Press*).
- **Conclusion**: Protected, but subject to Art 19(2).

### 2. 🧠 Introduction
Commercial speech refers to speech that proposes a commercial transaction, primarily advertisements. The judicial stance on whether advertisements are protected under the Freedom of Speech and Expression (Article 19(1)(a)) has evolved significantly in India.

### 3. 📖 Body
#### A. The Initial View: No Protection
In the early years, the Supreme Court held that commercial advertisements did not enjoy constitutional protection.
- **Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India (1960)**: The petitioner challenged an Act that banned ads claiming magical cures. The SC upheld the ban, stating that an advertisement is merely a commercial transaction. It does not propagate any social, political, or economic ideas, and therefore, is not a part of freedom of speech.

#### B. The Modern View: Full Protection
The view shifted as the court recognized the public's right to receive information.
- **Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985)**: The court observed that advertising is the primary source of revenue for newspapers. Taxing ads heavily would cripple the press.
- **Tata Press Ltd. v. MTNL (1995)**: This is the landmark case. The SC categorically held that "Commercial Speech" is a part of freedom of speech and expression guaranteed under Article 19(1)(a).
  - **Reasoning**: The public has a "Right to Receive" information about products and services. In a free market economy, the free flow of commercial information is essential.

#### C. Restrictions
While protected, commercial speech is not absolute. It can be restricted under Article 19(2) if it is deceptive, unfair, or promotes illegal/harmful activities (e.g., banning tobacco or liquor ads).

### 4. 🧾 Conclusion
The evolution from *Hamdard Dawakhana* to *Tata Press* shows the judiciary's recognition that in a modern economy, commercial information is vital for consumers. Thus, the right to advertise and the right to consume advertisements are both protected fundamental rights.
