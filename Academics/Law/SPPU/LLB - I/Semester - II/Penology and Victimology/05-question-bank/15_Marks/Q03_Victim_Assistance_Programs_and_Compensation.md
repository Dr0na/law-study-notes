# 👥 Q3. Critically evaluate the evolution of Victim Assistance programs in India. Discuss the statutory provisions for victim compensation under Section 357 and 357A of the CrPC/BNSS and highlight the relevant judicial approaches.

## 1. Introduction
Historically, the criminal justice system was centered almost exclusively on the rights and trial of the accused, while treating the victim as a mere witness for the State. **Victimology** emerged to correct this imbalance, focusing on the rights, support, and restoration of the victim.

In India, victim assistance has evolved from a purely welfare-based concept to a legally enforceable right. Supported by the Indian Constitution and key statutory reforms (such as Section 357 and 357A of the CrPC / BNSS), the legal framework ensures that victims are no longer passive bystanders in the justice process, but active participants with protected rights.

---

## 2. Global and Domestic Legal Foundations

### A. The UN Declaration on Victims' Rights (1985)
Passed by the UN General Assembly on November 29, 1985, this declaration is the global charter for victim advocacy. It outlines four core pillars of victim support:
1.  **Access to Justice & Fair Treatment**: Treating victims with compassion, providing timely access to judicial mechanisms, and informing them of their rights.
2.  **Restitution**: Directing offenders to pay fair restitution to victims, covering physical harm and financial losses.
3.  **Compensation**: Establishing national funds to compensate victims when the offender is unable to pay or cannot be identified.
4.  **Assistance Programs**: Providing comprehensive medical, psychological, and social support through governmental and community agencies.

### B. Constitutional Rights of Victims in India
*   **Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Dignity)**: The right to a fair and speedy trial, legal assistance, and protection from harassment applies to victims just as it does to the accused.
*   **Article 14 (Right to Equality)**: Guarantees equal protection under the law, preventing arbitrary denial of justice or discriminatory treatment of victims during trials.
*   **Article 39A (Equal Justice & Free Legal Aid)**: Mandates that the state provide free legal representation to indigent victims to ensure that economic barriers do not prevent access to justice.

## 3. Statutory Provisions for Victim Compensation

### A. Section 357 CrPC / Section 395 BNSS (Compensation from Fines)
*   **Provision**: Empowers courts to direct the offender to pay compensation to the victim out of any fine imposed as part of their sentence.
*   **Scope**: Compensation can be awarded for losses, injuries, or expenses incurred due to the offense, and can be awarded independently or in addition to imprisonment.

### B. Section 357A CrPC / Section 396 BNSS (State Victim Compensation Scheme)
Introduced through a key amendment in **2009**, this section addressed critical gaps in Section 357:
*   **State-Funded Fund**: Mandates that every state government, in coordination with the central government, establish a victim compensation fund.
*   **DLSA/SLSA Administration**: The fund is administered by the **District or State Legal Services Authority (DLSA/SLSA)**, which assesses and awards compensation.
*   **Key Triggers**:
    1.  The fine imposed under Section 357 is insufficient for rehabilitation.
    2.  The accused is acquitted or discharged, but the victim still requires rehabilitation.
    3.  The offender cannot be identified or traced (e.g., hit-and-run cases).

## 4. Landmark Case Laws

*   **Rudal Shah v. State of Bihar (1983)**: A historic judgment in which the Supreme Court introduced **compensatory jurisprudence for the violation of fundamental rights**. The Court directed the state to pay Rudal Shah Rs. 35,000 in interim compensation for 14 years of illegal confinement, holding that Article 21 empowers courts to award financial relief for state excesses.
*   **Ankush Shivaji Gaikwad v. State of Maharashtra (2013)**: The Supreme Court held that the power of courts to award compensation under Section 357 of the CrPC is **mandatory**, not discretionary. Courts must evaluate and record reasons in every criminal case explaining why they did or did not award compensation.
*   **Mallikarjun Kodagali v. State of Karnataka (2018)**: The Supreme Court held that the right of a victim to prefer an appeal under the proviso to Section 372 of the CrPC is a **real, substantive, and unrestricted right** that does not require prior permission (leave) from the High Court, ensuring direct access to justice.
*   **Laxmi v. Union of India (2014)**: The Supreme Court regulated the sale of acid, established a minimum compensation of Rs. 3 Lakhs for acid attack victims, and mandated that all public and private hospitals provide free medical treatment and reconstructive surgeries to survivors.

---

## 5. Conclusion
Victim assistance is a cornerstone of modern criminal justice. Supported by global frameworks like the 1985 UN Declaration and key domestic reforms (such as Section 357A and the Section 372 appeal rights), the Indian criminal justice system has established robust protections for victims. To ensure these measures are effective, courts must consistently apply compensatory rules, and states must ensure that victim compensation funds are easily accessible to all who need them.
