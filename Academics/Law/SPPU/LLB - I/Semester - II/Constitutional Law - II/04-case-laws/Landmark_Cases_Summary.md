## 📚 MODULE 1: FEDERALISM

### 1. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

**Bench**: 13 judges (largest ever)  
**Article**: Basic Structure Doctrine  

**Facts:**
- Challenge to Kerala land reform laws
- Also challenged Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights
- Built on Golaknath case (1967)

**Issue:**
Can Parliament amend any part of Constitution including Fundamental Rights?

**Held:**
- Parliament can amend Constitution including Fundamental Rights
- **BUT cannot alter or destroy Basic Structure**
- Basic features cannot be amended even with 2/3rd majority

**Basic Structure Includes:**
- Supremacy of Constitution
- Federalism
- Separation of powers
- Judicial review
- Democracy
- Secularism

**Significance:**
- Most important constitutional case
- Balanced Parliamentary supremacy with Constitutional supremacy
- Protects Constitution from destruction

**Exam Relevance:** Always cite when discussing Basic Structure or federalism

---

### 2. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

**Bench**: 9 judges  
**Articles**: 356 (President's Rule), Federalism  

**Facts:**
- Post-Babri Masjid demolition (December 1992)
- President's Rule imposed in MP, Rajasthan, HP, UP (BJP governments)
- Dismissed governments challenged proclamations

**Issue:**
- Is President's satisfaction under Article 356 subject to judicial review?
- Can courts interfere with President's Rule?

**Held (5 Main Rulings):**

**1. Judicial Review Possible:**
- Article 356 not immune from judicial review
- Courts can examine if material existed for President's satisfaction

**2. Governor's Report Not Conclusive:**
- President must independently apply mind
- Cannot mechanically accept Governor's report

**3. Floor Test Preferred:**
- Before dismissing government, floor test should be ordered
- Let Assembly decide majority
- Dismissal last resort

**4. Federalism is Basic Structure:**
- Arbitrary use of Article 356 violates federalism
- States' autonomy must be respected
- Cannot be used for political purposes

**5. Assembly Can Be Restored:**
- If Article 356 misused, courts can restore Assembly
- Can undo unconstitutional dissolution

**Impact:**
✅ Drastically reduced Article 356 misuse  
✅ Floor tests became norm  
✅ Protected federalism  

**Exam Relevance:** Must cite for Article 356 questions

## 📚 MODULE 2: LEGISLATIVE POWERS

### 3. State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)

**Article**: Ancillary powers doctrine  

**Issue:**
Whether Union cess on coal encroaches on State's power over land?

**Held:**
- Parliament's power over mines includes ancillary powers
- Power to levy cess on coal is incidental to mines regulation
- Ancillary powers doctrine established

**Principle:** Power to legislate on a subject includes power on incidental/ancillary matters

---

### 4. Deep Chand v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1959)

**Article**: 254 (Repugnancy on Concurrent subjects)  

**Issue:**
Conflict between UP Sugarcane Act (State law) and Essential Commodities Act (Union law) - both on Concurrent List

**Held:**
- When both Union and State make laws on Concurrent subject
- Union law prevails over State law
- State law void to extent of inconsistency
- Article 254 explained

**Principle:** On Concurrent subjects, Parliamentary law prevails in case of repugnancy

## 📚 MODULE 4: LEGISLATURE

### 5. Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)

**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Article**: Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection)  

**Facts:**
- Kihoto Hollohan, Nagaland MLA disqualified by Speaker
- Challenged validity of Tenth Schedule
- Questioned Speaker's power to decide

**Issues:**
1. Does Tenth Schedule violate freedom of speech?
2. Does it violate right to form associations?
3. Is Speaker's decision final and beyond judicial review?

**Held:**

**1. Tenth Schedule is Valid:**
- Doesn't violate basic structure
- Freedom of speech in Parliament not absolute
- Anti-defection law reasonable restriction

**2. Doesn't Violate Article 19(1)(c):**
- Right to form association doesn't include right to defect
- Regulates consequences, not formation

**3. Speaker's Decision Subject to Judicial Review:**
- Not completely final
- Courts can review on grounds of:
  - Malafide (bad faith)
  - Perversity (unreasonable)
  - Violation of constitutional mandate
- Limited review - not on merits

**Significance:** Upheld anti-defection law while ensuring some judicial oversight

**Exam Relevance:** Must mention for anti-defection questions

---

### 6. Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007)

**Article**: Parliamentary privileges (Article 105)  

**Issue:**
- MPs caught taking bribes on camera (cash-for-questions scam)
- Expelled by Lok Sabha
- Challenged expulsion - can courts interfere?

**Held:**
- Parliamentary privileges exist but not absolute
- Privilege doesn't protect criminal acts like bribery
- Courts can review if constitutional violation
- Expulsion for bribery upheld

**Principle:** Parliamentary privilege cannot shield criminal conduct

## 📚 MODULE 5: JUDICIARY

### 7. First Judges Case - S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)

**Issue:** Meaning of "consultation" in judicial appointments

**Held:**
- "Consultation" doesn't mean "concurrence"
- Government has final say in appointments
- CJI's opinion merely advice

**Impact:** Government dominated judicial appointments

**Later:** Overruled by Second Judges Case (1993)

---

### 8. Second Judges Case - Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. UOI (1993)

**Bench**: 9 judges  
**Article**: 124 (Appointment of judges)  

**Held:**
- **Overruled First Judges Case**
- "Consultation" means "concurrence"
- **CJI's opinion has primacy**
- **Collegium system** emerged: CJI + 2 senior-most judges

**Impact:** Shifted power from Executive to Judiciary in appointments

---

### 9. Third Judges Case - Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (1998)

**Issue:** Clarifying collegium system

**Held:**
- Collegium **expanded** from 3 to 5 for Supreme Court
- **CJI + 4 senior-most judges** form collegium
- Plurality of judges needed (not CJI alone)

**Current System:** Based on this case

---

### 10. Fourth Judges Case - NJAC Case (2015)

**Full Name:** Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India  
**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Articles**: 124A (NJAC), 124 (Appointment)  

**Facts:**
- 99th Amendment (2014) created NJAC
- NJAC composition: CJI + 2 SC judges + Law Minister + 2 eminent persons
- Replaced collegium system
- Challenged as violating judicial independence

**Held:**
- 99th Amendment creating NJAC is **unconstitutional**
- **Struck down** by 4:1 majority
- Violates **independence of judiciary** (basic structure)
- Executive's presence affects independence
- **Collegium system restored**

**Reasoning:**
- Law Minister's presence gives Executive control
- Veto power of any 2 members problematic
- Independence of judiciary is basic feature
- Cannot be compromised by amendment

**Dissent:** Justice Chelameswar dissented, favoring NJAC

**Impact:** Collegium continues despite criticism

**Exam Relevance:** Important for judicial appointments questions

---

### 11. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

**Article**: 323A, 323B (Tribunals)  

**Issue:**
Can Tribunals completely exclude High Courts' jurisdiction under Article 226?

**Held:**
- **NO** - Tribunals cannot exclude HC jurisdiction completely
- Judicial review by High Courts cannot be taken away
- Article 226 is basic structure
- All Tribunal orders subject to HC review under Article 226

**Principle:** Judicial review is inviolable

---

## 📚 MODULE 6: EXECUTIVE

### 12. Samsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

**Article**: 74 (Aid and Advice)  

**Issue:**
Does President/Governor have independent powers?

**Held:**
- President/Governor have **no independent powers**
- Must act on aid and advice of Council of Ministers
- Cannot act suo motu
- All executive actions on ministerial advice

**Principle:** President/Governor are nominal executives

---

### 13. Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh (2016)

**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Article**: Governor's powers, Article 356  

**Facts:**
- Governor of Arunachal Pradesh advanced Assembly session
- Led to removal of Speaker
- Constitutional crisis

**Held:**
- Governor cannot act arbitrarily
- Governor must follow constitutional norms
- Cannot aid horse-trading
- Cannot dissolve Assembly to help one party
- Discretionary powers have limits

**Principle:** Governor cannot act partisan; must maintain constitutional propriety

---

## 📚 MODULE 8: EMERGENCY

### 14. ADM Jabalpur v. Shivakant Shukla (1976)

**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Article**: Emergency, Fundamental Rights  

**Facts:**
- During 1975-77 Emergency
- Presidential order suspended right to move courts (Article 359)
- Thousands detained under MISA
- Challenged detention

**Issue:**
During emergency, when Article 21 enforcement suspended, can courts intervene?

**Held (4:1 majority):**
- During emergency, even **right to life suspended**
- No remedy available if Presidential order issued
- Government can detain anyone without reasons
- **Most criticized judgment** in Indian legal history

**Dissent:**
- Justice **H.R. Khanna** alone dissented
- Said right to life cannot be suspended
- Constitution not suspended during emergency
- Was superseded for his dissent (punished)

**Impact:**
- Led to **44th Amendment**
- Articles 20 and 21 now cannot be suspended
- Judicial failure during emergency

**Exam Relevance:** MUST mention for 1975 Emergency questions

---

### 15. Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980)

**Bench**: 5 judges  
**Articles**: Emergency powers, 42nd Amendment  

**Issue:**
Validity of 42nd Amendment provisions giving unlimited power to Parliament

**Held:**
- Struck down clauses giving Parliament unlimited amendment power
- Emergency powers don't give unlimited authority
- **Judicial review cannot be taken away** (basic structure)
- Balance between rights and state power necessary

**Significance:** Reaffirmed limits on emergency and amendment powers
