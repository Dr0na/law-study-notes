## 1. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

### 📋 Case Details

**Citation**: AIR 1973 SC 1461  
**Bench**: 13 judges (largest Constitutional Bench ever)  
**Topic**: Basic Structure Doctrine  
**Importance**: 🔥🔥🔥🔥🔥 (Most important constitutional case)  

### Facts

**Background:**
- Kesavananda Bharati was head of Edneer Mutt in Kerala
- Challenge to Kerala Land Reforms Amendment Act
- Mutt's properties being acquired by State
- Also challenged Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights

**Previous History:**
- **Shankari Prasad (1951)**: Parliament can amend FRs
- **Sajjan Singh (1965)**: Reaffirmed Parliament's power
- **Golaknath (1967)**: Parliament cannot amend FRs
- This case sought to clarify once and for all

### Issues Before Supreme Court

**3 Main Issues:**
1. Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?
2. Is there any limit to Parliament's amendment power?
3. What is the extent of Parliament's constituent power under Article 368?

### Supreme Court's Decision

**Majority View (7:6 decision):**

#### Held 1: Parliament Can Amend Constitution ✅
- Parliament has power to amend any part of Constitution
- Including Fundamental Rights
- Article 368 gives this power
- Overruled Golaknath case partially

#### Held 2: BUT Cannot Destroy Basic Structure ⚖️
**Revolutionary Ruling:**
- Parliament cannot alter or destroy **Basic Structure** of Constitution
- Even with 2/3rd majority
- Some features so fundamental they cannot be amended
- Balance between Parliamentary supremacy and Constitutional supremacy

#### Held 3: Basic Structure Includes 📋

**Features That Cannot Be Destroyed:**
1. Supremacy of Constitution
2. Republican and Democratic form of government
3. Secular character
4. Separation of powers
5. Federal character of Constitution
6. Judicial review
7. Parliamentary system
8. Rule of law
9. Harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

**Open List:** Not exhaustive, courts can add more features

### Significance

**Impact on Indian Democracy:**
✅ Protected Constitution from totalitarian amendments  
✅ Ensured democracy cannot be destroyed by majority  
✅ Balanced Parliamentary power with Constitutional limits  
✅ Basic features permanently protected  
✅ Federalism cannot be destroyed by amendment  

**Practical Effect:**
- Parliament can amend BUT within limits
- Judiciary decides what's "basic structure"
- Has prevented several amendments since then

### Relevance for Federalism

**Why Important for Module 1:**
- Established that **federalism is part of basic structure**
- Union cannot amend Constitution to abolish States
- Federal character permanently protected
- Division of powers cannot be completely destroyed

### Exam Usage

**When to Cite:**
- Any question on federalism
- Basic Structure doctrine questions
- Amendment power questions
- Balance of power questions

**Quote:** "Parliament can amend but cannot destroy basic structure - Kesavananda Bharati (1973)"

## 2. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

### 📋 Case Details

**Citation**: AIR 1994 SC 1918  
**Bench**: 9 judges  
**Topic**: Article 356, Federalism as Basic Structure  
**Importance**: 🔥🔥🔥🔥🔥 (Most important for Article 356)  

### Facts

**Background:**
- December 6, 1992: Babri Masjid demolished
- President's Rule imposed in 4 States
- BJP governments dismissed:
  - Madhya Pradesh
  - Rajasthan
  - Himachal Pradesh
  - Later Uttar Pradesh
- Dismissed Chief Ministers challenged proclamations

**Petitioners:**
- S.R. Bommai (Karnataka CM)
- Other dismissed CMs
- Question: Can President's Rule be challenged?

### Issues

**Main Questions:**
1. Is President's satisfaction under Article 356 subject to judicial review?
2. Can courts examine Governor's report?
3. What are limits on use of Article 356?
4. Is federalism part of basic structure?

### Supreme Court's Landmark Decision

**Held - 5 Major Rulings:**

#### Ruling 1: Judicial Review Possible ⚖️
**Breakthrough:**
- President's satisfaction under Article 356 **not immune** from judicial review
- Courts can examine if material existed for satisfaction
- Cannot be arbitrary, mala fide, or based on irrelevant considerations
- Subjective satisfaction has objective limits

#### Ruling 2: Governor's Report Not Conclusive 📋
**Clarification:**
- Governor's report is relevant but not conclusive
- President must independently apply mind
- Cannot mechanically accept Governor's recommendation
- Must examine material personally

#### Ruling 3: Floor Test Preferred 🗳️
**New Principle:**
- Before dismissing government, **floor test** should be ordered
- Let Legislative Assembly decide who has majority
- Governor cannot pre-judge majority
- Democratic way to resolve doubt
- Dismissal should be last resort

**Impact:** Floor tests became norm after this

#### Ruling 4: Federalism is Basic Structure 🏛️
**Constitutional Principle:**
- Federal structure is part of basic structure
- Arbitrary use of Article 356 violates federalism
- States' autonomy must be respected
- Cannot be used for political purposes
- Strengthened from Kesavananda Bharati doctrine

#### Ruling 5: Courts Can Restore Assembly 🔄
**Remedial Power:**
- If Article 356 proclamation unconstitutional
- Courts can restore dissolved/suspended Assembly
- Can undo damage caused by misuse
- Preventive and curative jurisdiction

### Guidelines Laid Down

**7 Bommai Guidelines (COUNT = 7):**

1️⃣ Use Article 356 **sparingly** - as last resort  
2️⃣ Give **warning** to State Government first  
3️⃣ **Floor test** preferred over dismissal  
4️⃣ Cannot be used for **political purposes**  
5️⃣ **Material** must exist for President's satisfaction  
6️⃣ President must **independently apply mind**  
7️⃣ Subject to **judicial review**  

🧠 **Memory**: **"SWFPMPI"** = Sparingly, Warning, Floor, Political-no, Material, President-mind, Judicial review

### Significance

**Impact on Article 356 Use:**
✅ **Drastically reduced** misuse (80%+ reduction after 1994)  
✅ **Floor tests** became standard practice  
✅ **Governors** more careful in reports  
✅ **Federal structure** strengthened  
✅ **States' autonomy** better protected  

**Statistics:**
- Before 1994: 100+ times used (many political)
- After 1994: Much less frequent, more justified

### Relevance for Federalism

**Why Important:**
- Reaffirmed federalism as basic structure
- Protected States from arbitrary dismissal
- Strengthened federal balance
- Made Centre more careful about State autonomy

### Exam Usage

**When to Cite:**
- Article 356 questions (mandatory)
- Federalism questions
- Centre-State relations
- Misuse of emergency provisions

**Quote:** "Federalism is part of basic structure; Article 356 cannot be misused - S.R. Bommai (1994)"

## 3. State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977)

### 📋 Case Details

**Citation**: AIR 1977 SC 1361  
**Bench**: 7 judges  
**Topic**: Emergency provisions, Federalism  

### Facts

**Context:**
- 1975-77 Emergency period
- After Emergency lifted (March 1977)
- Elections held, Janata Party won
- Congress dismissed in several States using Article 356
- Rajasthan government challenged dissolution

### Issue

Can proclamation of emergency under Article 352 be challenged in court?

### Held

**Decision:**
- Emergency proclamation **subject to judicial review**
- Courts can examine if grounds existed
- Presidential satisfaction not absolute
- Must be based on relevant material
- Federal structure is basic feature

**Principle:** Emergency provisions don't give unlimited power; must conform to Constitutional requirements

### Significance

- Opened door for judicial review of emergencies
- Established that federal structure is basic
- Led to stricter interpretation of emergency powers

### Exam Usage

Cite for: Emergency provisions, Federalism as basic structure

## 4. State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)

### 📋 Case Details

**Citation**: AIR 1963 SC 1241  
**Topic**: Legislative powers, Ancillary powers doctrine  

### Facts

- Union levied cess on coal
- West Bengal challenged: Coal mining on land, land is State subject
- Whether Union cess encroaches on State's power over land?

### Issue

Does Parliament's power over "regulation of mines and minerals" include power to levy cess on coal?

### Held

**Decision:**
- Parliament's power over mines includes **ancillary powers**
- Power to levy cess is incidental to regulation
- Entry in Union List should be interpreted broadly
- Ancillary/incidental powers included

**Principle:** Power to legislate on a subject includes power on incidental/ancillary matters connected with that subject

### Significance

**Ancillary Powers Doctrine:**
- Broad interpretation of List entries
- Entries not to be read narrowly
- Necessary incidental powers included

### Exam Usage

Cite for: Interpretation of Lists, Expansion of powers
