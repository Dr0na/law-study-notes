## Part A — Fifteen marks (5 questions)

### A1. Essential characteristics of Indian federalism; distinction from American federalism; link with basic structure

**Pattern:** Conceptual + comparative + doctrinal  
**Time:** 20 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Cover:** Meaning of federalism; India as “Union of States” / quasi-federal; six to seven structural features; concise India–USA contrast; how federalism relates to basic structure (judicial review, democracy).
- **Articles:** 1, 246 read with Seventh Schedule, 248, 368; Seventh Schedule (three lists).
- **Cases:** *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973); *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) (federalism as part of basic structure).
- **Layout:** Introduction → features → comparison → basic structure → conclusion.

#### Introduction

Federalism divides public power between a union and states so that each level has a defined sphere, yet both derive authority from the same Constitution. India is often described as **quasi-federal**: the structure is federal, but the text and practice tilt toward a strong centre. Article 1 describes India as a **“Union of States”**, not a “federation of states,” which signals asymmetry compared with classical federations such as the United States.

#### Body

**1. Written Constitution and rigid amendment**  
India’s Constitution is a single written charter that allocates powers and can be amended only by the procedure in Article 368. That gives a legal backbone to centre–state relations.

**2. Division of powers (legislative)**  
Article 246 and the **Seventh Schedule** distribute subjects across Union, State, and Concurrent Lists, with **residuary power** vested in Parliament (Article 248). This is a strong central marker compared with many federations.

**3. Single citizenship and integrated judiciary**  
Unlike the USA, India provides **single citizenship** (Part II) and a **hierarchically integrated judiciary** with the Supreme Court at the apex, enabling uniform interpretation of the Constitution.

**4. Dual polity with unequal weight**  
There is a **dual executive and legislature**, but during emergencies and under specific provisions (for example, President’s Rule under Article 356) state autonomy shrinks, which is less typical of “classic” federalism.

**5. Financial integration**  
Taxing and borrowing powers, Finance Commission (Article 280), and GST Council architecture show **fiscal interdependence** and central coordination.

**India vs United States (examiner-friendly contrast)**  
In broad terms: the USA reserves **residuary powers to the states** (Tenth Amendment); India reserves them to **Parliament**. The USA has **dual citizenship** (state and national) in a practical sense and a **dual court system** for federal and state matters; India stresses **single citizenship** and one integrated judicial hierarchy. The USA Senate represents **states as units**; India’s Rajya Sabha reflects **regional representation** but is not a “states’ house” in the same structural sense.

#### Basic structure angle

In *Kesavananda Bharati*, the Supreme Court held that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 is wide but cannot destroy the **basic structure** of the Constitution. In *S.R. Bommai*, a nine-judge bench held that **secularism** and **federalism** are part of the basic structure, so misuse of Article 356 to topple elected state governments is subject to **judicial review**.

#### Conclusion

Indian federalism is a **working compromise** between autonomy and integration. Examiners reward a clear **feature list**, a **tight USA contrast**, and a **basic-structure paragraph** tying federalism to judicial review and democracy.

### A2. Scheme of distribution of legislative powers — territorial extent, three lists, residuary power, and expansion of Union power

**Pattern:** Conceptual + problem-oriented (which legislature can legislate?)  
**Time:** 20 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Cover:** Articles 245–246; territorial limits; three lists; Article 248; Articles 249–253 as “expansions”; brief note on repugnancy (Article 254).
- **Cases:** *State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (pith and substance — if asked in oral follow-up); *T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (federalism in education — optional one-liner).
- **Layout:** Territorial → subject distribution → residuary → expansion provisions → short conclusion.

#### Introduction

Legislative federalism answers **who can make law on which topic and for which territory**. The Indian scheme is codified mainly in **Articles 245–254** and the **Seventh Schedule**.

#### Body

**1. Territorial jurisdiction (Article 245)**  
Parliament may legislate for the **whole or any part** of India. A state legislature may legislate for the **whole or part of the state**. Parliament retains extra-territorial legislative competence for India’s interests abroad where the Constitution assigns such power.

**2. Subject-matter distribution (Article 246)**  
- **Union List:** exclusive Union competence.  
- **State List:** exclusive state competence.  
- **Concurrent List:** both can legislate; **Union law prevails** in case of conflict except where state law reserved for President’s consideration (Article 254 framework — state law may prevail if assent pattern is satisfied as per constitutional text).

**3. Residuary power (Article 248)**  
Parliament has **exclusive** power to legislate on matters **not enumerated** in any list, including power to tax residuary subjects. This contrasts with the USA and strengthens the centre.

**4. Expansion / exceptional Union legislation**  
Briefly explain purpose and threshold:  
- **Article 249:** Rajya Sabha resolution + national interest — Parliament legislates on State List for a limited period.  
- **Article 250:** during national emergency — Parliament can legislate on State List.  
- **Article 252:** two or more states request — Parliament may legislate for those states.  
- **Article 253:** legislation to implement **international treaties** even if matter falls in State List.

#### Conclusion

The scheme is **rule plus exception**: ordinary distribution through lists, **Parliament-centric residuary power**, and **explicit gateways** (249–253) where Union legislation temporarily or specially enters state domains. Mentioning **Article 248** and **one example** from 249–253 is often decisive for full marks.

### A3. Tenth Schedule — Anti-defection law: grounds of disqualification, role of Speaker/Chairman, and constitutional significance

**Pattern:** Doctrinal + case-law  
**Time:** 20 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Cover:** 52nd Amendment; Tenth Schedule structure; disqualification grounds; time limit *Kihoto Hollohan*; 91st Amendment (split removed); significance for stable government vs fairness.
- **Cases:** *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992) — interim Speaker discretion, later constitutional amendment on time bar; *Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya* (2007) — voluntary giving up; *Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker* (2020) — timelines for disqualification petitions.
- **Articles:** 102(2), 191(2) read with Tenth Schedule.

#### Introduction

The **Tenth Schedule** was inserted by the **52nd Constitutional Amendment, 1985**, to curb **political defection** that destabilized legislatures. It lays down **when a legislator is disqualified** for leaving a political party or acting against its whip, and who decides disputes initially.

#### Body

**1. Whom it applies to**  
Members of **Parliament** and **state legislatures** are covered. Different paragraphs deal with disqualification on **defection** and exceptions for **mergers** (narrow after amendments).

**2. Grounds of disqualification (structure, not rote listing only)**  
If a member **voluntarily gives up membership** of the party on whose ticket they were elected; or if they **vote or abstain** contrary to any **direction** (whip) of that party without prior permission (and permission denied is treated as direction). **Independent** members are disqualified if they join a party.

**3. Merger exception (post-91st Amendment context)**  
Explain in principle: **two-thirds** “legislator group” merger with another party can avoid disqualification in the merger pathway; “split” as an exception was **removed** by the **91st Amendment** to prevent misuse.

**4. Authority of Speaker/Chairman**  
The Speaker (Lok Sabha) or Chairman (Rajya Sabha), and the presiding officer in state legislatures, decides disqualification petitions. The law attempts balance between **speed** and **fairness**; Supreme Court jurisprudence has stressed **timely** disposal and **judicial review** where constitutional requirements are breached.

**5. Significance**  
Reduces **aya–ram gaya–ram** politics, promotes **government stability**, but raises concerns about **intra-party democracy** and **Speaker neutrality** when the Speaker belongs to the ruling party.

#### Conclusion

A strong answer links **text (Tenth Schedule)**, **two disqualification ideas** (voluntary resignation of party membership; whip violation), **merger math after 91st Amendment**, and **at least two case names** showing judicial fine-tuning.

### A4. Emergency provisions — National, State (356), and Financial; constitutional safeguards and judicial control

**Pattern:** Conceptual + case-law  
**Time:** 20 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Cover:** Articles 352, 356, 360; 44th Amendment changes for Article 352; effects; misuse narrative; *Bommai* for 356; judicial review.
- **Cases:** *S.R. Bommai* (1994); *Minerva Mills* (1980) (emergency context / basic structure — short); *Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006) (Bihar dissolution — optional).
- **Layout:** Three emergencies → effects → safeguards → cases → conclusion.

#### Introduction

Emergency provisions are **exceptions** to normal federalism. They allow the Union to assume extraordinary powers to tackle **war/external aggression/revolt** (national), **breakdown of constitutional machinery in states** (356), or **threat to financial stability** (360).

#### Body

**1. National Emergency (Article 352)**  
After the **44th Amendment**, “internal disturbance” was replaced by **“armed rebellion”** for internal emergency, and **written advice** of the Council of Ministers (not the Prime Minister alone) is required for proclamation. Effects include **Article 353** (Union executive direction to states) and **Articles 358–359** on temporary suspension of enforcement of fundamental rights **as limited and structured by the amended text** (set out the exact clauses as in your module; do not treat suspension as unlimited).

**2. State Emergency / President’s Rule (Article 356)**  
Ground: President satisfied that state government **cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution**. Parliament’s approval, duration, and extension rules should be stated briefly. Effects: state executive to President/Governor; Assembly may be dissolved or kept in suspended animation; Parliament may legislate for the state.

**3. Financial Emergency (Article 360)**  
Rare in practice; direction to states on financial propriety; potential reduction of salaries. Mention **never used** in India (as general knowledge) if accurate to your course material.

**4. Judicial review**  
*Bommai* held **proclamation under Article 356** is subject to **judicial review** on **mala fides**, **irrelevant considerations**, and **lack of material**; **secularism** and **federalism** are basic structure; **floor test** preferred over Governor’s subjective report.

#### Conclusion

Emergencies are **constitutional safety valves** but historically sensitive; modern answers emphasize **amendment-based safeguards (44th)** and **Bommai-type judicial review** for Article 356.

### A5. Independence of judiciary and judicial activism; writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (Article 32) in outline

**Pattern:** Conceptual + rights-enforcement  
**Time:** 20 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Cover:** Why independence matters; appointments/collegium briefly; security of tenure/salaries; **Article 32** as “soul” of Part III; five writs in outline; PIL idea.
- **Cases:** *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (1993) Second Judges case — collegium; *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997) — power of judicial review; *Hussainara Khatoon* / *Olga Tellis* as PIL markers (any one).
- **Articles:** 124–147 (structure), **32**, 226 contrast one line.

#### Introduction

An independent judiciary secures **rule of law**, **rights**, and **federal balance**. In India, independence is woven into **appointment security, financial security, contempt power**, and the **power of judicial review**.

#### Body

**1. Structural guarantees**  
Security of tenure (removal only by rigorous process), salaries charged on Consolidated Fund, contempt jurisdiction, separation from executive control in day-to-day adjudication.

**2. Appointments debate (exam-short version)**  
Collegium system evolved through **Second** and **Third Judges** cases; NJAC **struck down** in *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (Fourth Judges case, 2015)* as affecting independence; mention **Memorandum of Procedure** as ongoing implementation reality without over-writing the whole controversy.

**3. Judicial activism / PIL**  
Courts expanded **locus standi** for public interest litigation to reach **marginalized groups** and **continuing wrongs** (under-trials, bonded labour, pavement dwellers). Critics warn of **judicial overreach** into policy.

**4. Article 32**  
Supreme Court’s power to issue **directions, orders, or writs** for enforcement of fundamental rights. Dr. Ambedkar described it as the **heart and soul** of the Constitution. Name **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto** with one-line role each if space permits.

#### Conclusion

Link **independence → impartial review → rights under Article 32**; one sentence contrasting **Article 226** (High Court wider remedial reach including non-FR in many situations) shows depth without a second essay.

## Part B — Ten marks (5 questions)

### B1. Discuss the powers and functions of the Election Commission of India. How does it ensure free and fair elections?

**Pattern:** Institutional + conceptual  
**Time:** 12–14 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Body:** Constitutional status (Article 324); **superintendence, direction, and control** of elections; powers: delimitation assistance, registration of parties, model code, disqualifications reference; multi-member Commission; independence concerns (appointment not detailed in Constitution — mention as reform debate briefly).
- **Case:** *T.N. Seshan v. Union of India* (1995) — powers of Chief Election Commissioner sphere (high level).
- **Conclusion:** ECI as **guardian of electoral democracy**.

#### Model answer (compact)

The **Election Commission of India (ECI)** is a constitutional body under **Article 324**, vested with **superintendence, direction, and control** of elections to Parliament, state legislatures, and offices of President and Vice-President.

**Powers and functions (clustered):**  
(1) **Administrative:** preparing electoral rolls, scheduling elections, enforcing **Model Code of Conduct**.  
(2) **Regulatory:** registration and recognition of political parties; allotment of symbols.  
(3) **Quasi-judicial:** adjudicating disputes on splits/mergers of parties where rules apply; recommending **disqualification** for corrupt practices under Representation of the People Act (framework level).  
(4) **Advisory:** opinion on disqualification questions under **Article 103/192** route in principle.

**Free and fair elections:** independence in day-to-day enforcement; deployment of observers; criminalization and expenditure monitoring mechanisms (name themes, not every rule); **technology** (EVM/VVPAT debate as “integrity measure” in one line if taught).

**Conclusion:** ECI converts **constitutional promise of democracy** into **operational fairness**, subject to legislative framework and judicial review where fundamental rights are involved.

### B2. President of India — executive powers, relationship with Council of Ministers, and discretionary powers (if any)

**Pattern:** Doctrinal  
**Time:** 12–14 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Articles:** 52–62; **74(1)** aid and advice; **75(3)** collective responsibility; **78** communication; **123** ordinance.
- **Cases:** *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974) — President bound by advice except narrow exceptions; *R. Venkataraman* discussion avoided; **Bommai**-type references not needed.
- **Discretion:** **narrow** — formal discretion (appoint PM, dissolve on advice); **residual** situations sometimes debated but exam answer: **“constitutional discretion is extremely limited.”**

#### Model answer (compact)

The President is the **formal head of the Union executive** (Article 52) but real executive power is exercised by the **Council of Ministers** headed by the Prime Minister (Article 74 read with 75).

**Aid and advice (Article 74(1)):** After the **42nd** and **44th** Amendments, **aid and advice is binding**; President must act on it except where **personal/pocket** discretion is textually contemplated (exam answers usually list: **appointment of PM** when no clear majority; **dissolution** when advised). *Shamsher Singh* confirms **President and Governor are constitutional mirrors** of nominal power.

**Powers to mention:** summoning Parliament, assent to bills (return once per Lok Sabha life for reconsideration under Article 111 framework), **ordinance** under Article 123, **references to Supreme Court** under Article 143 (advisory), appointment powers on **aid and advice**.

**Conclusion:** India follows a **parliamentary cabinet model**; the President’s strength is **moral symbolic leadership**, not day-to-day governance.

### B3. Governor — powers, role of discretionary powers, and relation with state ministry

**Pattern:** Comparative + institutional  
**Time:** 12–14 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Articles:** 153–167; **163** aid and advice; **200** reservation of bills; **356** report.
- **Cases:** *Shamsher Singh*; *Nabam Rebia* (2016) — Arunachal Pradesh assembly/President’s Rule context — use only if accurate to your notes: discretionary use of Governor challenged); *Rameshwar Prasad* (Bihar).

#### Model answer (compact)

The Governor is the **constitutional head of state executive** (Article 153) appointed by President (Article 155) and holds office during pleasure of President (Article 156) — a **federal tension** because Governor is not elected.

**Normal powers:** analogous to President at state level; acts on **aid and advice** of Council of Ministers (Article 163). **Chief Minister** must be appointed under Article 164; **Council of Ministers** collectively responsible to Legislative Assembly (Article 164(2)).

**Discretionary flashpoints:** **reservation of Bills for President’s consideration** (Article 200); **recommendation of President’s Rule** (Article 356 pathway); **choice of CM** in hung verdict scenarios (textbook “discretion”). Examiners like a **critical line**: misuse risks converting Governor into **agent of Union**, harming federal balance.

**Conclusion:** Governor’s role should be **impartial constitutional lubricant**, not partisan interference; judicial review and conventions (Bommai spirit) discipline extremes.

### B4. Money Bill — Article 110; certification by Speaker; why controversy matters

**Pattern:** Doctrinal + procedural  
**Time:** 12–14 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Article 110** definition; only Lok Sabha decisive; Rajya Sabha **14-day** recommendatory power; **Speaker’s certificate is final** under Article 110(3) — mention judicial touchstone cautiously: *Aadhaar* (Justice Chandrachud dissent) / majority on money bill — keep neutral: “judicial scrutiny debate.”
- **Contrast** financial bill under Article 117 briefly.

#### Model answer (compact)

A **Money Bill** is defined exclusively under **Article 110** and contains only matters listed there (taxation, borrowing, custody/consolidated funds, appropriation out of CFI, declaring expenditure charged, receipt into CFI or public account, subordinate matters). **Article 109** sets the special procedure: after Lok Sabha passes a Money Bill, it is transmitted to Rajya Sabha, which may **recommend amendments within 14 days**; Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations.

**Speaker’s role:** **Article 110(3)** makes Speaker’s decision **final** whether a bill is a Money Bill — procedural finality to prevent deadlock.

**Why asked:** If non-money matters are **smuggled** into a Money Bill, the Rajya Sabha’s federal checking role is bypassed. Students should note **academic debate** on **judicial review** of certificate versus textual finality.

**Conclusion:** Money Bill procedure balances **efficiency of finance legislation** with risk of **majoritarian shortcuts**; understanding **Article 110 list** is central.

### B5. National Commission for SCs/STs/BCs — constitutional mandate and functions (outline as one institution family)

**Pattern:** Conceptual  
**Time:** 12–14 minutes  

#### Answer synopsis

- **Articles:** 338 (NCSC), 338A (NCST), 338B (NCBC) — post-102nd Amendment architecture for NCBC with caveats on **identification of SEBC** by states (mention **102nd** briefly).
- **Functions:** investigative, recommendatory, reporting under Article **338(5)**-style duties; safeguards monitoring.

#### Model answer (compact)

The Constitution institutionalizes safeguards for **SCs, STs**, and (for backward classes) **NCBC** through dedicated commissions to monitor **constitutional protections** and **developmental outcomes**.

**NCSC / NCST:** investigate safeguards, inquire into complaints, advise on policy, **annual report** to President to be laid in Parliament — strengthens **accountability** without displacing legislature.

**NCBC (Article 338B):** inserted by **102nd Amendment**; examines welfare measures for socially and educationally backward classes; **important nuance:** after constitutional amendments and judicial decisions, **states’ power to identify SEBCs** for reservations was restored legislatively (students should cite the exact statutory layer taught in class if required).

**Conclusion:** These bodies are **watchdogs**, mainly **recommendatory**, but politically significant for **inclusive governance**.

## Part C — Five marks (5 questions)

### C1. What is Article 280? Explain the role of the Finance Commission.

**Model answer (about 200 words)**  
**Article 280** requires the President to constitute a **Finance Commission (FC)** every fifth year (or earlier) to recommend **Union–state fiscal distribution**: tax devolution, grants-in-aid, revenue augmentation measures, and any matter referred. The FC is an **independent recommendatory body** whose report is not binding but Parliament implements through **Orders** based on recommendations. It strengthens **cooperative fiscal federalism** by balancing richer and poorer states. Mention **15th Finance Commission** only as an example of periodicity, not as memorized numbers, unless your paper expects it.

---

### C2. Explain “aid and advice” of the Council of Ministers to the President (Article 74).

**Model answer**  
**Article 74(1)** mandates that **there shall be a Council of Ministers** with the Prime Minister at the head to **aid and advise** the President, who **shall act** in accordance with such advice. After amendments, advice is **binding**, converting the President into a **constitutional head** consistent with parliamentary government. **Article 74(2)** bars inquiry into whether advice was given (protects confidentiality). One-line contrast: **Article 163** is the state analogue for Governor.

---

### C3. Distinguish between Ordinary Bill and Money Bill in two or three grounds.

**Model answer**  
(1) **Definition:** Money Bill is confined to **Article 110** matters; ordinary bills can cover any legislative subject within competence.  
(2) **Procedure:** Money Bill **need not** go through same Rajya Sabha veto structure; RS has **14-day** recommendatory window under Article 111 read with Article 110.  
(3) **Certificate:** Speaker’s **Money Bill certificate** under **Article 110(3)**; ordinary bills have no such device.  
(Optional fourth) **Origination:** Money Bill **only** from Lok Sabha per Article 117 framework you were taught.

---

### C4. What is the Inter-State Council under Article 263?

**Model answer**  
**Article 263** empowers the President to establish an **Inter-State Council** if it appears that **public interests** would be served by an institutional mechanism to inquire into **disputes between states**, investigate subjects in which some or all states or Union and one or more states have a **common interest**, or make recommendations on any such subject. It is a **coordination and dispute-prevention** body supporting **cooperative federalism**; it does not replace **Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction** under Article 131 for legal disputes.

---

### C5. Write a short note on the **73rd Constitutional Amendment** and Panchayati Raj.

**Model answer**  
The **73rd Amendment (1992)** added **Part IX** and **Eleventh Schedule**, constitutionally recognizing **rural local self-government**. It provides for **three-tier Panchayati Raj** (village, intermediate, district), **direct elections**, **State Election Commission**, **five-year tenure**, and **mandatory devolution** by state legislatures of powers and responsibilities upon recommendation of **State Finance Commission**. Significance: transforms local bodies from **“agency of state”** to **“institutions of self-government”** (Article 243G theme) within state legislative scheme.
