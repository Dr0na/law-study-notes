### Introduction (1 mark)

**Distribution Scheme:**
The Indian Constitution distributes legislative powers between the Union and States through the **Seventh Schedule**, which contains three Lists - Union List, State List, and Concurrent List. This distribution is essential for the federal structure of India and ensures clarity about which government can legislate on which subjects. The scheme is laid down in **Article 246** read with the Seventh Schedule.

**Purpose of Distribution:**
- Prevents overlapping of powers
- Avoids conflicts between Union and States
- Ensures efficient governance
- Maintains federal balance

---

### ⚖️ The Scheme of Distribution (Article 246) (2-3 marks)

### Article 246 - 3 Clauses (COUNT = 3)

#### Clause 1: Union List (List I) 🏛️
**Article 246(1)**: Parliament has **exclusive power** to make laws on subjects enumerated in Union List.

- State Legislatures **cannot** make laws on these subjects
- Parliament's domain exclusively

#### Clause 2: Concurrent List (List III) ⚖️
**Article 246(2)**: **Both Parliament and State Legislatures** can make laws on subjects in Concurrent List.

- Shared legislative power
- Both can legislate simultaneously
- Article 254 resolves conflicts (Union law prevails)

#### Clause 3: State List (List II) 🏢
**Article 246(3)**: State Legislatures have **exclusive power** to make laws on subjects in State List.

- Parliament **cannot ordinarily** make laws on these subjects
- State's exclusive domain
- Exceptions exist (Articles 249, 250, 252, 253, 356)

### Article 254 - Conflict Resolution Rule

**If both Union and State make laws on Concurrent subject:**

**General Rule:**
- **Union law prevails**
- State law void to extent of repugnancy

**Exception:**
- If President has given assent to State law
- State law prevails in that State
- Union law prevails in other States

### 📌 Key Case: Deep Chand v. State of UP (1959)
**Principle**: On Concurrent subjects, Parliamentary law prevails over State law in case of conflict.

---

### 📚 Residuary Powers (Article 248) (1-2 marks)

**What are Residuary Powers:**
Subjects not mentioned in any of the three Lists fall under residuary category. Power to legislate on such subjects vests exclusively with **Parliament**.

**Examples of Residuary Subjects:**
- Computer and Internet laws (when technology emerged)
- Cyber crimes (before specific legislation)
- New technologies not contemplated in 1950
- Space law and satellite communications

**Comparison with USA:**
- **India**: Residuary with Union (Article 248)
- **USA**: Residuary with States
- Shows India's centralized bias

### Conclusion (1 mark)

The Seventh Schedule's three-List system provides a clear framework for distribution of legislative powers, preventing conflicts and ensuring efficient governance. With **210 subjects total** (97+66+47), the scheme covers all aspects of governance. The **Concurrent List** allows flexibility through cooperative federalism, while **residuary powers** with Union ensure no legislative vacuum. This distribution reflects India's federal structure with unitary bias, balancing national unity with regional diversity.
