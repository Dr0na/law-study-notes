# Module 1: Nature of Indian Federalism

## Introduction

Federalism is a system of government where power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units (states). India's Constitution establishes a federal structure, but with unique features that make it different from other federal systems like the United States.

---

## 1. Essential Characteristics of Indian Federalism

India's federal system has several defining features:

### A. Written Constitution
- India has a detailed written Constitution that clearly defines the powers and functions of both the Union and State governments
- This provides legal certainty and stability to the federal structure

### B. Division of Powers
- Powers are divided between the Union (Central Government) and the States through three lists:
  - **Union List** (List I): 97 subjects exclusively for the Union (e.g., defense, foreign affairs, railways)
  - **State List** (List II): 66 subjects exclusively for States (e.g., police, public health, agriculture)
  - **Concurrent List** (List III): 47 subjects where both can legislate (e.g., criminal law, marriage, forests)
- **Residuary Powers**: Any subject not mentioned in any list goes to the Union Parliament (Article 248)

### C. Supremacy of the Constitution
- The Constitution is the supreme law of the land
- Neither the Union nor the States can violate it
- Any law inconsistent with the Constitution is void (Article 13)

### D. Independent Judiciary
- The Supreme Court acts as the guardian of the Constitution
- It has the power of judicial review to check if laws violate the Constitution
- It settles disputes between the Union and States or between different States

### E. Bicameral Legislature at the Centre
- Parliament consists of two Houses:
  - **Lok Sabha** (House of the People): Represents the people directly
  - **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States): Represents the States
- Rajya Sabha ensures states have a voice in national legislation

### F. Dual Government and Dual Citizenship
- **Two levels of government**: Union Government and State Governments
- However, India has **single citizenship** (only Indian citizenship, not state citizenship)
- This is different from USA which has dual citizenship

### G. Rigidity and Flexibility in Amendment
- Constitution can be amended by Parliament (Article 368)
- Some provisions require:
  - Simple majority (e.g., creation of new states)
  - Special majority: 2/3rd of members present and voting + majority of total membership
  - Special majority + ratification by 50% of states (e.g., changes to federal structure)

## 2. Indian Federalism Distinguished from American Federalism

While both India and USA have federal systems, there are significant differences:

| Feature | Indian Federalism | American Federalism |
|---------|------------------|---------------------|
| **Nature** | Quasi-federal (federal in structure, unitary in spirit) | True federation |
| **How Created** | Constitution created the States (States were reorganized after independence) | States came together to form the Union |
| **Division of Powers** | Three Lists (Union, State, Concurrent) | Two Lists (Federal and State) |
| **Residuary Powers** | Vested in Union Parliament | Vested in States |
| **Citizenship** | Single citizenship (Indian only) | Dual citizenship (Federal + State) |
| **Emergency Provisions** | Strong emergency powers; can become unitary during emergency | No such comprehensive emergency provisions |
| **Amendment** | Parliament can amend with special procedures | More difficult; States have greater say |
| **Supremacy** | Constitution is supreme, but Union has more power | States have more autonomy |
| **Secession** | States cannot secede from the Union | States theoretically have more sovereignty |
| **Uniformity** | Single Constitution for entire country | Each State has its own Constitution |
| **Governor/President** | Governor appointed by President (acts as Union's agent) | Governors elected by people of State |
| **Flexibility** | Can shift between federal and unitary (emergency) | Rigid federal structure |

### Key Differences Explained:

#### 1. **Origin**
- **USA**: States existed first, then joined together to form the Union
- **India**: Union created the States through reorganization

#### 2. **Residuary Powers**
- **USA**: What is not given to the federal government stays with States
- **India**: What is not specifically given to States goes to the Union

#### 3. **Emergency Powers**
- **India**: During national emergency, India can function as a unitary state
- **USA**: No such provision for converting to unitary system

#### 4. **Centre-State Relations**
- **India**: Union has more control; can dismiss state governments, redraw state boundaries
- **USA**: States have more independence and autonomy

#### 5. **Uniformity of Laws**
- **India**: Concurrent List allows Union to override State laws
- **USA**: States have more freedom in their sphere

## 3. Federalism and Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine is a landmark constitutional principle established by the Supreme Court that protects the fundamental features of the Constitution from being amended or destroyed.

### What is Basic Structure?

- Certain essential features of the Constitution cannot be amended even by Parliament
- These features form the "basic structure" or foundation of the Constitution
- Even with a special majority, Parliament cannot change these fundamental features

### Landmark Case: **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**

**Background:**
- Question arose: Can Parliament amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights?
- Earlier in **Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)**, Supreme Court had said Fundamental Rights cannot be amended

**Supreme Court's Decision (13-judge bench):**
- Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution
- BUT Parliament cannot alter or destroy the "Basic Structure" of the Constitution
- This was a historic judgment that balanced parliamentary supremacy with constitutional supremacy

### Federalism as Part of Basic Structure

**Why Federalism is a Basic Feature:**

1. **Federal Structure Cannot Be Destroyed**
   - Parliament cannot convert India into a unitary state by amendment
   - The division of powers between Union and States must be maintained
   - States' existence and their role cannot be eliminated

2. **Balance Must Be Preserved**
   - While amendments can adjust Centre-State relations
   - The fundamental federal character must remain intact
   - Neither level should become completely subordinate to the other

3. **Important Cases:**

**S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)**
- Supreme Court held that federalism is a basic feature
- Misuse of Article 356 (President's Rule) violates federalism
- States' autonomy must be respected

**State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977)**
- Reaffirmed that federal structure is part of basic structure
- Union cannot destroy the states or make them mere administrative units

### What This Means in Practice:

✅ **Parliament CAN:**
- Adjust the distribution of powers between Union and States
- Create new states or alter boundaries (with procedures)
- Modify financial relations
- Add or remove subjects from the three Lists

❌ **Parliament CANNOT:**
- Abolish the States entirely
- Make India a unitary state (one central government only)
- Destroy the federal structure completely
- Take away all significant powers from States

### Why This Doctrine is Important:

1. **Protects Democracy**: Ensures power remains distributed
2. **Prevents Dictatorship**: No single authority can have absolute power
3. **Protects States**: Guarantees states continue to exist and have powers
4. **Ensures Debate**: Major changes need broader consensus, not just parliamentary majority
5. **Constitutional Morality**: Upholds the spirit and intent of the Constitution

## Key Constitutional Provisions

### Articles Related to Federalism:

- **Article 1**: India as a Union of States (not "Federation")
- **Articles 245-255**: Distribution of Legislative Powers
- **Articles 256-263**: Administrative Relations
- **Articles 264-293**: Financial Relations
- **Article 368**: Amendment Procedure
- **Seventh Schedule**: Three Lists (Union, State, Concurrent)

---

## Important Terms to Remember

**Federation**: A system where power is divided between central and regional governments

**Quasi-Federal**: Partly federal, partly unitary (India's character)

**Unitary Features in India**: 
- Strong Centre
- Single Constitution
- Emergency provisions
- Appointment of Governors
- Parliament can change state boundaries

**Federal Features in India**:
- Written Constitution
- Division of powers
- Independent judiciary
- Bicameral legislature
- Dual government
