# Module 5: Structure, Powers and Functions of Supreme Court, High Court and Tribunals

## Introduction

The Judiciary is the guardian of the Constitution and protector of Fundamental Rights. India has an **integrated judicial system** with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts in States, and subordinate courts below. This ensures uniform interpretation of law across the country.

---

## 1. Independence of Judiciary and Judicial Activism

### A. Independence of Judiciary

**Why Independent Judiciary is Important?**
- To protect Fundamental Rights
- To act as check on Legislature and Executive
- To interpret Constitution impartially
- To resolve disputes fairly
- To maintain rule of law

### Constitutional Safeguards for Independence:

#### 1. **Security of Tenure**
- Judges can be removed only by **impeachment**
- Cannot be removed at will of Executive
- Serve till retirement age

#### 2. **Fixed Service Conditions**
- Salaries and conditions cannot be changed to their disadvantage
- Charged on Consolidated Fund (not subject to vote)
- Independent of parliamentary approval

#### 3. **Appointment Process**
- Collegium system ensures judges select judges
- Executive's role limited

#### 4. **Prohibition on Practice After Retirement**
- Supreme Court judges cannot practice in any court in India
- High Court judges cannot practice in their High Court or subordinate courts
- Ensures impartiality while serving

#### 5. **Contempt of Court**
- Parliament/Legislature cannot discuss conduct of judges (except in impeachment)
- Protects judges from public pressure
- Maintains dignity of court

#### 6. **Separation from Executive**
- Judiciary separated from executive in States (Article 50)
- Judges don't perform executive functions

### B. Judicial Activism

**What is Judicial Activism?**
- When courts take active role in protecting rights
- Interpret Constitution liberally
- Issue directions to government
- Expand scope of Fundamental Rights

**Forms of Judicial Activism in India:**

1. **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**
   - Relaxation of locus standi
   - Anyone can file for public interest
   - Even postcard is enough
   - **Started with**: Hussainara Khatoon case (1979)

2. **Expanding Article 21**
   - Right to Life expanded to include:
   - Right to livelihood, education, clean environment, speedy trial, legal aid, etc.

3. **Issuing Directions**
   - Mandamus to government
   - Guidelines when law is inadequate
   - Example: Vishaka Guidelines on sexual harassment

4. **Basic Structure Doctrine**
   - Limits Parliament's power to amend
   - Protects Constitution's fundamental features

**Advantages:**
✅ Protects rights of marginalized  
✅ Fills legislative vacuum  
✅ Quick justice  
✅ Holds government accountable  

**Criticisms:**
❌ Judicial overreach  
❌ Encroaches on Legislature's domain  
❌ Lack of expertise in policy matters  
❌ Can't implement decisions

## PART A: SUPREME COURT OF INDIA

## 2. Appointment of Judges (Articles 124-126)

### A. Who Can Be Appointed?

**Qualifications (Article 124(3)):**
1. Must be **citizen of India**
2. AND either:
   - **Judge** of High Court for 5 years, OR
   - **Advocate** of High Court for 10 years, OR
   - **Distinguished jurist** in opinion of President

### B. How Are Judges Appointed?

**Constitutional Provision (Article 124):**
- President appoints
- After consultation with judges of Supreme Court and High Courts

**Evolution of Appointment Process:**

#### **Stage 1: Primacy of Executive (1950-1973)**
- President (i.e., Government) had final say
- Consultation with CJI was formal

#### **Stage 2: Primacy of Judiciary (1993 onwards) - Collegium System**

**Three Judges Cases:**

**1. S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) - First Judges Case**
- Held: "Consultation" doesn't mean "concurrence"
- Government has final say
- CJI's opinion is just advice

**2. Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993) - Second Judges Case**
- **Overruled First Judges Case**
- Held: "Consultation" means "concurrence"
- CJI's opinion has primacy
- **Collegium system** emerged
- CJI + 2 senior-most judges form collegium

**3. Re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (1998) - Third Judges Case**
- Expanded collegium to **CJI + 4 senior-most judges**
- Collegium recommends, President appoints
- Government's role minimal

**Current Collegium System:**
- **For Supreme Court**: CJI + 4 senior-most judges
- **For High Court**: CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges + Chief Justice of concerned HC
- Collegium recommends names
- Government usually accepts
- If rejected, collegium can reiterate, then binding

**Criticism of Collegium:**
❌ Opaque (no transparency)  
❌ Judges appointing judges (nepotism concerns)  
❌ No objective criteria  
❌ No parliamentary oversight  

### C. Number of Judges

- Originally: 1 Chief Justice + 7 judges
- Current: 1 Chief Justice + 33 judges (total 34)
- Parliament can increase number by law

### D. Term and Removal

**Term:**
- Holds office till age of **65 years**
- Can resign earlier
- Can be removed by impeachment

**Removal (Article 124(4)):**
- Only by **impeachment**
- Grounds: **Proved misbehavior** or **incapacity**
- **Procedure**:
  1. Motion initiated in either House
  2. Signed by 100 MPs (Lok Sabha) or 50 MPs (Rajya Sabha)
  3. Investigated by committee
  4. Passed by both Houses with special majority
  5. President removes judge
- **No judge has been successfully impeached** in India so far

## 3. Jurisdiction, Powers and Functions of Supreme Court

### A. **Original Jurisdiction** (Article 131)

**Exclusive Power** to decide disputes between:
1. Union vs One or more States
2. Union and States vs One or more States
3. Two or more States

**Examples:**
- Border disputes between States
- River water disputes (before tribunals)
- Constitutional interpretation disputes

**Exceptions (Cannot hear):**
- Disputes arising from pre-Constitution treaties
- Disputes on matters where treaty provides different mechanism
- Election disputes (go to High Courts)

### B. **Writ Jurisdiction** (Article 32)

**For enforcement of Fundamental Rights:**

**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** called Article 32 **"Heart and Soul"** of Constitution

**Can issue 5 types of writs:**

1. **Habeas Corpus** ("You may have the body")
   - To release person from illegal detention
   - Most important writ for personal liberty

2. **Mandamus** ("We command")
   - To command public official to perform duty
   - Cannot be issued to private person

3. **Prohibition** ("To prohibit")
   - To prevent lower court/tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction
   - Issued before judgment

4. **Certiorari** ("To be certified")
   - To quash order of lower court/tribunal
   - Issued after judgment
   - Corrects jurisdictional errors

5. **Quo Warranto** ("By what authority")
   - To check if person holding public office is legally qualified
   - Prevents usurpation of public office

**Important Points:**
- Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right
- Supreme Court MUST entertain such petitions
- Cannot be suspended (except during emergency for some rights)
- **PIL** expanded access to Article 32

### C. **Appellate Jurisdiction**

**1. Appeals from High Courts (Article 132-134):**

**Constitutional Matters (Article 132):**
- If High Court certifies case involves substantial question of constitutional interpretation
- Appeal lies to Supreme Court

**Civil Matters (Article 133):**
- If High Court certifies case is fit for appeal
- Involves substantial question of law of general importance

**Criminal Matters (Article 134):**
- If High Court:
  - Reversed acquittal and sentenced to death, OR
  - Withdrew case from subordinate court and convicted, OR
  - Certified case is fit for appeal

**Other Matters (Article 136):**
- Special Leave Petition (SLP)
- Supreme Court's discretion
- From any court or tribunal
- Most common route to Supreme Court

**2. Special Leave to Appeal (Article 136):**
- Supreme Court may grant special leave
- From any judgment/order of any court/tribunal
- Discretionary power
- Used when substantial injustice occurred

### D. **Advisory Jurisdiction** (Article 143)

**President can refer questions to Supreme Court:**
- Questions of law or fact
- Of public importance
- Supreme Court gives opinion (advisory)
- **Not binding** on President (but usually followed)

**Examples:**
- Ayodhya dispute reference
- Questions on Presidential powers

### E. **Revisionary Jurisdiction** (Article 137)

**Review of Own Judgments:**
- Supreme Court can review its own judgments
- Correct apparent errors
- Limited power (not appeal)
- Same bench or larger bench

### F. **Power of Judicial Review**

**What is Judicial Review?**
- Power to examine if laws and executive actions are constitutional
- Can declare them void if unconstitutional

**Basis of Judicial Review:**
- Articles 13, 32, 136, 226, 227
- Supremacy of Constitution
- Separation of Powers

**Scope:**
- Review of Legislative actions (laws)
- Review of Executive actions (orders, notifications)
- Review of Judicial actions (lower courts)

**Landmark Cases:**
- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: Basic Structure doctrine
- **Minerva Mills (1980)**: Judicial review is basic structure
- **Maneka Gandhi (1978)**: Procedure established by law must be fair and reasonable

---

## 4. Other Powers of Supreme Court

### A. **Contempt of Court**
- Can punish for its own contempt
- Protects dignity and authority

### B. **Transfer of Cases** (Article 139A)
- Can transfer cases from one High Court to another
- Or from High Court to itself
- If question of law of general importance involved

### C. **Enforcement of Decrees**
- Decrees of Supreme Court enforceable throughout India
- All courts must follow Supreme Court's decisions (Article 141)

### D. **Power to Make Rules**
- Can make rules for its functioning
- Subject to President's approval

## PART B: HIGH COURTS

## 3. Appointment of Judges (Article 217)

### Qualifications:
1. Must be **citizen of India**
2. AND either:
   - Held judicial office in India for 10 years, OR
   - Been advocate of High Court for 10 years

### Appointment Process:
- **President appoints**
- **Collegium System** (like Supreme Court):
  - CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges + Chief Justice of concerned High Court
- After consultation with Governor of State

### Number of Judges:
- Varies from High Court to High Court
- Parliament can increase/decrease by law
- Each High Court has 1 Chief Justice + other judges

### Term:
- Till age of **62 years**
- Can resign
- Can be removed by impeachment (like Supreme Court)

---

## 4. Jurisdiction, Powers and Functions of High Court

### A. **Original Jurisdiction**

**Wide Original Jurisdiction:**
- Can hear cases in the first instance
- Civil and criminal cases
- Writ jurisdiction (Article 226)
- Admiralty jurisdiction (in presidency towns)
- Probate jurisdiction (wills)
- Matrimonial jurisdiction

### B. **Writ Jurisdiction** (Article 226)

**Wider than Supreme Court's Article 32:**

**Can issue writs for:**
1. **Enforcement of Fundamental Rights**, AND/OR
2. **Any other purpose** (legal rights, non-fundamental rights)

**Can Issue Same 5 Writs:**
- Habeas Corpus
- Mandamus
- Prohibition
- Certiorari
- Quo Warranto

**Comparison with Supreme Court:**

| Feature | Supreme Court (Art 32) | High Court (Art 226) |
|---------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **For FRs** | Yes | Yes |
| **For other rights** | No | Yes |
| **Can refuse** | No (must entertain) | Yes (discretionary) |
| **Territorial jurisdiction** | All India | Only that State |
| **Against whom** | Any authority | Any authority |

### C. **Appellate Jurisdiction**

**Appeals from Subordinate Courts:**
- Civil appeals
- Criminal appeals
- Revenue appeals

**Powers:**
- Enhance or reduce sentence
- Acquit or convict
- Order retrial

### D. **Supervisory Jurisdiction** (Article 227)

**Over All Courts and Tribunals in State:**
- Can call for records
- Can issue directions
- Ensures subordinate courts function properly
- Not an appellate power (supervisory only)

### E. **Power of Judicial Review**
- Review laws of State Legislature
- Review executive actions
- Declare them void if unconstitutional

### F. **Other Powers**
- Transfer of cases
- Contempt of court
- Review of own judgments
- Issue directions and orders

---

## 5. Subordinate Courts

**Hierarchy Below High Court:**
1. **District Courts** (District Judges)
2. **Civil Courts** (Civil Judges)
3. **Criminal Courts** (Magistrates - Chief Judicial Magistrate, Judicial Magistrates)
4. **Special Courts** (Family Courts, Fast Track Courts, etc.)

**Appointment:**
- District Judges appointed by Governor in consultation with High Court
- Other judges appointed by Governor as per High Court rules

**Control:**
- High Court controls subordinate courts (Article 227)
- Postings, promotions, transfers controlled by High Court

## 4. Tribunals - Administrative Tribunals and Others

### What are Tribunals?

**Definition:**
- Specialized quasi-judicial bodies
- Decide specific types of disputes
- Alternative to regular courts
- Faster and specialized justice

### Constitutional Provisions:

### A. **Administrative Tribunals** (Article 323A)

**For Service Matters:**

**Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT):**
- Established by Parliament
- Deals with service matters of:
  - Central Government employees
  - All-India Service officers
  - Union Territory employees

**State Administrative Tribunals (SAT):**
- For State Government employees
- Most States have SATs

**Jurisdiction:**
- Recruitment
- Appointment
- Promotion
- Seniority
- Discipline
- Pension
- All service matters

**Exclusion of Court's Jurisdiction:**
- Tribunals have exclusive jurisdiction
- High Courts and Supreme Court cannot hear (except under Article 226/32)
- Intended to reduce burden on courts

### B. **Tribunals for Other Matters** (Article 323B)

**Parliament or State Legislature can establish tribunals for:**

1. **Taxation** - Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT)
2. **Foreign Exchange** - FEMA Tribunal
3. **Industrial Disputes** - Labour Courts, Industrial Tribunals
4. **Land Reforms** - Land Tribunals
5. **Food Stuff** - Food Tribunals
6. **Rent and Tenancy** - Rent Control Tribunals
7. **Compensation** - Motor Accident Claims Tribunal
8. **Environment** - National Green Tribunal (NGT)
9. **Consumer Disputes** - Consumer Forums (District, State, National)
10. **Intellectual Property** - IPAB (Intellectual Property Appellate Board)

### Advantages of Tribunals:

✅ **Speedy justice**: Faster than courts  
✅ **Specialized**: Experts in specific fields  
✅ **Less formal**: Simpler procedures  
✅ **Less expensive**: Lower costs  
✅ **Reduced burden**: Courts relieved  

### Criticisms:

❌ **Judicial independence**: Appointments controlled by Executive  
❌ **Quality of members**: Not always judicial officers  
❌ **Multiple appeals**: Defeats purpose of quick justice  
❌ **Executive influence**: Lack of independence

## 5. Judicial Accountability and Demand for NJAC

### Need for Accountability:

**Concerns:**
- Collegium system lacks transparency
- No external oversight
- Judges selecting judges (closed system)
- No objective criteria
- Delays in appointments

### National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)

**99th Constitutional Amendment (2014)** introduced NJAC

#### Composition of NJAC:
1. **Chief Justice of India** - Chairperson
2. **Two senior-most judges** of Supreme Court
3. **Union Law Minister**
4. **Two eminent persons** (nominated by PM, CJI, Leader of Opposition)
   - Nominated for 3 years
   - Cannot be renominated

#### Functions:
- Recommend appointments to Supreme Court and High Courts
- Recommend transfers of High Court judges
- Ensure transparency

#### Decision Making:
- Decisions by majority
- Any 2 members can veto a recommendation

### Supreme Court Struck Down NJAC

**Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015) - Fourth Judges Case**

**Held:**
- NJAC is **unconstitutional**
- Violates **independence of judiciary**
- Independence is part of **basic structure**
- **Collegium system restored**

**Reasoning:**
- Executive's presence in NJAC affects independence
- Veto power problematic
- Independence of judiciary is basic feature
- Cannot be compromised even by Constitutional amendment

**Current Status:**
- **Collegium system continues**
- Debates continue on reforming collegium
- Government and judiciary at impasse

### Proposed Reforms:

**Suggestions for Improving Collegium:**
- More transparency in selections
- Objective criteria for appointments
- Secretariat for collegium
- Broader consultation
- Fixed timelines for decisions
- Rationale for selections to be recorded

## Landmark Cases

### 1. **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**
- Basic Structure doctrine
- Judicial review is basic structure
- Parliament cannot destroy Constitution's basic features

### 2. **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)**
- Judicial review cannot be taken away
- Articles 32 and 226 are basic structure

### 3. **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)**
- **Issue**: Can Tribunals completely exclude High Courts' jurisdiction?
- **Held**: 
  - NO
  - Judicial review by High Courts under Article 226 cannot be excluded
  - All Tribunal decisions subject to High Court review

### 4. **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (NJAC Case) (2015)**
- Struck down NJAC
- Restored Collegium system
- Independence of judiciary is basic structure

### 5. **S.P. Sampat Kumar v. Union of India (1987)**
- **Issue**: Validity of Administrative Tribunals
- **Held**: Tribunals are constitutional but must have judicial members
- Standards of independence must be maintained

## Comparison: Supreme Court vs High Court

| Feature | Supreme Court | High Court |
|---------|---------------|------------|
| **Establishment** | Article 124 | Article 214 |
| **Judges** | CJI + 33 judges | Varies (each HC) |
| **Retirement** | 65 years | 62 years |
| **Appointment** | President + Collegium | President + Collegium + Governor |
| **Writ Jurisdiction** | Only for FRs (Art 32) | FRs + other rights (Art 226) |
| **Original Jurisdiction** | Centre-State disputes | Wide civil/criminal |
| **Appellate Jurisdiction** | From HCs | From subordinate courts |
| **Advisory Jurisdiction** | Yes (Article 143) | No |
| **Territorial Jurisdiction** | All India | Only that State |
| **Supervision** | Over all courts | Over subordinate courts in State |
