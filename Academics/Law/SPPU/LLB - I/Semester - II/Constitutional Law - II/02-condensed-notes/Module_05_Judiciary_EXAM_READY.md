## 📚 Topic 1: Independence of Judiciary

**What This Covers:** Judiciary must be independent from Legislature and Executive to protect rights and interpret Constitution fairly. Understanding WHY independence matters is key.

### ⚖️ 6 Constitutional Safeguards (COUNT = 6)

**Context:** These safeguards ensure judges can decide cases without fear, favor, or pressure from government or politicians.

#### 1️⃣ Security of Tenure 🛡️
**What it protects:** Judges cannot be removed easily - only through difficult impeachment process, ensuring they don't fear losing job for unpopular decisions.

- Removal only by impeachment
- Cannot be dismissed at will
- Serve till retirement age

#### 2️⃣ Fixed Service Conditions 💰
**What it protects:** Salaries cannot be reduced, preventing government from punishing judges financially for unfavorable judgments.

- Salaries charged on Consolidated Fund
- Cannot be reduced
- Not subject to parliamentary vote

#### 3️⃣ Collegium Appointment 👨‍⚖️
**What it protects:** Judges select judges (collegium system), limiting government's role in appointments and preventing political appointments.

- CJI + senior judges select
- Government's role minimal
- Ensures merit-based selection

#### 4️⃣ No Practice After Retirement 📵
**What it protects:** Retired SC judges cannot practice in any court; HC judges cannot practice in their HC - ensures they don't favor lawyers/parties expecting future work.

- SC judges: Cannot practice anywhere
- HC judges: Cannot practice in that HC

#### 5️⃣ Contempt Protection ⚖️
**What it protects:** Parliament cannot discuss judges' conduct (except impeachment), protecting them from public/political pressure.

- No discussion of conduct in Parliament
- Maintains dignity
- Prevents pressure tactics

#### 6️⃣ Separation from Executive (Article 50) 🔀
**What it protects:** Judges don't perform executive functions, maintaining independence and impartiality.

- No executive roles
- Judicial work only
- Clear separation

## 📚 Topic 2: Supreme Court - Appointment of Judges

**What This Covers:** The appointment process has evolved through 4 "Judges Cases" from government control to judicial control through the collegium system.

### ⚖️ Evolution in 3 Stages (COUNT = 3)

#### Stage 1️⃣: Executive Primacy (1950-1993) 👔
**How it worked:** Government had final say in appointments with CJI's consultation being merely formal advice.

- President appoints (government decides)
- CJI's opinion just advisory
- Government had final say
- Led to biased appointments

#### Stage 2️⃣: Birth of Collegium (1993) 👨‍⚖️
**What changed:** Second Judges Case (1993) revolutionized appointments - CJI's opinion made binding, collegium system emerged.

**Second Judges Case (1993):**
- Overruled First Judges Case (1981)
- "Consultation" means "concurrence"
- CJI + 2 senior judges = Collegium
- Collegium recommends, government appoints

#### Stage 3️⃣: Expanded Collegium (1998-Present) 👨‍⚖️👨‍⚖️👨‍⚖️
**What changed:** Third Judges Case (1998) expanded collegium from 3 to 5 judges for Supreme Court.

**Current Collegium:**
- **For SC**: CJI + 4 senior-most judges
- **For HC**: CJI + 2 SC judges + HC Chief Justice
- Collegium's decision binding (if reiterated)

### ⚠️ NJAC Controversy (2014-2015)

**What Happened:**
- Government passed 99th Amendment creating NJAC (National Judicial Appointments Commission)
- NJAC had 6 members: CJI + 2 SC judges + Law Minister + 2 eminent persons
- Struck down by SC in 2015 (Fourth Judges Case)
- Reason: Violates independence of judiciary (basic structure)
- Collegium system restored

## 📚 Topic 3: Supreme Court - Jurisdictions

**What This Covers:** Supreme Court has 5 different types of jurisdictions - understanding each is crucial for exams as questions often ask about specific jurisdictions.

### ⚖️ 5 Types of Jurisdiction (COUNT = 5)

#### 1️⃣ Original Jurisdiction (Article 131) 🏛️
**What it means:** Supreme Court is the FIRST and ONLY court for certain disputes - parties directly file cases in SC, not lower courts.

**Exclusive disputes:**
- **Union vs State(s)**
- **Union + State(s) vs Other State(s)**
- **State vs State**

**Examples:** Border disputes, river water disputes, constitutional interpretation disputes between governments

#### 2️⃣ Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32) 📜
**What it means:** Supreme Court can issue special orders (writs) to enforce Fundamental Rights. Dr. Ambedkar called this "Heart and Soul" of Constitution.

**5 Types of Writs:**

**1. Habeas Corpus** 🔓 ("Produce the body")
- To release person from illegal detention
- For personal liberty

**2. Mandamus** 📋 ("We command")
- To command public official to perform duty
- Cannot issue to private person

**3. Prohibition** 🚫 ("Stop")
- To prevent lower court from exceeding jurisdiction
- Before final judgment

**4. Certiorari** ✔️ ("To be certified")
- To quash order of lower court/tribunal
- After judgment given
- Corrects errors

**5. Quo Warranto** ❓ ("By what authority")
- To check if person holding public office is qualified
- Prevents unauthorized holding of office

🧠 **Mnemonic for 5 Writs**: **"H-M-P-C-Q"** = **"Help Me Please, Correct Quickly"**

**Article 32 vs Article 226 (High Court):**
- Article 32: Only for Fundamental Rights
- Article 226: For FRs + other legal rights (WIDER)

#### 3️⃣ Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132-136) 📤
**What it means:** Supreme Court hears appeals from High Courts when cases involve important questions of law or justice.

**3 Types of Appeals:**

**A. Constitutional Cases (Article 132):**
- If HC certifies substantial constitutional question
- Automatic right of appeal

**B. Civil & Criminal Cases (Articles 133-134):**
- If HC certifies case fit for appeal
- Death sentence appeals
- High value cases

**C. Special Leave Petition - SLP (Article 136):**
- **Most common route** to Supreme Court
- SC's discretion to grant leave
- From any court/tribunal
- Used when substantial injustice

#### 4️⃣ Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143) 💭
**What it means:** President can refer questions of law/public importance to SC for opinion. SC's opinion is advisory (not binding) but usually followed.

- President refers questions
- SC gives opinion
- Not binding (advisory only)
- Example: Ayodhya reference

#### 5️⃣ Review Jurisdiction (Article 137) 🔄
**What it means:** Supreme Court can review its own judgments to correct errors - acts as check on itself.

- Can review own judgments
- Corrects errors
- Limited power
- Not an appeal

## 📚 Topic 4: High Courts

**What This Covers:** Each State/group of States has a High Court. They have wider powers than Supreme Court in some ways (Article 226 vs Article 32).

### ⚖️ High Court Essentials - 6 Points (COUNT = 6)

#### 1️⃣ Appointment 👨‍⚖️
**How HC judges appointed:** Through collegium of CJI + 2 SC judges + Chief Justice of that High Court, in consultation with Governor.

- President appoints
- Collegium recommends
- Governor consulted
- Age: Must be 30+, Retire at 62

#### 2️⃣ Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226) 📜
**Why wider than SC:** High Court can issue writs not just for Fundamental Rights but for ANY legal right, making it more accessible.

- For Fundamental Rights
- **Also for other legal rights** (unlike Article 32)
- Same 5 writs
- **Discretionary** (can refuse unlike SC)

#### 3️⃣ Original Jurisdiction 🏛️
**What it means:** High Court can hear certain cases directly (not just on appeal) - wider than SC's original jurisdiction.

- Civil and criminal cases
- Writ petitions
- Admiralty, probate, matrimonial cases

#### 4️⃣ Appellate Jurisdiction 📤
**What it means:** High Court hears appeals from all subordinate courts in the State.

- From District Courts
- From Magistrate Courts
- Civil and criminal appeals

#### 5️⃣ Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227) 👁️
**What it means:** High Court supervises all courts and tribunals in the State, ensuring they function properly - not appellate power but supervisory.

- Over all subordinate courts
- Can call for records
- Can issue directions
- Not appeal, only supervision

#### 6️⃣ Powers 💪
**What HC can do:** Judicial review of State laws and executive actions, transfer cases, review own judgments, punish for contempt.

## 📚 Topic 5: Tribunals

**What This Covers:** Tribunals are specialized quasi-judicial bodies for specific matters, providing faster and expert justice compared to regular courts.

### ⚖️ 2 Types of Tribunals (COUNT = 2)

#### 1️⃣ Administrative Tribunals (Article 323A) 🏛️
**What they handle:** Service matters of government employees - recruitment, promotion, dismissal, pension, etc.

**Main Tribunals:**
- **CAT**: Central Administrative Tribunal (Central employees)
- **SAT**: State Administrative Tribunals (State employees)

**Jurisdiction:**
- All service matters
- Replaces courts for these matters
- Faster disposal

#### 2️⃣ Other Tribunals (Article 323B) ⚖️
**What they handle:** Specialized matters like taxation, consumer disputes, environment, requiring expert knowledge.

**9 Types Possible:**
1. **Taxation**: Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT)
2. **Foreign Exchange**: FEMA Tribunal
3. **Industrial Disputes**: Labour Courts
4. **Land Reforms**: Land Tribunals
5. **Environment**: National Green Tribunal (NGT)
6. **Consumer**: Consumer Forums
7. **Rent**: Rent Tribunals
8. **Motor Accidents**: MACT
9. **Intellectual Property**: IPAB

### 📌 L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)
**What this case held:** Tribunals cannot completely exclude High Court's jurisdiction under Article 226. All tribunal orders subject to HC review - protects judicial review.

## ⚡ 30-SECOND RECALL - SC vs HC

| Feature | Supreme Court | High Court |
|---------|--------------|------------|
| **Judges** | Retire at **65** | Retire at **62** |
| **Writ** | Only for **FRs** (Art 32) | FRs + **other rights** (Art 226) |
| **Original** | Centre-State disputes | Wide civil/criminal |
| **Advisory** | Yes (Art 143) | No |
| **Area** | All India | That State only |
