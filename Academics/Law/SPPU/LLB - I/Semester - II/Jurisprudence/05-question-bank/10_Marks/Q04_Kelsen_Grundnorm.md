## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- Pure Theory — purge psychology, sociology, ethics, politics.
- Hierarchy of norms (Stufenbau).
- Grundnorm — the presupposed basic norm.
- Static vs dynamic systems.
- Revolutionary application in *Dosso* and later cases.

### 🧠 Introduction

Hans Kelsen (1881–1973), founder of the **Pure Theory of Law**, sought a normative science of law unmixed with morality, sociology, or politics. The cornerstone of his theory is the **Grundnorm** — the basic norm that supplies the ultimate validity of the legal order.

### 📖 Purity and method

Law studies **norms**, not behaviour. A norm is an *“ought”* statement issued by a competent authority. Kelsen excludes:

- moral evaluation (ethics),
- description of regularities (sociology),
- expressions of political preference.

The result is a **purely formal science of normative orders**.

### 📖 Hierarchy of norms (Stufenbau)

Norms are arranged in a hierarchy: every lower norm derives validity from a higher norm. Constitution → statutes → regulations → individual judicial and administrative norms.

### 📖 The Grundnorm

The highest norm in any legal system is **presupposed** rather than enacted. It cannot itself be derived from a higher legal norm; it is a **transcendental-logical postulate** without which we could not treat the order as a system of *ought*-norms.

For India, the Grundnorm corresponds roughly to the **Constitution of India** — the historically first constitution accepted as the source of validity for everything below.

### 📖 Static vs dynamic legal systems

- In a **static** system, validity is determined by **content** (norms derived from a basic moral value).
- In a **dynamic** system, validity is determined by **formal authorisation** (a norm is valid if enacted by the procedure prescribed by a higher norm).

Legal systems, says Kelsen, are predominantly **dynamic**.

### 📖 Revolutionary application

- *State v Dosso* (Pakistan, 1958) — a successful revolution shifts the Grundnorm because a new constitution becomes the basic norm.
- *Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke* (Rhodesia, 1968) — court applied Kelsen but ultimately distinguished revolutionary effectiveness from legal validity.
- *Asma Jilani v Government of Punjab* (Pakistan, 1972) — doubted *Dosso*; mere effectiveness cannot supply legal validity.

### 🔻 Criticism

- **Hart** — the Grundnorm is mysterious; the rule of recognition is a better foundation.
- **Realists** — purity is unrealistic; law is influenced by politics and sociology.
- **Natural lawyers** — Kelsen’s system cannot distinguish a tyrant’s legal order from a just one.

### 🧾 Conclusion

The Pure Theory remains the most rigorous **structural** account of law. Its Grundnorm explains why a constitution is binding and why a successful revolution can supplant the legal order. Its formal purity, however, makes it ill-suited for assessing **what** the law ought to be.
