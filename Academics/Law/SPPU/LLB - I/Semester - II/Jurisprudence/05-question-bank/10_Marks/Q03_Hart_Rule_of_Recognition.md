# Q03 (10 Marks) — Hart’s rule of recognition

**Module:** 04 — Legal Positivism
**Marks:** 10

## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- Hart’s union of primary and secondary rules.
- Three secondary rules — **Recognition, Change, Adjudication (RCA)**.
- Internal point of view.
- Indian application via the Constitution.
- Hart’s answer to Austin’s gunman analogy.

### 🧠 Introduction

In *The Concept of Law* (1961), H.L.A. Hart replaced Austin’s command theory with a more sophisticated model: a legal system is the **union of primary and secondary rules**. The **rule of recognition** is the master test of validity.

### 📖 Primary and secondary rules

- **Primary rules** impose duties — *do not kill, pay tax, perform the contract.*
- **Secondary rules** confer powers and operate on primary rules.

Hart classifies secondary rules into three (**RCA**):

1. **Rule of recognition** — identifies what counts as law.
2. **Rules of change** — empower legislatures and individuals to alter rules.
3. **Rules of adjudication** — empower courts to determine and apply rules.

### 📖 The rule of recognition itself

- It is **practised** by officials of the legal system who accept it from an **internal point of view**.
- It cannot itself be valid — it is the **ultimate** test; its existence is a *social fact*.
- A modern rule of recognition is usually complex — for India, the Constitution (along with valid amendments and recognised judicial sources) functions as the rule of recognition.

### 📖 Internal vs external point of view

- **External** — observer describes regularities of behaviour ("most people stop at red signals").
- **Internal** — participant treats the rule as a **standard of criticism** — uses normative language ("you ought to stop").

The internal point of view, especially among officials, sustains the legal system.

### 📖 Reply to Austin

- Hart’s gunman analogy shows command-plus-sanction cannot distinguish law from an armed robber. Authority requires acceptance of rules, not just fear.
- Customary law and constitutional limits fit comfortably under recognition.

### ⚖️ Indian application

- The Indian Supreme Court’s **basic-structure doctrine** can be seen as Hart-style identification of what constitutional rules count.
- *Indra Sawhney v UoI* and constitutional adjudication generally use a Hartian rule-of-recognition framework even when not citing him.

### 🔻 Criticism

- **Dworkin** — Hart neglects principles; the rule of recognition cannot identify *principles* like *no one shall profit from his own wrong* (*Riggs v Palmer*).
- **Realists** — over-emphasises rules; understates the role of judicial discretion.

### 🧾 Conclusion

The rule of recognition is the **keystone** of Hart’s positivism. It explains validity, accommodates custom and constitution, and supplies the test of internal acceptance. Yet, it leaves room for moral principles to influence law — a tension that has driven post-Hartian jurisprudence.
