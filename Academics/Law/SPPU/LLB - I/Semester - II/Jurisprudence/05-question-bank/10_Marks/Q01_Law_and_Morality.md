# Q01 (10 Marks) — Examine the relationship between law and morality

**Module:** 01 — Introduction to Jurisprudence
**Marks:** 10
**Difficulty:** 🟡 Medium
**ID:** JUR-10M-Q001

## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧾 Synopsis

- **Issue:** Are law and morality conceptually linked, or do they belong to separate enquiries?
- **Two theses:** Overlap (natural lawyers) vs Separation (positivists).
- **Key debates:** Hart–Fuller (1958), Devlin–Hart (1959–63).
- **Indian touch:** *Naz Foundation* (2009), *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018).
- **Conclusion:** Law and morality are *distinguishable* but not *unrelated*.

### 🧠 Introduction

Few questions in jurisprudence are older than the relationship between **law** and **morality**. The classical tradition (Plato, Aquinas) saw law as a moral order; modern positivism (Bentham, Austin, Hart, Kelsen) treats law as a social fact whose **validity** does not depend on its **moral merit**.

### 📖 Body

#### 1. The overlap thesis — natural lawyers

For natural lawyers, an unjust enactment loses the *quality* of law — *lex iniusta non est lex* (Aquinas). Lon Fuller went further and argued that even a positivist system must respect an **internal morality of law** — eight desiderata such as publicity, prospectivity, and clarity. John Finnis listed **seven basic goods** that any reasonable legal system serves.

#### 2. The separation thesis — positivists

Austin and Bentham insisted on the **separation thesis**: "the existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another." Hart conceded a **minimum content of natural law** but kept it within positivist limits. Kelsen pushed the divide further with his Pure Theory — law studied without moral admixture.

#### 3. Hart–Fuller debate (1958)

Triggered by Nazi-era statutes, Hart argued that even wicked regimes can produce *valid* law, but citizens may have **moral grounds** to disobey. Fuller replied that systems failing the inner morality of law (clarity, publicity, etc.) are not law at all.

#### 4. Devlin–Hart debate (1959–63)

Lord Devlin, in *The Enforcement of Morals*, argued the law may legitimately enforce shared social morality. Hart, drawing on Mill, restricted the law to preventing **harm to others**. The Indian Supreme Court has echoed Hart in privacy and personal autonomy jurisprudence.

#### 5. Indian application

- *Naz Foundation v NCT of Delhi* (Delhi HC, 2009) and *Navtej Singh Johar v UoI* (SC, 2018): the court preferred **constitutional morality** over **popular morality**.
- *Joseph Shine v UoI* (SC, 2018, adultery): morality cannot be used to enforce stereotype-based criminal sanctions.

### ⚖️ Cases to cite

- *Riggs v Palmer* (1889) — moral principle limits literal text.
- *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) — neighbour principle blends moral and legal duty.
- *Navtej Singh Johar v UoI* (2018) — Indian invocation of constitutional morality.

### 🧾 Conclusion

Law and morality occupy **overlapping spheres**. Even on positivist premises, morality intrudes through Fuller’s **inner morality**, Hart’s **minimum content**, and India’s constitutional morality. The exam-friendly answer: keep the conceptual question separate from the **policy** question.
