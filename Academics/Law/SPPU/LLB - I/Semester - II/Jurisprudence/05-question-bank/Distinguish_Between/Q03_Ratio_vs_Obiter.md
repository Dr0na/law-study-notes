# Distinguish — Ratio Decidendi vs Obiter Dicta

**ID:** JUR-D03

## MODEL ANSWER

### 🧠 Introduction

Within every judgment, only the **ratio decidendi** binds future courts; the rest is **obiter dicta**, persuasive at best. The distinction is fundamental to the doctrine of precedent under **Article 141** of the Constitution.

### 📊 Table of distinctions

| 🔍 Basis | Ratio decidendi | Obiter dicta |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Meaning | The legal reason for the decision | Side observations not necessary to the decision |
| Binding force | **Binds** lower courts (Art. 141) | **Persuasive** only |
| Identification | Material facts + the rule applied | Anything else in the judgment |
| Test | Would the decision change if this statement were removed? | Does the judgment still stand without it? |
| Examples | Neighbour principle — *Donoghue v Stevenson* | Hypothetical illustrations or general remarks |
| Function | Settles the legal point | Guides future development |
| Discarded by | Overruling, *per incuriam* | Disagreement |
| Topper one-liner | **Ratio binds, obiter persuades (RB-OP)** | — |

### ⚖️ Indian illustration

- In *Maneka Gandhi v UoI* (1978), the holding that "procedure established by law" under Article 21 must be "just, fair, and reasonable" is the **ratio**. Observations on natural justice exceeding that point are widely treated as **persuasive obiter** that have nonetheless guided later decisions.

### 🧾 Conclusion

Identifying the ratio is a craft skill — separate the **material facts**, identify the **rule that was actually applied**, and treat everything else as persuasive. A topper answer always notes that *finding the ratio is a matter of substance, not labels*.
