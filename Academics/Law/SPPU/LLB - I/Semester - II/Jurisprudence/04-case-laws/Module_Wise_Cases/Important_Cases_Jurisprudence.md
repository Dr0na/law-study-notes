# ⚖️ Important Cases — Jurisprudence (LCC 0604)

> Concise digest, organised module-wise. **Verify** every citation against an authoritative reporter before quoting in an exam.

## Module 02 — Sources of Law

### Golak Nath v State of Punjab, AIR 1967 SC 1643

- **Facts:** Constitutional amendments curtailed property rights of land-holders.
- **Issue:** Whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights and the doctrine of prospective overruling could apply.
- **Held:** Majority held Parliament cannot abridge FRs; introduced **prospective overruling** in India.
- **Principle:** A constitutional court may declare law **only for the future** to protect past transactions.
- **Exam relevance:** Cite for **prospective overruling** in the precedent topic.

### Bengal Immunity Co. v State of Bihar, AIR 1955 SC 661

- **Facts:** Question whether the Supreme Court was bound by its own previous decisions.
- **Held:** SC is **not** bound by its earlier decisions; can reconsider when justice and public good require.
- **Principle:** Vertical *stare decisis* (Article 141) is strong; horizontal stare decisis within SC is **flexible**.

### Union of India v Raghubir Singh, (1989) 2 SCC 754

- **Facts:** Decision of two-judge bench in conflict with three-judge bench.
- **Held:** Larger bench prevails; *per incuriam* decisions are not binding.
- **Principle:** Hierarchy among coordinate benches.

## Module 04 — Legal Positivism

### State v Dosso, PLD 1958 SC (Pak) 533

- **Facts:** Validity of laws passed by a military regime following a successful revolution.
- **Held:** A successful revolution becomes a *legal fact* and supplies a new **Grundnorm** (citing Kelsen).
- **Principle:** Illustrates Kelsen’s **Pure Theory** in real-world adjudication.
- **Exam relevance:** Direct authority for Grundnorm discussion (Module 04).

### Asma Jilani v Government of Punjab, PLD 1972 SC (Pak) 139

- **Held:** *Dosso* doctrine doubted; mere effectiveness of a regime cannot supply legal validity.
- **Principle:** Criticism of pure Grundnorm shifts; introduces *normative* limits.

## Module 03 / 04 — Law and morality

### Riggs v Palmer, 115 N.Y. 506 (1889)

- **Facts:** Grandson murdered grandfather to inherit under valid will.
- **Held:** Court denied inheritance applying the maxim *"no one shall profit from his own wrong."*
- **Principle:** Demonstrates **moral principle** trumping literal statutory text — central example in the Hart–Dworkin debate.

### Donoghue v Stevenson, [1932] AC 562

- **Facts:** Decomposed snail in a bottle of ginger beer; consumer fell ill.
- **Held:** Manufacturer owes duty of care to ultimate consumer.
- **Principle:** **Neighbour principle** — a key illustration of judicial law-making (constitutive theory of precedent).

## Module 06 — Person, Rights, Duties

### Pramatha Nath Mullick v Pradyumna Kumar Mullick, AIR 1925 PC 139

- **Facts:** Dispute over the worship of a family deity.
- **Held:** A Hindu idol is a **juristic person** capable of holding property through trustees/shebaits.
- **Principle:** Cornerstone authority for **legal personality of idols** in India.

### Shiromani Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee v Som Nath Dass, (2000) 4 SCC 146

- **Held:** *Guru Granth Sahib* is a juristic person.
- **Principle:** Expands the concept of legal personality beyond idols.

## Module 07 — Ownership & Possession

### Asher v Whitlock, (1865) LR 1 QB 1

- **Facts:** Adverse possessor died; his successors sued the next intruder.
- **Held:** **Possession is good against all but the true owner**; even a wrongful possessor can sue subsequent trespassers.
- **Principle:** Strength of possessory remedies.

### Yashwant Singh v Jagdish Singh, AIR 1968 SC 620

- **Held:** Suit under **Section 9 (now Section 6), Specific Relief Act** does not require proof of title; only proof of prior possession and dispossession otherwise than in due course of law.
- **Principle:** Statutory protection of possession in India.

## Module 08 — Liability

### Rylands v Fletcher, (1868) LR 3 HL 330

- **Facts:** Mill-pond water escaped through disused mine-shafts into the plaintiff’s colliery.
- **Held:** Person who brings a **non-natural** thing onto land that escapes is liable without proof of fault, subject to classical defences.
- **Principle:** **Strict liability** — defences include act of God, plaintiff’s default, third-party act, statutory authority, consent.

### M.C. Mehta v Union of India, (1987) 1 SCC 395 (Oleum Gas Leak)

- **Facts:** Oleum gas escape from Shriram Foods and Fertilizers caused public injury.
- **Held:** An enterprise engaged in **hazardous** activity owes an **absolute and non-delegable** duty. No exceptions; quantum is exemplary.
- **Principle:** **Absolute liability** — Indian SC departs from *Rylands*. Foundation for Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991; Environment Protection Act, 1986.

### Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement, (2005) 4 SCC 530

- **Held:** A corporation **can be prosecuted and punished** for offences requiring imprisonment **and** fine — the court can impose **fine**.
- **Principle:** Settles **corporate criminal liability** in India.

### Iridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola Inc., (2011) 1 SCC 74

- **Held:** Mens rea can be attributed to a company through the **directing mind** (alter ego) doctrine.
- **Principle:** Companies are not immune from criminal prosecution requiring mens rea.

### State of Rajasthan v Vidhyawati, AIR 1962 SC 933

- **Facts:** Government driver killed pedestrian.
- **Held:** State is vicariously liable; doctrine of sovereign immunity is **not** to be applied to ordinary tort claims.

### Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v State of UP, AIR 1965 SC 1039

- **Facts:** Police misappropriated seized gold.
- **Held:** State not vicariously liable for acts done in exercise of sovereign functions (the surviving residual doctrine, now considerably narrowed).

## Quick-recall thread

> 🧠 **String of facts for revision:** Golak Nath (prospective overruling) → Bengal Immunity (SC not bound) → Dosso/Asma Jilani (Grundnorm) → Riggs (moral check) → Donoghue (neighbour) → Pramatha Nath (idol = legal person) → Asher (possession good against all but true owner) → Rylands → Mehta (strict to absolute) → Standard Chartered/Iridium (corporate criminal) → Vidhyawati/Kasturilal (State vicarious).
