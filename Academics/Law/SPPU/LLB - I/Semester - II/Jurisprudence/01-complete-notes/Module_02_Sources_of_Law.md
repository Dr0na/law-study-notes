# Module 2: Sources of Law

## Introduction

Sources of law are the origins from which law derives its authority and binding force. Understanding sources helps us know **where law comes from**, **how it develops**, and **what gives it validity**. The Constitution, statutes, customs, judicial precedents, and scholarly writings are all sources that feed into the legal system.

---

## 1. CUSTOM - First Source of Law

### A. Meaning of Custom

**What is Custom?**
Custom is a rule of conduct that has been followed by people from ancient times consistently and uniformly, with a belief that it is legally obligatory. It is the oldest source of law - law often originates from customs that society has followed for generations.

**Examples:**
- Hindu law largely based on customs
- Customary succession practices
- Traditional trade practices
- Local customs in marriage ceremonies

### B. Nature of Custom

**Key Features:**

#### **1. Long Established Practice** ⏰
- Must be followed from ancient/immemorial times
- Not recent practice
- Passed down through generations

#### **2. Continuous and Uniform Observance** 🔄
- Must be practiced continuously (not occasionally)
- Must be uniform (not varying)
- Consistency over time

#### **3. Accepted as Law** ⚖️
- Community must believe it's legally obligatory
- Not just habit or convention
- Sense of legal duty (opinio juris)

#### **4. Reasonable** 💡
- Must not be unreasonable or absurd
- Should accord with justice
- Courts examine reasonableness

### C. Essential Conditions for Valid Custom

**For custom to be recognized as law, it must satisfy certain tests:**

#### **Condition 1: Antiquity (Immemorial)** 🏛️
**What it requires:** Custom must be ancient - existing from time immemorial.

**India:** Reasonable period of antiquity sufficient (not necessarily from "beginning of time")

#### **Condition 2: Continuity** ⏰
**What it requires:** Must have been followed continuously without interruption.

- Not occasional practice
- Consistent observance
- If discontinued for long period, loses validity

#### **Condition 3: Uniformity** 📏
**What it requires:** Must be observed uniformly in similar circumstances.

- Not varying practice
- Consistent application
- Same rule in similar cases

#### **Condition 4: Certainty** ✅
**What it requires:** Custom must be clear and definite, not vague or ambiguous.

- People know what it requires
- Not uncertain or doubtful
- Clear rule

#### **Condition 5: Reasonableness** 💡
**What it requires:** Must be reasonable and not opposed to public policy or morality.

- Not absurd or irrational
- Accords with justice
- Not against public interest

**Example:** Custom allowing sati (widow burning) rejected as unreasonable

#### **Condition 6: Conformity with Statute** 📜
**What it requires:** Must not be opposed to any statute law.

- Statute prevails over custom
- Custom cannot override legislation
- Unless statute saves custom

#### **Condition 7: Not Opposed to Morality** 🕊️
**What it requires:** Should not be immoral or against accepted moral standards.

- Conforms to public conscience
- Not shocking to moral sense

#### **Condition 8: Peaceable Enjoyment** ☮️
**What it requires:** Must have been enjoyed peacefully, not by force or fraud.

- Voluntary acceptance
- Not imposed forcibly
- Not based on deception

### D. Kinds of Custom

#### **1. By Area of Application:**

**A. Local Custom:**
- Applies to specific locality
- Limited geographical area
- Example: Village customs, tribal customs

**B. General Custom:**
- Applies throughout the country
- Universal in nation
- Example: Customs followed across India

#### **2. By Legal Recognition:**

**A. Legal Custom (Customary Law):**
- Recognized and enforced by courts
- Has force of law
- Example: Hindu customary law

**B. Conventional Custom:**
- Based on agreement/convention
- Binding on parties who adopted it
- Example: Trade customs (CIF, FOB in commerce)

### E. Volkgeist (Savigny's Concept)

**What is Volkgeist?**

**Meaning:**
- German word: **"Volk"** (people) + **"Geist"** (spirit)
- **"Spirit of the people"** or **"National spirit"**

**Savigny's Theory:**
- Law is not made artificially
- Law grows organically from the **spirit of the people** (Volkgeist)
- Like language, law evolves naturally from people's consciousness
- Legislator cannot create law arbitrarily - must reflect Volkgeist

**Significance:**
- Emphasizes custom as source of law
- Law must accord with people's consciousness
- Imported laws may fail if don't match Volkgeist
- Historical school's key contribution

**Example:** British tried to impose their laws in India, but many didn't work because they didn't match Indian Volkgeist (customs, traditions, values)

## 2. LEGISLATION - Most Important Modern Source

### A. Meaning of Legislation

**What is Legislation?**
Legislation is the process of law-making by a competent authority (legislature). It is the formal enactment of law through deliberate and conscious process.

**Etymology:** Latin "Legis" (law) + "Latum" (to make) = Law-making

### B. Nature of Legislation

#### **Characteristics:**

**1. Made by Competent Authority**
- Legislature (Parliament, State Legislature)
- Executive (delegated legislation)
- Not by individuals

**2. Formal Procedure**
- Definite process (bill, debate, vote, assent)
- Not informal or spontaneous
- Systematic and organized

**3. Written Form**
- Always in writing
- Published officially
- Accessible to all

**4. Certain**
- Clear and definite
- People know the law
- More certain than custom

**5. Prospective Usually**
- Normally applies to future conduct
- Retrospective legislation rare and disfavored
- People can plan conduct

### C. Kinds of Legislation

#### **1. By Authority:**

**A. Supreme Legislation:**
- Made by supreme law-making body (Parliament)
- Cannot be challenged or set aside (except constitutional review)
- Highest form of legislation

**B. Subordinate/Delegated Legislation:**
- Made by authority subordinate to Parliament (Executive, local bodies)
- Under powers delegated by Parliament
- Can be challenged if exceeds delegation
- Examples: Rules, regulations, bye-laws, ordinances

#### **2. By Nature:**

**A. Substantive Legislation:**
- Creates new law or modifies existing law
- Affects rights and duties
- Example: New criminal offenses added

**B. Procedural Legislation:**
- Prescribes procedure for enforcing substantive law
- Doesn't create new rights
- Example: Amendment to CPC

#### **3. By Function:**

**A. Codifying Legislation:**
- Codifies existing laws (custom, precedents) into one statute
- Systematic arrangement
- Example: Indian Penal Code (1860) codified criminal law

**B. Consolidating Legislation:**
- Consolidates various statutes on same subject into one
- Doesn't change law substantially
- Makes law accessible

**C. Amending Legislation:**
- Amends existing legislation
- Modifies, adds, or deletes provisions

**D. Repealing Legislation:**
- Repeals (abolishes) existing legislation
- Express repeal (explicitly states) or Implied repeal (new law inconsistent)

### D. Merits of Legislation

#### **Merit 1: Certain and Definite** ✅
- Written in clear language
- Easily ascertainable
- No ambiguity (ideally)

#### **Merit 2: Can Bring Rapid Change** 🚀
- Quick law-making possible
- Can respond to urgent needs
- Not slow like custom formation

#### **Merit 3: Systematic and Organized** 📋
- Logically arranged
- Coherent structure
- Easy to study and apply

#### **Merit 4: Reflects Popular Will** 🗳️
- Made by elected representatives
- Democratic legitimacy
- Represents people's choice

#### **Merit 5: Can Abolish Obsolete Customs** 🔨
- Can remove outdated customs
- Social reform possible
- Example: Sati abolition, Untouchability abolition

### E. Demerits of Legislation

#### **Demerit 1: May Be Rigid** 🔒
- Once enacted, difficult to change immediately
- Cannot adapt quickly to changing situations
- Amendment process takes time

#### **Demerit 2: May Not Reflect Social Reality** ⚠️
- Law may be ahead or behind social practices
- Gap between law in books and law in action
- Example: Prohibition laws often not followed

#### **Demerit 3: Technical Language** 📖
- Legal jargon difficult for common people
- Not easily understood
- Needs legal experts to interpret

#### **Demerit 4: May Be Political** 🎭
- Influenced by political considerations
- May favor powerful groups
- May not always be just

### F. Relation with Other Sources

**Legislation and Custom:**
- Legislation can abolish custom
- Legislation can recognize and confirm custom
- Statute prevails over custom in case of conflict

**Legislation and Precedent:**
- Legislation can overrule judicial precedents
- Courts interpret legislation (creating precedents)
- Statute binds courts

**Legislation and Juristic Writings:**
- Legislature may adopt views of jurists
- Scholars critique legislation

## 3. PRECEDENT - Judge-Made Law

### A. Meaning of Precedent

**What is Precedent?**
Precedent (or Case Law) is a judicial decision that serves as authority for deciding future similar cases. When a court decides a case, that decision becomes a precedent for future cases involving similar facts and legal issues.

**Also Called:**
- Case law
- Judge-made law
- Common law (in countries like England, India)

### B. Nature of Precedent

**Key Features:**

#### **1. Based on Judicial Decisions**
- Emerges from court judgments
- Not legislation
- Judicial law-making

#### **2. Gradual Development**
- Develops case by case
- Slow evolution
- Not sudden like legislation

#### **3. Flexible**
- Can adapt to new situations
- Courts can distinguish
- More flexible than legislation

#### **4. Requires Legal Reasoning**
- Courts give reasons (ratio decidendi)
- Logical basis
- Persuasive reasoning

### C. Doctrine of Stare Decisis

**What is Stare Decisis?**

**Meaning:**
- Latin phrase: **"Stare decisis et non quieta movere"**
- **"Stand by decisions and do not disturb settled matters"**
- Principle that courts should follow previous decisions

**Why Stare Decisis?**

**Reasons:**

**1. Certainty and Predictability** 📏
- People know what law is
- Can plan conduct
- Avoids arbitrary decisions

**2. Consistency** 🔄
- Similar cases decided similarly
- Equality before law
- No discrimination

**3. Efficiency** ⚡
- Saves judicial time
- No need to decide same issue repeatedly
- Precedents provide ready answers

**4. Respect for Past Wisdom** 📚
- Earlier judges were wise
- Their decisions deserve respect
- Continuity maintained

### D. Article 141 of Constitution of India

**Constitutional Provision:**

**Article 141:** "The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India"

**What it means:**
- Supreme Court's decisions bind all lower courts (High Courts, District Courts, etc.)
- All courts in India must follow SC's rulings
- Ensures uniformity of law throughout India

**Important Points:**
- "Law declared" not "judgment" - principle laid down binds, not just the decision
- Binding on all courts - no court can disobey
- Within territory of India - applicable throughout nation

---

### E. Kinds of Precedent

#### **1. By Binding Nature:**

**A. Binding Precedent (Authoritative Precedent):**
- Must be followed by lower courts
- No choice
- Supreme Court decisions bind all courts
- High Court decisions bind lower courts in that State

**B. Persuasive Precedent:**
- Not binding but can be followed
- Courts may consider but not compelled
- Examples:
  - Decisions of foreign courts
  - Decisions of other High Courts
  - Obiter dicta of higher courts
  - Dissenting judgments

#### **2. By Type:**

**A. Original Precedent:**
- Decision in new situation (first impression)
- No previous precedent exists
- Court creates new rule

**B. Declaratory Precedent:**
- Merely declares existing law
- Applies established principle
- No new law made

---

### F. Ratio Decidendi and Obiter Dicta

**Two Parts of Judgment:**

#### **1. Ratio Decidendi** ⚖️

**Meaning:** "Reason for deciding" - the legal principle on which decision is based

**What it is:**
- The rule of law on which court's decision is founded
- Necessary for the decision
- This part is binding

**How to Find Ratio:**
- Identify material facts
- Identify legal issue
- Find rule applied to decide
- That rule is ratio

**Example:** If court holds "contract without consideration is void" and decides accordingly, this principle is ratio decidendi

**Binding Effect:** Ratio decidendi of higher court binds lower courts

#### **2. Obiter Dicta** 💭

**Meaning:** "Things said by the way" - observations not necessary for decision

**What it is:**
- Remarks made by judge incidentally
- Not essential to decision
- Hypothetical observations
- General principles stated

**Examples:**
- "If facts were different, decision would be..."
- General observations on law
- Comments on other issues not before court

**Binding Effect:** Obiter dicta is NOT binding (but has persuasive value)

**Important Distinction:**
- **Ratio** = Binding (must follow)
- **Obiter** = Persuasive (may follow)

---

### G. Doctrine of Prospective Overruling

**What is Prospective Overruling?**

**Traditional Rule:**
When court overrules previous decision, new law applies:
- To future cases (prospectively), AND
- To past transactions (retrospectively)

**Prospective Overruling:**
Court overrules previous decision BUT:
- New law applies only to **future cases** (from date of judgment)
- Does **not affect past transactions** decided under old law
- Protects vested rights

**Why Needed:**
- People relied on old law
- Acted in good faith
- Retrospective application would cause hardship
- Balances need for change with protection of settled rights

**Example in India:**
- **Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)**: Supreme Court used prospective overruling
- Held: Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights
- But applied prospectively only (past amendments not affected)

**Criticism:**
- Courts making law for future (legislative function?)
- Selective application
- May deny justice to party before court

---

### H. Circumstances Destroying Binding Force of Precedent

**When can courts not follow precedent?**

#### **Situation 1: Distinguishing** 🔍
**What it means:** Material facts of current case different from precedent case.

- If facts substantially different, precedent not applicable
- Courts "distinguish" the case
- Can avoid unwanted precedent

#### **Situation 2: Overruling** ❌
**What it means:** Higher court declares previous decision wrong.

- Supreme Court can overrule its own decisions
- Or overrule High Court decisions
- Precedent loses binding force

#### **Situation 3: Sub Silentio** 🤫
**What it means:** Decision given without considering relevant law or precedent.

- Important point not argued
- Relevant statute/precedent not cited
- Such decision has weak authority

#### **Situation 4: Per Incuriam** ⚠️
**What it means:** Decision given in ignorance of statute or binding precedent.

- Court didn't consider relevant law
- Through inadvertence
- Not binding

#### **Situation 5: Overruled by Legislation** 📜
**What it means:** Statute passed changing the law laid down by precedent.

- Parliament can override court decisions by legislation
- Courts then follow statute
- Legislative supremacy

#### **Situation 6: Reversed on Appeal** 🔄
**What it means:** Higher court reverses decision on appeal.

- Loses binding force
- No longer law

---

### I. Significance of Precedent

**Why Precedent Important:**

✅ **Provides certainty** - People know what law is  
✅ **Ensures consistency** - Similar cases decided similarly  
✅ **Develops law gradually** - Case by case evolution  
✅ **Flexible** - Can adapt to changing times  
✅ **Detailed** - Covers situations legislation may not  
✅ **Based on real cases** - Practical not abstract  

---

### J. Relation with Other Sources

**Precedent and Legislation:**
- Legislation prevails
- Courts interpret legislation (creating precedents)
- Balance between both

**Precedent and Custom:**
- Courts recognize customs (custom becomes law through precedent)
- Precedent may supersede custom

## 4. JURISTIC WRITINGS - Scholarly Works

### A. Meaning

**What are Juristic Writings?**
Writings of eminent jurists, legal scholars, and commentators on law. These include textbooks, commentaries, treatises, and scholarly articles that explain and analyze law.

**Examples:**
- Blackstone's Commentaries (England)
- Salmond on Jurisprudence
- Mulla on Hindu Law
- Ratanlal on IPC

### B. Nature

**Characteristics:**

#### **1. Scholarly Works**
- Written by legal experts
- Based on research and analysis
- Systematic exposition of law

#### **2. Persuasive Authority**
- Not binding like legislation or precedent
- Courts may follow but not compelled
- Persuasive value based on author's eminence

#### **3. Explain and Criticize**
- Explain existing law
- Critique judicial decisions
- Suggest reforms

### C. Significance

**Why Juristic Writings Important:**

#### **1. In Ancient Times** 📜
**High Authority:**
- Roman jurisconsults' opinions had force of law
- Manu, Yajnavalkya in Hindu law had great authority
- Islamic jurists' interpretations binding

#### **2. In Modern Times** 📚
**Persuasive Value:**
- Courts cite eminent jurists
- Textbooks consulted for interpretation
- Commentaries guide judges

**Examples in India:**
- Mulla on Hindu Law widely cited
- Ratanlal & Dhirajlal on IPC quoted in judgments
- Salmond, Austin quoted by Supreme Court

#### **3. For Students** 🎓
**Educational Value:**
- Primary study material
- Explains complex concepts
- Systematic learning

#### **4. For Law Reform** 🔧
**Reform Proposals:**
- Jurists suggest reforms
- Law Commission considers scholarly views
- Influences legislation

### D. Relation with Other Sources

**Juristic Writings and Legislation:**
- Legislature may adopt jurists' suggestions
- Writings critique legislation

**Juristic Writings and Precedent:**
- Courts cite juristic works
- Jurists analyze and critique judgments
- Mutual influence

**Juristic Writings and Custom:**
- Jurists record and explain customs
- Help courts recognize customs

## Summary of Four Sources

### Comparison Table:

| Source | Origin | Binding Force | Certainty | Development |
|--------|--------|---------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Custom** | People's practice | Medium (if recognized) | Low (unwritten) | Very slow |
| **Legislation** | Legislature | High (compulsory) | High (written) | Fast (can be instant) |
| **Precedent** | Courts | High (binding) | Medium | Gradual |
| **Juristic Writings** | Scholars | Low (persuasive) | Medium | Continuous |
