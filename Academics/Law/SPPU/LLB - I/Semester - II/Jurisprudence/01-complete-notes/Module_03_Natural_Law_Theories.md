# Module 3: Natural Law Theories and Revival of Natural Law

## Introduction

Natural Law is one of the oldest schools of jurisprudence, believing that there exists a **higher law** based on morality, reason, or nature that is superior to human-made law. According to Natural Law theorists, law should conform to moral principles, and unjust law is not true law. This module covers the evolution of Natural Law from ancient Greek philosophy to modern revivals.

---

## 1. Classical Natural Law Theory: Plato to St. Thomas Aquinas

**Time Period:** Ancient Greece (400 BC) to Medieval Europe (1200 AD)

### A. Plato (427-347 BC) 🏛️

**Greek Philosopher - Father of Idealism**

**Main Ideas:**

#### **1. Theory of Ideas/Forms**
**What Plato believed:**
Every physical object is imperfect copy of perfect "Idea" or "Form" existing in ideal world.

- Physical chair = imperfect
- Idea of perfect chair exists
- We see shadows of perfect Forms

**Application to Law:**
- Physical laws made by humans are imperfect
- Perfect law (natural law) exists in world of Ideas
- Human law should try to approximate perfect law

#### **2. Justice as Harmony**
**Plato's concept of justice:**
Justice is giving each person their due based on their nature and capacity.

- Each class (rulers, soldiers, workers) performs their function
- When each does their duty, society is just
- Harmony in state and individual

#### **3. Law and Morality Inseparable**
- Law must be based on moral principles
- Unjust law is not law
- Ruler should be philosopher-king (wise and moral)

---

### B. Aristotle (384-322 BC) ⚖️

**Plato's Student - Father of Realism**

**Main Ideas:**

#### **1. Natural Justice**
**Aristotle distinguished:**
- **Natural Justice**: Universal, unchangeable, based on reason (same everywhere)
- **Conventional Justice**: Varies by place and time, made by humans

**Example:**
- Natural: Murder is wrong (universal)
- Conventional: Drive on left/right side (varies by country)

#### **2. Man is Political Animal**
- Humans naturally live in society
- State is natural institution
- Law necessary for orderly society
- State exists for good life, not mere life

#### **3. Law as Reason Free from Passion**
- Law is impartial
- Based on reason, not emotion
- Governs without bias
- Better than rule of men

#### **4. Distributive Justice**
- Equals should be treated equally
- Unequals unequally
- Proportion and fairness

---

### C. Roman Law and Cicero (106-43 BC) 🏛️

**Roman Philosopher and Statesman**

**Cicero's Contribution:**

#### **1. Natural Law Universal**
- True law is right reason
- In agreement with nature
- Universal application
- Unchanging and everlasting

#### **2. Higher Law Exists**
- Natural law superior to human law
- Based on reason and nature
- Cannot be altered by human authority

**Famous Quote:**
"True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting"

---

### D. St. Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) ⛪

**Medieval Christian Philosopher - Most Influential Natural Law Thinker**

**Synthesized:** Greek philosophy (Aristotle) + Christian theology

### Aquinas's Four Types of Law:

#### **1. Eternal Law** 🌟
**What it is:** God's plan for the universe; divine reason governing all creation.

- God's law
- Perfect and unchanging
- Governs entire cosmos
- Known fully only to God

#### **2. Divine Law** 📖
**What it is:** God's law revealed to humans through scriptures (Bible).

- Revealed in holy books
- For salvation
- Examples: Ten Commandments
- Guides to eternal life

#### **3. Natural Law** 🧭
**What it is:** That part of Eternal Law which human reason can discover.

**Key Points:**
- Based on human reason
- Inherent in human nature
- Accessible to all through reason
- Universal moral principles

**Example:** "Do good and avoid evil," "Live in society," "Preserve life"

**How known:**
- Through reason and conscience
- Self-evident to rational humans
- Not revelation (differs from divine law)

#### **4. Human Law (Positive Law)** 📜
**What it is:** Law made by human authorities (kings, parliaments).

**Validity Depends On:**
- Must be derived from natural law
- Must not contradict natural law
- If contradicts natural law = unjust law = not true law

**Famous Statement:**
"Unjust law is no law at all" (Lex injusta non est lex)

**Example:** If ruler makes law allowing murder, it violates natural law, so not valid law

### Aquinas's Influence:

✅ Most systematic natural law theory  
✅ Influenced Western legal thought for centuries  
✅ Christian foundation to natural law  
✅ Basis for human rights concepts

## 2. Natural Law and Social Contract Theory

**Social Contract theorists explained state and law through idea of contract between people**

### A. Hugo Grotius (1583-1645) 🌐

**Dutch Philosopher - Father of International Law**

**Main Contribution: Secularized Natural Law**

#### **1. Natural Law Based on Human Nature**
**Grotius's innovation:**
Natural law not based on God's will but on **human nature and reason**.

**His Famous Statement:**
Natural law would exist "even if God did not exist" (etiam si daremus Deum non esse)

**Why Revolutionary:**
- Separated law from religion
- Made natural law secular
- Based on human reason

#### **2. Sociability of Man**
**Human nature:**
Humans have natural desire for peaceful society.

- Social impulse inherent
- Rational social beings
- Need for society
- Right and wrong based on what promotes/hinders social life

#### **3. Principles of Natural Law**
**Basic rules according to Grotius:**
- Keep promises
- Respect others' property
- Compensate for harm done
- Punish wrongdoers

#### **4. International Law**
**Major work:** "De Jure Belli ac Pacis" (On the Law of War and Peace, 1625)

- Applied natural law to relations between nations
- Founded international law
- Rules for war and peace

---

### B. Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679) 👤

**English Philosopher - Pessimistic View of Human Nature**

**Main Work:** "Leviathan" (1651)

#### **1. State of Nature** 🌲
**What Hobbes imagined:**
Before society and government, humans lived in "state of nature."

**Characteristics:**
- No law, no government
- Everyone equal
- Everyone free
- BUT: "**War of all against all**"
- Life was "**solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short**"

**Why War:**
- Humans selfish by nature
- Compete for resources
- No authority to control
- Constant conflict and insecurity

#### **2. Natural Rights**
**In state of nature:** Everyone had **unlimited right** to everything.

- Right to self-preservation
- Right to use any means for survival
- But insecure (others also have same rights)

#### **3. Social Contract** 🤝
**Solution to chaos:**
People made a contract to escape state of nature.

**Contract Terms:**
- Everyone surrenders all rights to a sovereign
- Sovereign gets absolute power
- In return, sovereign provides security and order
- Once contract made, cannot be revoked

#### **4. Absolute Sovereign**
**Hobbes's conclusion:**
- Sovereign must have absolute power
- No limits on sovereign
- No right to rebel
- Even bad government better than anarchy

**Why Absolute:**
- To prevent return to state of nature
- Security requires strong authority
- Divided power = civil war

**Criticism:**
- Too pessimistic about human nature
- Justifies dictatorship
- No limits on government power

---

### C. John Locke (1632-1704) 🕊️

**English Philosopher - Optimistic View, Father of Liberalism**

**Main Work:** "Two Treatises of Government" (1690)

#### **1. State of Nature** 🌳
**Locke's version:** More peaceful than Hobbes's.

**Characteristics:**
- State of perfect freedom
- State of equality
- Governed by natural law (reason)
- Generally peaceful
- BUT: No organized mechanism to enforce rights (inconvenient)

**Natural Rights in State of Nature:**
- **Life**
- **Liberty**  
- **Property**

#### **2. Natural Law**
- Reason teaches natural law
- Don't harm others' life, liberty, property
- Most people follow reason
- But some violate (problem)

#### **3. Problems in State of Nature**
**Why government needed:**
- No established law (uncertainty)
- No impartial judge (everyone judges own case)
- No executive power (no enforcement mechanism)

**Not terrible, but inconvenient**

#### **4. Social Contract** 🤝
**Locke's contract:**
People make contract to establish government.

**Important Differences from Hobbes:**

**A. Limited Sovereignty:**
- People don't surrender all rights
- Retain natural rights (life, liberty, property)
- Government's power limited to protecting these rights

**B. Trust, Not Absolute Surrender:**
- Government holds power in **trust** for people
- Must use for people's benefit
- People remain ultimate sovereign

**C. Right to Rebel:**
- If government violates trust
- If government becomes tyrannical
- People can overthrow and create new government

**D. Separation of Powers:**
- Legislative and executive should be separate
- Prevents concentration of power

#### **5. Property Theory**
**Locke's famous labor theory:**
- In nature, everything common
- When person mixes labor with nature, becomes their property
- Example: Pick fruits from tree = your fruits (labor mixed)

**Influence:**
- Influenced American Declaration of Independence
- "Life, Liberty, and Pursuit of Happiness"
- Liberal democratic thought
- Human rights concepts

---

### D. Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778) 🗳️

**French Philosopher - Most Democratic Social Contract Theory**

**Main Work:** "The Social Contract" (1762)

#### **1. State of Nature** 🌺
**Rousseau's romantic view:**
- Humans naturally good and free
- "Noble savage"
- Peaceful and happy
- No private property (no conflicts)

**Famous Quote:** "Man is born free, but everywhere he is in chains"

#### **2. Problem: Private Property** 💰
**What corrupted humans:**
- Introduction of private property
- Led to inequality
- Rich vs Poor
- Conflicts arose

**First person who fenced land and said "this is mine" caused problems**

#### **3. Social Contract** 🤝
**Rousseau's solution:**
People make contract to form society and government.

**Key Concept: General Will:**
- When people contract, they form "general will"
- General will = common good, not individual interests
- Sovereign = people collectively
- Law = expression of general will

#### **4. Popular Sovereignty** 👥
**Most democratic theory:**
- People are sovereign
- Government is servant
- People can change government
- Direct democracy preferred

**Influence:**
- French Revolution inspired by Rousseau
- Modern democracy concepts
- People's sovereignty

---

### Summary: Three Social Contract Theorists

| Thinker | State of Nature | Why Contract? | Type of Govt | Can Rebel? |
|---------|----------------|---------------|--------------|------------|
| **Hobbes** | War, chaos | Escape war | Absolute monarchy | NO |
| **Locke** | Peaceful but inconvenient | Protect rights | Limited govt, separation of powers | YES (if trust violated) |
| **Rousseau** | Free and happy | Protect from inequality | Democracy, popular sovereignty | YES (people sovereign) |

## 3. Revival of Natural Law (20th Century)

**Context:** After World War II (Holocaust, Nazi atrocities), natural law revived to assert moral limits on law.

### A. Lon Fuller (1902-1978) 📏

**American Jurist - The Morality of Law (1964)**

**Fuller's Contribution: Inner Morality of Law**

**What Fuller argued:**
Law has its own **internal morality** - procedural standards that law must meet to be law.

### 8 Requirements of Law's Inner Morality (COUNT = 8):

1️⃣ **Generality**: Law must be general rules, not particular commands  
2️⃣ **Promulgation**: Laws must be published, made known to people  
3️⃣ **Prospectivity**: Laws should apply to future conduct (not retroactive)  
4️⃣ **Clarity**: Laws must be understandable, not obscure  
5️⃣ **Non-Contradiction**: Laws must not contradict each other  
6️⃣ **Possibility**: Laws must not require impossible acts  
7️⃣ **Stability**: Laws should not change constantly  
8️⃣ **Congruence**: Officials' actions must conform to declared law  

**Fuller's Point:**
- If law fails these requirements, it's not law (even if made by proper authority)
- Nazi "laws" failed these tests (secret laws, retroactive, contradictory)
- These are moral requirements (hence "morality" of law)

**Criticism:**
- These are procedural, not moral requirements
- Even immoral law can meet these (e.g., apartheid laws)

---

### B. H.L.A. Hart on Natural Law 🎓

**British Philosopher - The Concept of Law (1961)**

**Hart's Position: Minimum Content Natural Law**

**What Hart argued:**
While generally positivist (law and morality separate), Hart recognized **minimum content of natural law**.

**Why Minimum Natural Law Necessary:**

**Human Facts:**
- Humans are vulnerable (can be hurt/killed)
- Resources are scarce (not unlimited)
- People have limited altruism (not always selfless)
- People have limited understanding and willpower

**Therefore Law Must Have:**
- Rules against violence (protect life)
- Rules about property (distribute scarce resources)
- Mutual forbearance (respect others)
- Rules of cooperation
- Sanctions for violations

**Hart's Point:**
- For society to survive, law must have this minimum content
- Based on human nature and survival needs
- Not full natural law, but minimum necessary rules

---

### C. John Finnis - Restatement of Natural Law 📚

**British/Australian Philosopher - Natural Law and Natural Rights (1980)**

**Finnis's Project:** Restate natural law in modern terms without religious foundation.

**Main Ideas:**

#### **1. Seven Basic Human Goods**
**What humans naturally seek (self-evident goods):**

1️⃣ **Life** - Preservation of life and health  
2️⃣ **Knowledge** - Desire to know truth  
3️⃣ **Play** - Recreation and enjoyment  
4️⃣ **Aesthetic Experience** - Beauty and art  
5️⃣ **Sociability** - Friendship and community  
6️⃣ **Practical Reasonableness** - Using reason in choices  
7️⃣ **Religion** - Spiritual dimension, concern with ultimate questions  

**Finnis's claim:** These are self-evidently good, need no justification.

#### **2. Practical Reasonableness**
**How to pursue these goods:**
Using practical reason (rationality) to choose and pursue basic goods.

**Requirements of Practical Reasonableness:**
- Have coherent life plan
- No arbitrary preference among goods
- Respect all basic goods
- Don't harm others pursuing your goods
- Foster common good
- Act according to conscience

#### **3. Law and Common Good**
**Purpose of law:**
Law coordinates pursuit of basic goods and promotes common good.

**Unjust Law:**
- If law doesn't promote common good
- If law arbitrary or unreasonable
- May not be binding morally (though still legally valid)

**Modern Approach:**
- Doesn't rely on God or religion
- Based on human reason and goods
- Accessible to all rational beings
- Secular natural law

---

## Summary: Evolution of Natural Law

### From Ancient to Modern:

**Classical (Plato, Aristotle, Aquinas):**
- Based on reason, nature, and/or God
- Unjust law is no law
- Strong moral foundation

**Social Contract (Grotius, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau):**
- Explained origin of state and law through contract
- Natural rights before society
- Government to protect rights

**Modern Revival (Fuller, Hart, Finnis):**
- After WWII atrocities
- Reasserting moral limits on law
- Secular approaches
- Practical reasonableness

---

## Common Themes in Natural Law

**All Natural Law theories share:**

✅ **Higher law exists** - based on reason/nature/God  
✅ **Law should be moral** - conform to higher principles  
✅ **Unjust law problematic** - may not be true law or not binding  
✅ **Universal principles** - apply to all humans  
✅ **Reason is key** - humans can discover through reason  
✅ **Law and morality connected** - not separate  

---

## Criticism of Natural Law

**Problems with Natural Law Theory:**

❌ **Who decides what's natural?** - People disagree on moral principles  
❌ **Varying morals** - Morality differs across cultures and times  
❌ **Dangerous in practice** - Can justify disobedience to law  
❌ **Too abstract** - Doesn't give clear answers to specific problems  
❌ **Religious basis problematic** - In pluralistic societies
