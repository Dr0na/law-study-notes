## 🛒 Q5. Discuss the rule "Nemo dat quod non habet" (no one can transfer a better title than he himself has) under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. Explain its exceptions in detail.

### 1. Introduction: The General Rule
The fundamental principle of commercial law regarding the transfer of ownership is encapsulated in the Latin maxim **"Nemo dat quod non habet."** 
Literally translated, it means *"no one gives what he does not have."*

**Section 27** of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, embodies this rule:
*"Where goods are sold by a person who is not the owner thereof and who does not sell them under the authority or with the consent of the owner, the buyer acquires no better title to the goods than the seller had."*

**Significance:** If a thief steals a car and sells it to an innocent buyer, the buyer does not become the legal owner. The true owner can recover the car from the innocent buyer because the thief had no title to pass on. This rule protects the true owner of the goods.

### 2. Exceptions to the Rule (Where a Non-Owner CAN Pass Valid Title)
While the general rule protects property rights, strictly applying it would paralyze commerce. Buyers cannot realistically investigate the title history of every item they purchase. Therefore, to protect innocent buyers acting in good faith, the law recognizes several exceptions where a non-owner can pass a valid title.

#### I. Sale by a Mercantile Agent (Sec 27 proviso)
If a mercantile agent (e.g., a factor, broker, or auctioneer) is entrusted with the goods or documents of title to the goods, a sale made by them passes a good title to the buyer, provided:
1. The agent is in possession of the goods or documents of title with the **consent of the owner**.
2. The agent sells the goods while acting in the **ordinary course of business** of a mercantile agent.
3. The buyer acts in **good faith** and has no notice that the agent lacks the authority to sell.

#### II. Sale by One of Joint Owners (Sec 28)
If one of several joint owners of goods has the sole possession of them with the permission of the co-owners, the property in the goods is transferred to any person who buys them from such joint owner.
*Condition:* The buyer must purchase in good faith and without notice that the seller had no authority to sell.

#### III. Sale by a Person in Possession under a Voidable Contract (Sec 29)
When the seller of goods has obtained possession of them under a contract voidable under Section 19 or 19A of the Indian Contract Act (e.g., by fraud, coercion, misrepresentation, or undue influence), but the contract has **not been rescinded (cancelled)** at the time of the sale.
*Condition:* The buyer acquires a good title if they buy them in good faith and without notice of the seller's defective title.
*(Note: This exception does not apply if the goods were obtained by theft, as that makes the contract void ab initio, not voidable).*

#### IV. Sale by a Seller in Possession After Sale (Sec 30(1))
Where a person, having sold goods, continues or is in possession of the goods (or documents of title), the delivery or transfer by that person (or their mercantile agent) of the goods to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of the previous sale, passes a good title.
*Example:* A sells a TV to B but asks to keep it for one more day. The next day, A sells the same TV to C, an innocent buyer. C gets a good title. B's only remedy is to sue A for damages.

#### V. Sale by a Buyer in Possession Before Transfer of Property (Sec 30(2))
Where a person, having bought or agreed to buy goods, obtains possession of the goods (or documents of title) with the consent of the seller, any delivery or transfer by that person (or their mercantile agent) to an innocent buyer passes a good title.
*Example:* A buys a car from B on a hire-purchase agreement (where ownership passes only after the last installment). A sells the car to C before paying the last installment. C gets a good title if C bought in good faith.

#### VI. Sale by an Unpaid Seller (Sec 54(3))
Where an unpaid seller who has exercised their right of lien or stoppage in transit resells the goods, the new buyer acquires a good title to the goods as against the original buyer, even if the seller did not give notice of resale to the original buyer.

#### VII. Sale by a Finder of Goods (Sec 169, Indian Contract Act)
Under specific circumstances, a finder of goods can sell them (e.g., if the goods are perishable, or if the lawful charges for finding the owner and preserving the goods amount to two-thirds of their value). The buyer acquires a valid title.

#### VIII. Sale by an Official Receiver or Assignee
In cases of insolvency, the Official Receiver or Assignee can sell the goods of the insolvent and pass a valid title, even though they are not the owner.

### 3. Conclusion
The rule *Nemo dat quod non habet* ensures the sanctity of private property, while its exceptions are carefully carved out to lubricate the wheels of modern commerce and protect bona fide purchasers. In any conflict between a true owner and an innocent buyer, these provisions dictate who retains the property and who is left to claim damages.
