## 👔 Q4. Under what circumstances is an agent personally liable for the contracts entered into by him on behalf of the principal? Discuss the rights of third parties against an undisclosed principal.

### 1. Introduction: The General Rule
The foundational principle of the law of agency is that an agent is merely a connecting link between the principal and the third party. 
**Section 230** of the Indian Contract Act lays down the general rule: *"In the absence of any contract to that effect, an agent cannot personally enforce contracts entered into by him on behalf of his principal, nor is he personally bound by them."*
Because the agent acts on behalf of the principal, it is the principal who is liable for the agent's acts.

### 2. Exceptions: Circumstances of Personal Liability
There are specific circumstances where the agent sheds their protective shield and becomes personally liable to the third party.

**I. Express Contract (Sec 230)**
If the agent expressly agrees or stipulates in the contract with the third party that they will be personally liable.

**II. Presumed Contracts (Sec 230 Exceptions)**
The law presumes a contract of personal liability in the following three cases (unless the contrary is proved):
1. **Foreign Principal:** Where the contract is made by an agent for the sale or purchase of goods for a merchant resident abroad. (This is because suing a foreign merchant was historically difficult).
2. **Undisclosed Principal:** Where the agent does not disclose the name of the principal. (If the third party doesn't know who the principal is, they must rely on the agent's credit).
3. **Incompetent Principal:** Where the principal, though disclosed, cannot be sued (e.g., a foreign sovereign, an ambassador, or a minor). The third party has only the agent to look to.

**III. Pretended Agent (Sec 235)**
A person untruly representing themselves to be the authorized agent of another, and thereby inducing a third person to deal with them as such, is liable. If the supposed employer does not ratify the acts, the pretended agent is personally liable to make compensation to the third party for any loss or damage.

**IV. Agent Exceeds Authority (Breach of Warranty of Authority)**
If an agent acts outside the scope of their authority and the principal refuses to ratify the act, the agent becomes personally liable to the third party for the unauthorized act.

**V. Agent Signing Negotiable Instruments**
If an agent signs a promissory note, bill of exchange, or cheque in their own name without indicating that they are signing as an agent on behalf of a principal, the agent is personally liable on the instrument (under the Negotiable Instruments Act).

**VI. Trade Custom or Usage**
Where the custom or usage of trade makes the agent personally liable.

### 3. Rights of Third Parties Against an Undisclosed Principal
An "undisclosed principal" situation arises when an agent makes a contract with a third party without disclosing that they are acting as an agent. The third party believes the agent is the actual contracting party.

If the third party later discovers the existence of the principal, what are their rights?

**I. Right to Sue Either or Both (Sec 233)**
In cases where the agent is personally liable (as in the case of an undisclosed principal), a person dealing with them may hold either the agent, or the principal, or both of them, liable. 
*Example:* A enters into a contract with B to sell him 100 bales of cotton, and afterwards discovers that B was acting as agent for C. A may sue either B or C, or both, for the price of the cotton.

**II. Principal Can Require Performance (Sec 231)**
If an agent makes a contract for an undisclosed principal, the principal may require the performance of the contract.
*Condition:* The principal can only step in subject to the rights and obligations existing between the agent and the third party.

**III. Third Party's Right of Set-off (Sec 232)**
Where the principal discloses himself before the contract is completed, the other contracting party has the same rights against the principal as they would have had against the agent if the agent had been the principal. If the third party had a set-off against the agent, they can claim that set-off against the principal.

**IV. Right of Refusal (Sec 231)**
If the principal discloses themselves, the third party can refuse to fulfill the contract if they can show that:
- If they had known who the principal was, or
- If they had known that the agent was not a principal,
they would not have entered into the contract.

### 4. Conclusion
While the general rule protects agents from personal liability, the exceptions ensure that third parties are not left without a remedy when dealing with foreign, incompetent, or undisclosed principals. The law balances the commercial necessity of agency with the protection of third-party rights.
